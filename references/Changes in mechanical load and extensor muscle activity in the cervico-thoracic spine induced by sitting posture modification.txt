Changes in mechanical load and extensor muscle activity in the cervico-thoracic spine induced by
sitting posture modiﬁcation
Stephen J. Edmondston*, Michael Sharp, Andew Symes, Nawaf Alhabib and Garry T. Allison
School of Physiotherapy and Curtin Health Innovation Research Institute, Curtin University of Technology, Perth, Western
Australia
(Received 13 September 2010; ﬁnal version received 16 November 2010 )
The inﬂuence of whole body sitting posture on cervico-thoracic posture, mechanical load and extensor muscle
activity was examined in 23 asymptomatic adults. Cervical and upper thoracic extensor muscle activity measured in
guided slouched and lumbo-pelvic neutral postures was normalised to that measured in a self-selected habitual
posture. Head and neck posture and gravitational load moment measurements were obtained in each posture.
Sagittal head translation, upper cervical extension and load moment were signiﬁcantly greater in the slouched
posture ( p 5 0.001). Contrasting patterns of cervical and thoracic extensor activity were observed in the slouched
and neutral postures, with cervical extensor activity 40% higher in the slouched posture ( p 5 0.0001). Thoracic
extensor activity was signiﬁcantly higher in the lumbo-pelvic neutral posture than the habitual posture ( p ¼ 0.002).
The signiﬁcant changes in extensor muscle activity with postural modiﬁcation appear to be induced by the associated
change in mechanical load moment of the head.
Statement of Relevance: More neutral sitting postures reduce the demand on the cervical extensor muscles and modify
the relative contribution of cervical and thoracic extensors to the control of head and neck posture. Postures that
promote these patterns of muscular activity may reduce cervical spine loading and the development of posture-related
neck pain.
Keywords: cervical spine; cervico-thoracic muscle activity; electromyography; sitting posture
1. Introduction
Sustained sitting postures and the related load on the
cervical spine are important contributors to the high
prevalence of neck pain in oﬃce workers and individuals
performing computer-based tasks (Coˆte´et al. 2008). The
loads transferred to the neck are likely to be inﬂuenced
by the posture of the head and neck and related levels of
muscle activity (Choi 2003). Flexed head and neck
postures are associated with an increase in gravitational
load moment and cervical extensor muscle activity,
which may contribute to the higher prevalence of neck
pain in individuals with this postural habit (Yip et al.
2008, Strakeret al. 2009). Similarly, correction towards a
more upright posture tends to decrease cervical extensor
activity and increase activation of the deep ﬂexor muscles
(Falla et al. 2007, Caneiro et al. 2010).
Changes in whole body sitting posture have been
shown to inﬂuence head and neck posture and levels of
activity in the neck ﬂexor and extensor muscles (Black
et al. 1996, McLean 2005, Falla et al. 2007). Sitting
postures that promote cervico-thoracic ﬂexion are
associated with greater cervical extensor muscle activity,
while more upright sitting postures that reduce forward
head translation and cervical ﬂexion appear to reduce
the level of cervical extensor activity (Harms-Ringdahl
et al. 1986, Caneiro et al. 2010). Changes in sitting
posture inﬂuence the alignment of both the cervical and
thoracic regions (O’Sullivan et al. 2006, Edmondston
et al. 2007). The associated changes in cervical extensor
muscle activity have been previously reported
(Thuresson et al. 2005), but the associated changes in
thoracic extensor activity are less clear.
Cervical spine extensor muscle activity is likely
to vary according to the load demands induced by
the position of the head and neck. The loading
demands increase as the head and neck are more
ﬂexed, increasing the gravitational load moment
around the seventh cervical vertebra (Straker et al.
2009). The inﬂuence of cervical load moment on
extensor muscle activity in functional postures has
been examined in only a limited number of studies.
In helicopter pilots, a 20 8 increase in cervical ﬂexion
was associated with a three-fold increase in cervical
load moment, but a very small increase in neck
extensor muscle activity (Thuresson et al. 2005). The
limited change in muscle activity was despite the

cervical load moment when sitting postures were
modiﬁed from neutral to end-range ﬂexion. However,
the associated median extensor muscle activity was 6%

higher levels (10% MVC) were recorded in some
subjects. The relationship between cervical load
moment and extensor muscle activity has not been
examined with regard to mid-range cervical postures
and the inﬂuence of whole body postural adjustment.
Sitting postures are typically associated with levels
of neck muscle activation of between 2 and 6% of
MVC and changes in response to postural adjustment
are similarly small (McLean 2005, Mork and West-
gaard 2006). Normalisation of neck muscle electro-
myography (EMG) using a MVC is typically employed
but MVC can be inﬂuenced by factors such as subject
motivation and the posture in which the maximal
contraction is performed (Mathiassen et al. 1995).
Although postural modiﬁcation has been associated
with signiﬁcant changes in neck extensor muscle
activity, the magnitude of this change is commonly
less than 5% of MVC (McLean 2005, Thuresson et al.
2005, Caneiro et al. 2010). Therefore, in studies
examining postural neck muscle activity, EMG nor-
malisation using a reference posture, has been recom-
mended (Sommerich et al. 2000). This approach may
provide a better appreciation of the relative changes in
cervical and thoracic muscle activity in response to
postural modiﬁcation. Therefore, the objective of this
study was to examine the inﬂuence of whole body
sitting posture on cervico-thoracic posture and the
gravitational load moment around an axis in the low
cervical spine induced by the weight of the head. A
further objective was to measure the posture-related
changes in cervical and thoracic extensor muscle
activity, where muscle activity in each posture was
referenced to that measured in the habitual sitting
posture.
2. Methods
2.1. Subjects
Altogether, 23 asymptomatic subjects (11 males and 12
females) with a mean age of 27.7 years (SD 3.5) were
recruited for the study. Subjects had no history of neck
pain in the last 3 months and no restriction of cervico-
thoracic range of motion. Subjects were excluded if
they had pain or medical conditions aﬀecting cervical
mobility, neurological symptoms aﬀecting the upper
limb, arm pain or pain associated with upper limb
movement, previous neck trauma or were currently
receiving treatment. All subjects signed a written
consent form following a detailed explanation of the
study procedures. Approval for the study was granted
by the institutional Human Research Ethics
Committee.
2.2. Experimental procedure
A within subjects, repeated measures design was
employed to examine the inﬂuence of postural change
on cervico-thoracic load moment and muscle activity
as measured using surface EMG. Three postures were
examined, which were modiﬁed from those described
by Burnett et al. (2009):
. Habitual posture: Subjects were asked to sit in
a relaxed position on a treatment couch
without any speciﬁc instruction as to how they
should sit (Figure 1a). To facilitate relaxation
into a habitual posture, subjects were engaged
in conversation to distract their attention from
their posture. This position was maintained for
a minimum of 3 min prior to posture analysis.
. Lumbo-pelvic neutral posture: Subjects were
guided into a neutral lumbo-pelvic posture. This
posture required anterior rotation of the pelvis, a
neutral lumbar lordosis and positioning of the
thorax over the pelvis but without excessive
extension (Figure 1b).
. Slouched posture: Subjects were guided into a
position of end-range lumbo-pelvic ﬂexion,
which induced thoracic ﬂexion and cervical spine
and head protraction (Figure 1c).
For all postures examined, subjects sat on a
treatment couch with no back support. The height of
the couch was adjusted to accommodate 90 8 of knee
ﬂexion with the feet positioned on the ﬂoor, shoulder
width apart. Arms were relaxed with the hands resting
palm down on the thighs. In all postures, subjects were
asked to ﬁx their gaze on an adjustable wall marker,
which was positioned at eye level. The participants
were guided into the prescribed postures by an
experienced physiotherapist. Subjects maintained each
posture for 10 s to allow for posture analysis
photographs and EMG measurements to be
completed. Subjects were given 1 min rest between
postures and each posture was tested in a random
order.

alcohol rub, bipolar surface electrodes (3M, Ag/AgCl

Telemetric system (Bortec Electronics Inc., Calgary,

(National Instruments, Austin, TX, USA) and a
customised software program (LabVIEW V7.0;

All raw EMG signals were pre-ampliﬁed and
visually inspected for artefacts (heart rate and move-
ment). For each channel, individual ampliﬁcation
gains were set to ensure appropriate ampliﬁcation
without the potential for amplitude clipping. Post-
processing included demeaning and secondary band-
pass ﬁltering (fourth order zero lag Butterworth ﬁlter
20–500 Hz). The EMG signal amplitude for each
channel was determined using a total root mean square
(RMS) for each second of the 10 s task duration. EMG
signals were amplitude normalised (RMS) using a sub-
maximal reference signal recorded during the habitual
sitting posture, according to the recommendations for
examining low levels of neck muscle activation
(Sommerich et al. 2000). The normalised mean
amplitude over the 10 s data collection period was
used in the analysis.
3.2. Posture measurements
Reﬂective markers were adhered unilaterally over the
C7 and T4 spinous processes, tragus of the ear, lateral
margin of the orbit and sternal notch. An experienced
physiotherapist identiﬁed the anatomical landmarks
for marker placement and these were checked by a
second investigator.
A digital camera (Sony DCR-SX403; Sony Corp.,
Tokyo, Japan) was mounted on a tripod and
positioned 2.5 m to the left side of the subject.
Photographs (2592 6 1944 pixels) were taken in each
test position by the same investigator. The images were
analysed on a personal computer using image analysis
software (Image J; National Institutes of Health,
Bethesda, MD, USA). All image analysis was
performed by one investigator in order to minimise
image analysis error. The postural measurements were
the following:
. Cervico-thoracic angle: The angle formed by the
intersection of lines connecting the tragus and
the C7 spinous process, and the C7 spinous
process and the T4 (Figure 1a).
. Upper cervical angle: The angle formed by a line
extending from the tragus to the lateral margin
of the orbit and the horizontal plane (Figure 1b).
. Sagittal plane head translation: The horizontal
distance between the vertical line extending from
the T4 spinous process and the tragus (Figure 1c).

3.4. Posture measurement reliability
A reliability study was conducted on all posture
measurements prior to commencing the study. Five
images were acquired from one subject in each of the
three postures. Each image was analysed by one
investigator who was blinded to the measurement
results. Coeﬃcients of variation (CV) and standard
error of measurement (SEM) were calculated for each
posture. The CV was between 0.44% and 0.54% for
the cervico-thoracic angle, 0.87% and 3.98% for upper
cervical angle and 0.30% and 0.70% for anterior
sagittal translation. The SEM for all postural measure-
ments was 5 18.
3.5. Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistic analysis was performed to
summarise the posture and load moment measure-
ments in each of the sitting positions. Since no
signiﬁcant diﬀerence in EMG amplitude between
sides was identiﬁed, muscle amplitudes were averaged
and the mean value used in all comparative analyses.
The mean EMG amplitude expressed as change in
amplitude (%) from the reference habitual posture
amplitude was calculated for each prescribed posture.
A one-factor repeated measures ANOVA was used
to test for signiﬁcant diﬀerences in the head and
neck posture measures, and load moment, between
the three sitting postures. Post hoc contrast analysis
was then used to test for pair-wise diﬀerences. For
the EMG data, a repeated measures ANOVA was
used to determine if there was a signiﬁcant eﬀect of
posture and muscle or a posture by muscle interac-
tion. Post hoc pair-wise comparisons were then
performed to test for signiﬁcant diﬀerences in
EMG amplitude between postures and muscles. For
all analyses, the criterion for statistical signiﬁcance
was set at p 5 0.05.
4. Results
4.1. Postural analysis
The posture measurements for each of the sitting
postures are summarised in Table 1. There were no
signiﬁcant diﬀerences in the posture measures between
the habitual and lumbo-pelvic neutral postures. The
slouched posture was associated with a slight increase
in the cervico-thoracic angle compared with the
habitual and lumbo-pelvic neutral posture but the
diﬀerences were not statistically signiﬁcant ( p ¼ 0.17
and p ¼ 0.13 respectively). Upper cervical extension
was signiﬁcantly greater in the slouched posture than
in the habitual ( p 5 0.0001) or neutral ( p ¼ 0.0004)
postures. Similarly, anterior translation of the head
was signiﬁcantly greater in the slouched posture than
in the habitual ( p 5 0.0001) or neutral postures
(p 5 0.0001).
4.2. Cervico-thoracic load moment
The moment arm length and resultant gravitational
load moment calculated in each posture are
summarised in Table 1. The load moment in the
slouched posture was signiﬁcantly greater than in the
habitual ( p 5 0.0001) and lumbo-pelvic neutral
(p 5 0.0001) postures. There was no signiﬁcant
diﬀerence in load moment between the habitual and
lumbo-pelvic neutral postures. The load moment in the
slouched posture increased by 57.2% compared with
the habitual posture. There was a 2.2% decrease in the
load moment in the lumbo-pelvic neutral posture
compared with the habitual posture.

5. Discussion
Guidelines for computer and workstation use recom-
mend lumbo-pelvic support to assist the maintenance
of optimal head and neck postures (Cook and Burgess-
Limerick 2003). This study has shown that modiﬁca-
tion of the lumbo-pelvic position in unsupported
sitting has a signiﬁcant inﬂuence on the posture of
the head and neck. More upright lumbo-pelvic
postures signiﬁcantly reduce anterior translation of
the head, and upper cervical extension, which are
features of the forward-head posture, associated with
an increased prevalence of neck pain (Aaras et al. 1998,
Yip et al. 2008). This postural adjustment resulted in a
signiﬁcant decrease in the gravitational load moment
around the low cervical spine, induced by the weight of
the head and neck. While previous studies have
reported signiﬁcant increases in the load moment
around the low cervical spine in mid-range and
extreme neck ﬂexion (Finsen 1999, Thuresson et al.
2005), the present results demonstrate a similar eﬀect
of neck protraction, a posture more commonly
associated with computer use by oﬃce workers (Szeto
et al. 2002, Yoo and Kim 2010).
Under low load conditions, posture of the head and
neck is controlled predominantly by the deep anterior
and posterior muscles of the neck (Mayoux-Benhamou
et al. 1997). Changes in neck ﬂexor and extensor
muscle activity in response to whole body postural
adjustments in sitting have previously been
demonstrated (McLean 2005, Falla et al. 2007,
Caneiro et al. 2010). In the present study, the level of
activation of the cervical and thoracic extensor muscles
appeared to be driven by the magnitude of the load
moment of the head and neck. The higher load
moment in the slouched posture was associated with
signiﬁcantly greater cervical extensor muscle activity
than that associated with the lumbo-pelvic neutral
posture. Importantly, the activation of the cervical
extensor muscles in the more protracted head position
was 40% greater than in the more upright habitual and
lumbo-pelvic postures. These higher levels of extensor
muscle activity may disturb the balance between

extensor and ﬂexor muscle activity, changing the

Previous studies examining muscle activity during
sitting tasks have normalised the EMG measurements
using maximal contraction in order to compare levels
of activity in diﬀerent muscles (Harms-Ringdahl et al.
1986, McLean 2005). The rationale for this is that
%MVC gives a better indication of the relative
demand on the muscle and its capacity (Straker et al.
2009). Given the low muscular demands of static
sitting postures, the posture-related changes in cervical
extensor activation relative to the MVC are typically
very low, making the measurement vulnerable to error
due to variability in the MVC (Jensen et al. 1996). The
approach taken in the present study was to use a
posture-speciﬁc normalisation technique so that EMG
measurements in the prescribed postures were normal-
ised to those in the subject’s self-selected (habitual)
posture (Bauer and Wittig 1998). As a result, cervical
extensor muscle activity was about 40% greater in the
slouched posture than in the habitual and lumbo-pelvic
neutral postures. In previous studies, similar postural
adjustments have been associated with changes in
cervical extensor activity of between 1 and 5%MVC
(McLean 2005, Caneiro et al. 2010). This suggests that
EMG normalisation to a reference posture may
provide a better indication of the relative changes in
cervical muscle activity associated with changes in
sitting posture.
While the changes in cervical extensor muscle
activity associated with changes in head and neck
posture are well documented, the changes in other
related muscles are less clear. Contrasting patterns of
thoracic and cervical extensor muscle activity were an
unexpected ﬁnding of the present study. Upper
thoracic extensor activity increased signiﬁcantly in
the lumbo-pelvic neutral posture compared with that
in the habitual posture, despite the small changes in
posture measurements and related load moment. This
ﬁnding is consistent with that of Harms-Ringdahl et al.
(1986), who reported a 2%MVC increase in upper
thoracic extensor activity in a retracted head and neck
posture, compared with that measured in a neutral
posture. The cervical and thoracic extensor muscles
may therefore have diﬀerent roles in maintaining head
and neck posture and resisting the ﬂexion moments
induced by the weight of the head. The upper thoracic
extensors such as semi-spinalis cervicis and splenius
cervicis may work to control ﬂexion and protraction of
the upper thoracic and low cervical spine through their
cervical attachments. This would explain the signiﬁ-
cantly higher thoracic extensor activity in this muscle
group in the more upright lumbo-pelvic posture and is
consistent with the proposed postural role of these
muscles (Conley et al. 1995). Despite the signiﬁcant
increase in thoracic muscle activity in the lumbo-
pelvic neutral posture, there was no signiﬁcant
change in the posture measurements compared with
those in the habitual posture. The likely explanation
for this is that cervico-thoracic angle measurement
used in this study was not sensitive to these subtle
postural adjustments.
The contrasting patterns of cervical and thoracic
muscle activity observed in this study provide an
insight into optimal patterns of cervico-thoracic
extensor muscle activity and how changes associated
with forward head postures may lead to the
development of postural neck pain. In the more
upright lumbo-pelvic neutral posture, the increased
activity in the upper thoracic extensors maintains a
more retracted head position and a relatively low level
of cervical extensor activity. The cervical lordosis
would be further supported in this posture by increased
activity in the deep anterior neck muscles (Fountain
et al. 1966, Falla et al. 2007). In the forward head
posture, decreased activation of the thoracic extensors
is associated with a signiﬁcant increase in cervical
extensor activity. The increased activation in the
cervical extensors, which have a smaller cross-sectional
area compared to the extensors with a thoracic origin
(Elliott et al. 2007), may result in decreased
oxygenation and decreased force output in the low
threshold motor units of these muscles (Kallenberg
et al. 2007, Flodgren et al. 2009). Activation of higher
threshold motor units may help oﬀ-set this fatigue
response in the short term (Hodson-Tole and Wakeling
2009) and may explain the higher levels of muscle
activity associated with low-load tasks, observed in
some individuals with posture-related neck pain
(Johnston et al. 2008). However, the higher levels of
muscle activity would contribute to elevated levels of
muscle strain and loading of cervical spine structures,
increasing the potential for the development of pain.
Inferences drawn from the results of this study
should acknowledge that the subjects were young and
asymptomatic so that the ﬁndings may not be
transferable to older individuals or those with neck
pain. The study was conducted in unsupported sitting
in order to evaluate muscular responses to postural
change without the inﬂuence of external support.
Although there was considerable variability in the
habitual posture, the mean habitual posture used as
the reference for the two prescribed postures was not
signiﬁcantly diﬀerent from the lumbo-pelvic neutral
posture. Subjects were encouraged to relax into their
preferred posture prior to the photographic analysis.
However, in some cases, there may have been a
tendency to adopt a posture that was diﬀerent to their
true unsupported posture. The short duration of

6. Conclusion
Work-related sitting postures that support the lumbo-
pelvic region in a neutral position promote greater
activation of the thoracic extensor muscles and
signiﬁcantly reduce the levels of cervical extensor
muscle activity associated with a forward head posture.
These posture-related changes in extensor muscle
activity appear to be related to the position of the
head relative to the trunk and changes in the
gravitational load moment induced by the weight of
the head. These observations will further inform the
development of guidelines for optimal sitting support
