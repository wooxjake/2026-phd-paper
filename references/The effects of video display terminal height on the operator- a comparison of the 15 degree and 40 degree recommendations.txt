Prolonged use of video display terminals (VDTs) has been shown to be a risk factor for musculo-
skeletal and visual discomfort. A standard workplace design recommendation is to position the
centre of the VDT 15¡ below horizontal eye level. Recently a viewing angle of 40¡ below horizontal
has been suggested based on studies that have indicated that this is the preferred viewing angle for
visually intensive tasks. The goal of this study was to investigate the e¤ects of these two VDT
positions on muscular activity, muscular fatigue, head/neck posture, visual acuity, operator perfor-
mance (productivity and quality), heart rate and operator subjective assessment. The experimental
task consisted of reading text from a computer screen and answering reading comprehension
questions using a mouse and a keyboard. Each experimental session lasted 2 h. The 40 ¡ VDT
position showed signiÞcantly greater head tilt angles and higher muscle activity levels for six of the
10 neck, shoulder and back muscles sampled. No signiÞcant di¤erences in visual acuity, operator
performance or heart rate were detected as a result of monitor location. Seven of the 12 subjects
preferred the 15¡ monitor position. ( 1998 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

Introduction
By the end of 1993, 74 million computers were being used
in the United States, making up 43% of the world-wide
computer market (Juliussen and Petska-Juliussen, 1994).
The widespread use of computers and video display ter-
minals (VDTs) has resulted in an increasing concern for
the physical well-being of the user, speciÞcally the mus-
culoskeletal and visual systems. Accordingly, consider-
able research on positioning the VDT to minimize
discomfort and fatigue has been undertaken (Arndt,
1983; Jaschinski-Kruza, 1988, 1991; Krueger, 1984;
Pickett and Lees, 1991; Sauter et al, 1991; Villanueva et
al, 1996) Despite this e¤ort, 10 million cases of VDT-
related eyestrain and physical discomfort are reported to
the American Optometric Association each year (Leavitt,
1995). Though many of these complaints arise from
workstations that are incorrectly adjusted for the indi-
vidual, Arndt (1983) found that complaints may still arise
from individuals working with properly adjusted
workstations.
Leavitt (1995) reported that eyestrain symptoms may
occur in up to 75% of all VDT users, while Pickett and
Lees (1991) found the percentage reporting eyestrain to
be as high as 85%. Tyrrell and Leibowitz (1990) de-
scribed eyestrain as a Ôvague discomfort which may be
localized in the head or eyesÕ, and included eyestrain in
a larger category termed visual fatigue. Visual fatigue can
be described as any subjective visual symptom or distress
resulting from the use of ones eyes (Tyrrell and
Leibowitz, 1990). Visual fatigue during VDT use is often
caused by prolonged visual activity performed during
near-work. Near-work causes the eyes to converge and
the lens to accommodate in order to focus, forcing the
extraocular and ciliary muscles of the eye to contract and
maintain contraction. According to Jaschinski-Kruza
(1988, 1991), this extraocular and ciliary muscle work
leads to eye strain and visual fatigue. Thus, as long as
VDT work causes the eyes to converge, complaints of eye
fatigue can be expected to continue.
Musculoskeletal discomfort of VDT users most com-
monly includes pain in the back, neck, and shoulders
(Arndt, 1983). Sauter et al (1991) reported that musculo-
skeletal discomfort was ÔcommonplaceÕ during VDT
work, Þnding 33% of 539 data entry VDT users reported

VDT users often perform prolonged seated tasks that
can cause static loading of the muscles of the neck and
upper back. Awkward, static postures tend to reduce
blood circulation to the muscles, decreasing the ßow of
nutrients and removal of wastes, which can lead to
muscle fatigue and pain (Arndt, 1983). Static postures can
also lead to elevated heart rate (Sanders and McCormick,
1993) and cause chronic problems in joints and tendons
(Grandjean, 1980). While VDT users are often advised to
take frequent breaks that include movement and stretch-
ing, most operators do not strictly follow these guide-
lines, but instead, simply alter their seated posture (either
consciously or subconsciously) by shifting around in
their chair. The postures resulting from these attempts to
alleviate static loading are often awkward and have the
potential to cause as much discomfort as the original
static posture (Watson and Trott, 1993; Kilbom et al
1986; Kumar, 1994). One basic VDT workplace design
principle is to position the monitor such that the posture
that the operator assumes will not require signiÞcant
static muscle forces that will result in a reduction of
blood ßow.
An often encountered recommendation for VDT
placement is to place the topmost line of the viewing
monitor at or slightly below eye level (OSHA, 1991; US
Department of Labor, 1995). This positioning strategy
places the centre of the monitor approximately 15¡ below
horizontal eye level and has been recommended because
it allows the operator to assume a comfortable and
natural posture, as the normal line of sight is actually 15¡
below horizontal (Sanders and McCormick, 1993; East-
man Kodak Human Factors Section, 1983).
Eye gaze angle and visual convergence have been
shown to be two important areas of research in VDT
workplace design. Eye gaze angle has been reported to be
dependent on head inclination angle, as eye gaze angle
can be adjusted by varying the head angle and/or the
inclination of the eye (Heuer and Owens, 1989). Previous
research studies have found that eyes tend to diverge as
they are elevated and converge as they are lowered (Her-
ing, 1977; Hemholtz, 1962; Heuer and Owens, 1989). The
results of these previous studies showed that natural
near-distance convergence occurs when the gaze angle is
below horizontal while divergence is found with elevated
gaze angles. Both of the monitor placement angles tested
within this current study allow for natural near-distance
convergence by positioning the centre of the monitor at
or below the natural line of sight.
Due to the large numbers of visual, postural and mus-
culoskeletal complaints still surrounding VDT worksta-
tions, new ideas concerning workstation design are
beginning to surface. One workstation modiÞcation is to
lower the VDT to a position beyond 15 ¡ so that the
centre of the monitor is actually below the work surface.
It has been suggested that 40 ¡ is the preferred viewing
angle for seated work (Eastman Kodak Human Factors
Section, 1983) and for visual tasks such as VDT use
(Kroemer and Hill, 1986). The lower position may aid
vision by improving eye moisture. Sotoyamaet al (1996)
reported that downward gaze angles decrease the surface
area of the eye exposed to the atmosphere, thus reducing
dry eye problems that arise during VDT use. Other
research reports blink rate as a reliable indicator of dry
eyes and visual discomfort (Villanueva et al, 1996). Be-
yond visual recommendations, Ankrum and Nemeth
(1995) postulated that the user is able to adopt a wider
range of head tilt angles while viewing the monitor at 40¡,
thereby reducing static loading of the muscles of the neck,
shoulder and back. Conversely, as the monitor is placed
below the work surface, deviated neck postures and re-
sulting elevated muscle activity are a concern. Fatigue
may also become a problem if head tilt angles associated
with use of lower monitors remain moderately static over
the course of VDT use.
Although some theoretical exploration supports the
idea of lowering the monitor, limited research has actual-
ly investigated the e¤ects of a 40¡ position. The goals of
the present study are to examine the biomechanical,
performance, physiological and visual e¤ects on the user
of a 40¡ and a 15¡ monitor position.

Experimental task
The task consisted of reading text of 12 pt. Arial font,
black letters on white background, presented on a 13 in
VDT. The character height provided the preferred range
of 20 Ð22 min of arc, optimizing both readability and
legibility (ANSI/HFS 100-1988). The reading text,

54.7) and interest factor for the average college student.
Speed of text presentation was controlled by the subject
with mouse or keyboard and at the end of each chapter

or multiple choice selection. A program recorded the
time spent reading each chapter and answering the cor-

task was designed with minimal typing and mousing to
reduce muscle and postural contributions arising from
task related movement.

Procedure
The subjects participated in two experimental sessions
which included preparatory information, measurement
of anthropometry (Session d1 only), electrode place-

Session d1. The subject was informed of the require-

A battery of seven di¤erent maximal isometric exer-
tions were performed by each subject. The subjects were

ßat on the ßoor while concentrating on using only the

Verbal encouragement was given to the subjects for mo-
tivation. The Þrst exertion required the subject to extend
the neck and head against the resistant arm of the Kin

only the neck and upper back muscles (Schu¬ ldt, 1988).
This cervical spine extension was useful in generating
a maximum exertion from the cervical erector spinae.
The next contractions involved lateral ßexion of the head
to the right and left sides with resistance at the level of the
temples (Schu¬ ldt, 1988). Forward pushing of the head,
with resistance against the forehead, generated maximum
activity from the sternocleidomastoid (Schu¬ ldt, 1988). In
order to generate maximum contractions from the tra-
pezius muscles two maximum exertions were performed.
In the Þrst trial the subjects attempted to abduct their
arm against resistance while their arm was in a 90 ¡
abducted posture (Ha¬g get al, 1987). The second method,
shoulder elevation, was achieved by having the subjects
pull up on two inextensible straps that were secured to
a platform on which the subject stood (Glasscocket al,i n
review).
After the maximum exertions, the subject was taken to
the experimental VDT workstation. The monitor was
placed at either 15¡ or 40¡ below the horizontal plane of
the eye. The subject was positioned in the workstation
and asked to perform a 2 h reading comprehension task.
A script of basic instructions was read to each subject,
describing in detail how to perform the task and instruct-

explicit instructions concerning trunk posture were pro-

Performance was measured by grading the answers given

calculated on all subjects by recording the time required
to read each chapter and to complete each question and

At 5 min intervals during the reading task muscle

Data analysis
C programs were developed and utilized to process each
subjectÕs integrated EMG data and calculate normalized
EMG signals. Fast Fourier transform programs written
in MatLab software were used to evaluate raw EMG
data. MANOVA and ANOVA techniques compared
muscle activation and fatigue levels as a function of

The video tapes of each session for each subject were
evaluated using video analysis. From this analysis blink
rate, postural shifts (anteriorÐposterior and lateral) and
sagittal head tilt angle were acquired. Subjective assess-
ment/discomfort, performance rating variables, visual

analysed and compared as a function of monitor height.
Analyses of variance were used to detect signiÞcant
e¤ects of the independent variable and the interac-

Discussion
The results of the normalized EMG analysis showed
signiÞcantly higher muscle activity in the 40 ¡ monitor
condition for the cervical and thoracic erector spinae.
The magnitude of EMG activity doubled from 3% max-
imum voluntary contraction (MVC) in the 15¡ condition
to 6% MVC in the 40¡ condition (Figure 4). According to
Jonsson (1978) static load level should not exceed 5%
MVC for work tasks of long duration. Additionally,
Sj+gaard et al(1986) found that static contractions of 5%
MVC sustained for 1 h induced muscle fatigue as in-
dicated by intramuscular pressure. Although subjects
were allowed to move throughout the experiment, oper-
ating a VDT is deÞned as a static task. The FFT analysis
of muscle fatigue did not show onset of fatigue for either
condition. This may be due to the relatively short dura-
tion of the sessions, 2 h, versus a typical 8 h work day.
The left trapezius was the only muscle that showed
higher activity at the 15 ¡ condition. The magnitude of
EMG activity for both conditions was substantially less
than the 5% MVC level shown to induce fatigue. The 15¡
condition exhibited a 2.5% MVC while the 40¡ condition
was at 1.9%. This activity could be a result of the posture
assumed between the two conditions. At the 15¡ condi-
tion, most subjects were sitting more erect, while at the
40¡ position, the shoulders tended to relax forward. The
right trapezius muscle activity levels were comparable,
possibly resulting from the use of the right arm for
scrolling through the text in both conditions.
Analysis of the video tapes for each session revealed
a signiÞcant increase ( p(0.05) over time in all body
movements for both conditions. Between the two moni-
tor placement conditions however, no signiÞcant di¤er-
ence was found in the total number of movements
throughout the experiment. These results contradict
Ankrum and Nemeth (1995) who suggested that a lower
monitor placement would encourage movement as users
could assume a wider variety of postures without sacriÞ-
cing visual acuity. Instead, our results reveal the in-
creased desire to shift postures in both conditions over
the duration of the task, thus reßecting the discomfort
reported in subjective questioning.
Video analysis further allowed for quantiÞcation of
head tilt. The average head tilt, measured below the
horizontal, at the 40¡ condition was !21.3 (SD"8.7)¡
and 3.1 (SD"6.8)¡ during the 15 ¡ condition. The 40 ¡
condition was found to necessitate a signiÞcantly greater
head tilt than the 15¡ condition. A lower monitor height
is recommended for its ßexibility in allowing the user to
assume either a lower gaze angle or greater head tilt
(Ankrum and Nemeth, 1995). Our Þndings indicate that
subjects at the lower monitor condition are not shifting
between the postures that are theoretically available and
are instead assuming a relatively static deviated posture.
In a previous study, Cha¦n (1973) found no musculo-
skeletal problems when the head tilt was less than 15 ¡,
but when the head tilt increased to 30 ¡, fatigue was
apparent. However, there has been insu¦cient evidence
of fatigue within the range of 15¡ and 30¡ of head tilt. The
21.3¡ of average head tilt found at the 40¡ condition falls
into this range. Although fatigue was not present in the
FFT analysis, the signiÞcantly elevated muscle activity in
the erector spinae indicates that the muscles were work-
ing harder to maintain the deviated head position. Fu-
ture work that includes a more realistic 8 h work day
may be necessary to elicit the longer term e¤ects of
muscular fatigue, visual fatigue, degraded operator per-
formance and distinct changes in operator physiology.
Conclusions
As the use of VDTs increases in the home, school and
workplace, the number of related physical discomfort
complaints is likely to increase. Many have suggested
alternate workstation conÞgurations in an attempt to
alleviate physical factors promoting discomfort. One
such suggestion is lowering the height of the video dis-
play terminal, which has been argued to reduce visual
fatigue, allow the user to assume a wider variety of
postures and reduce static loading. In this study we
compared a 15¡ and 40¡ below horizontal monitor place-
ment and investigated the correlating physical di¤erences
during an experimental session. The expressed advant-
ages of the lower monitor were not found in this 2 h
