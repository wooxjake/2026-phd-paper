ABSTRACT
Laptop work may increase risks of neck and back injuries in the long term due to awkward postures and 
increased mechanical loads on cervical and lumbar spine. This study was intended to compare the 
muscle activities and postural changes as well as discomfort of the neck and back resulted from the 
laptop work in a conventional and adjusted setup. 20 young healthy males performed two 8-min 
sessions of computer-mouse work and typing in a conventional setup and in an ergonomically adjusted 
setup during laptop work. In the adjusted setup, the screen height was individually adjusted by a riser 
and the keyboard was externally connected. The posture of the cervical, thoracic, lumbar spines, and 
shoulder, as well as electromyographic activity of cervical and lumbar erector spinae, multifidus, upper 
trapezius and anterior deltoid muscles, were recorded during the first and last 2 min of each computer 
work session. Having completed the computer work session, furthermore, the participants scored their 
neck, back, and general discomfort. The very research findings implied that adjusting the screen height 
and keyboard placement was effective during laptop work in reducing the cervical (−4.53 deg) and 
thoracic flexion angles (−3.14 deg) as well as the cervical erector spinae (−10.31%) and multifidus 
(−15.57%) muscle activities, and resulted in lower discomfort. The typing task was associated with 
higher muscle activity and awkward posture compared with the mouse work. As for workstation 
setup, simple modifications may reduce risk factors of musculoskeletal complaints.
1. Introduction
Using laptop has become very popular owing to its portability 
and light weight (Osama et al., 2018 ; Straker et al., 1997a ; 
Woo et al., 2016 ). Many people, including students, have 
a sedentary work pattern over long periods, especially as 
working with electronic devices like laptops and tablets 
(Ryan et al., 2011 ; Straker et al., 2018 ). Based on several 
studies, the continuous use of a laptop has been found out 
to be associated with increased risks of musculoskeletal com -
plaints, specifically discomfort and pain in the neck, shoulder, 
and back regions (Allafan et al., 2018 ; Arshad et al., 2020 ; 
Hafeez et al., 2013 ; Janneck et al., 2018 ; Osama et al., 2018 ; 
Werth & Babski-Reeves, 2014 ; Woo et al., 2016 ). Apart from 
short- and long-term adverse effects caused by musculoskele -
tal disorders (MSD) on individual’ quality of life and perfor -
mance (Suka & Yoshida, 2008 ), MSD can result in a huge 
economic burden (Stewart et al., 2003 ). Thus, studying possi -
ble risk factors of neck and back discomfort, associated with 
working with a laptop, can be effective to prevent and manage 
such a device-related musculoskeletal complaints. As com -
pared to desktop computers, previous studies have shown 
that laptop work is associated with a greater neck flexion 
(Reinert et al., 2018 ; Straker et al., 1997a ; Yu et al., 2018 ) 
and discomfort (Bubric & Hedge, 2016 ; Straker et al., 1997a ). 
A reason for this awkward working posture and increased 
discomfort can be related to the attachment of the keyboard 
to the screen in the design of the laptop, which does not allow 
for fitting such computer components to the users’ anthropo -
metrics parameters (Arshad et al., 2020 ; Werth & Babski- 
Reeves, 2014 ). As the laptop screen and keyboard are attached 
to each other, the proper screen height is purposely deemed 
lower than the recommended one for computer work (Asundi 
et al., 2012 ; Seghers et al., 2003 ). Placing the laptop on 
a higher workstation surface may spontaneously adjust the 
screen height, but this can increase an awkward posture risk 
in shoulder, forearm and wrist as the keyboard placement 
does, then, not follow the recommended ergonomic guide -
lines (G. G. Simoneau & Marklin, 2001 ; Zhu & Shin, 2012 ). 
Previous studies have indicated that an elevated keyboard 
height might increase shoulder abduction and flexion angles 
as well as shoulder and neck extensor muscle activities 
(Erdelyil et al., 1988 ; Life & Pheasant, 1984 ; Zhu & Shin, 
2012 ). Particularly, increased shoulder flexion and abduction 
may result in increased the trapezius tonic activity (Aarås 
et al., 2000 ; Aarås & Westgaard, 1987 ; Chiu et al., 2015 ; Life 
& Pheasant, 1984 ). The elevated keyboard height can

subsequently cause the shoulder, neck, and whole body dis -
comfort (Balliett et al., 1996 ; Bergqvist et al., 1995b ; Erdelyil 
et al., 1988 ; Price & Dowell, 1998 ; Wu & Yang, 2005 ; Zhu & 
Shin, 2012 ). In order to prevent such awkward forearm and 
shoulder postures, along with raising the level of the laptop, 
an external keyboard is also recommended (Arshad et al., 
2020 ; Berkhout et al., 2004 ).
Several studies have been conducted to explore using lap -
top risers and external keyboard during laptop work. 
However, these studies have rarely addressed the load distri -
bution on the neck, shoulder and back muscles objectively 
(Asundi et al., 2012 ; Berkhout et al., 2004 ; Sommerich et al., 
2002 ). Asundi et al. ( 2012 ) compared postural changes while 
using a simple inclined stand under the laptop, as well as 
a commercially available riser with an external mouse and 
a keyboard. Their study showed that using a keyboard, riser, 
and a separate mouse reduced neck flexion. However, no 
information was observed in their findings about the level of 
neck, back, and shoulder muscle activities. Having simulta -
neously evaluated muscle activities and postural changes, 
further insight would be provided into the biomechanical 
loads associated with the workstation setup (Asundi et al., 
2012 ; Szeto & Lee, 2002 ). Berkhout et al. ( 2004 ) have demon -
strated that the discomfort and the mechanical load is reduced 
on the neck by increasing the laptop height and using an 
external keyboard. Additionally, the user performance (the 
number of correct typed entries in a unit of time) has been 
improved (Berkhout et al., 2004 ). However, this investigation 
has been limited to assess the discomfort and the torque 
around the neck, and the load distribution on the neck and 
back muscles has not been assessed. Low back pain has often 
been reported to be associated with sitting and working with 
computers or laptop (Anuar et al., 2016 ; Hafeez et al., 2013 ; 
Janneck et al., 2018 ; Ren et al., 2019 ; Stewart et al., 2003 ). 
Furthermore, the back position affects the posture and muscle 
load in the neck and shoulder (Aarås et al., 2000 ). Similarly, 
back muscle activities are affected by the shoulder/neck and 
upper back postures (Kamil & Dawal, 2015 ; Keyserling et al., 
2005 ; Lee et al., 2017 ). Therefore, it is important to evaluate 
the full picture of neck, shoulder, and back posture and 
muscle activities during laptop work. Although computer 
mouse work and typing on a keyboard are reckoned very 
typical computer tasks, both tasks have been shown to impose 
quite different demands in terms of neck and shoulder pos -
ture and muscle activities (Aarås et al., 2000 ; Szeto et al., 2009 ; 
Won et al., 2009 ). In addition, previous studies reported 
somewhat contradictory findings as comparing mouse work 
and typing task in terms of difference of neck and shoulder 
posture and muscle activities (Cooper & Straker, 1998 ; 
J. Dennerlein et al., 2002 ; Szeto et al., 2009 ; Yang & Cho, 
2012 ). Thus, studying the load distribution in these two tasks 
and the workstation setup effect on the load distribution 
seems mostly relevant.
As outlined above, to the best of our knowledge, no study 
has simultaneously investigated the workstation setup effect 
on the activity of neck, shoulder, and back muscles as well as 
the postural changes during laptop work. In particular, pre -
vious studies have not compared the workstation setup effect 
on back muscle activities during laptop work. Thus, this study 
was purposed to compare the muscle activities, postural 
changes, and discomfort of the neck and back during the 
laptop work in both conventional and adjusted setups (adjust -
ing screen height and keyboard placement). Even though the 
muscle activity level is often quantified by the amplitude of 
EMG signals, i.e., EMG RMS, the direct measure relevance of 
EMG amplitude has been debated in predicting the muscu -
loskeletal disorder risk in low load tasks (Hansson, Balogh, 
et al., 2000 ). As an alternative relative rest time (RRT) has 
been suggested to be effective in reflecting the muscular 
loading risk in such tasks, and it is less sensitive to inter- 
subject variability (Hansson, Nordander, et al., 2000 ). 
Previous studies have demonstrated that the low degree of 
RRT in neck/shoulder muscles has been being deemed a risk 
factor to develop work-related musculoskeletal disorders 
(Thorn et al., 2007 ; Veiersted et al., 1993 ). Thus, the RRT 
analysis along with the muscle activity level and postural 
change can be useful to compare laptop work in both con -
ventional and adjusted setups. It was hypothesized that the 
neck, shoulder, and lumbar flexion angles, the neck and 
shoulder muscle activities and the neck and back discomfort 
would be lower (but relative rest time of the neck and 
shoulder muscles would be higher) during the laptop work 
in the adjusted setup compared with the conventional one.
2. Materials & methods
2.1. Participants and ethical consideration
Twenty healthy male university students (age: 27.05 ± 
3.5 years, height: 176.9 ± 5.5 cm, weight: 71 ± 11.8 kg, BMI: 
22.4 ± 3.0) participated voluntarily in this study. The partici -
pants have been frequently using computer or laptop with 
5.6 ± 3.1 hours a day. The physical activity readiness ques -
tionnaire (PAR-Q) (Cardinal & Cardinal, 1995 ) and the mod -
ified Standardized Nordic Questionnaires (Dawson et al., 
2009 ) were adopted to verify the participant healthiness. 
Histories of neurological-cardiovascular problems, significant 
back and neck pains (in the past 6 months) resulted in greater 
than 3 days off work or school, current neck, or low back 
pains at the time of the research were included in the study 
exclusion criteria. All the procedures and experimental pro -
tocols performed, including Ethics for human participants, 
were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Human 
Subjects at Kharazmi University, Tehran, Iran. The partici -
pants have been thoroughly provided with instructions so as 
to explain the study purpose and procedures, and they signed 
a consent form following highly informed.
2.2. Experimental protocol
Initially, the participants were introduced to the experimental 
procedure, and familiarized with the experimental computer 
task (as described below). Then, the skin surface preparation 
(i.e., shaving and rinsing with alcohol) was carried out to put 
the EMG electrodes according to the SENIAM guidelines 
(Hermens et al., 2000 ). Having placed the electrode, inertial 
motion sensors were located based on the manufacturer’s 
recommendations (Noraxon U.S.A. Inc, 2014 ) to assess

posture. Finally, the workstation was set up. The tasks file and 
the word files related to performing the typing and mouse 
work tasks were prepared on the desktop to the participant, 
and the chair height was adjusted for each individual so that 
the feet were placed on the ground, and the knee angle would 
be 90°.
The participants sat on a chair without backrest and armrests 
to reduce motion artifact caused by physical contact on the 
electrode surface. They performed a computer task with two 
workstation setups, namely, the conventional and the adjusted 
ones. The laptop was placed on a desk, and the desk height was 
adjusted to be 5 cm above the elbow level corresponding to the 
requirements supposed for light precision tasks (Sanders & 
McCormick, 1998 ; Simoneau & Marklin, 2001 ). In the conven -
tional setup, the laptop was placed so that its keyboard (spacebar 
key) was some 10 cm away from the near edge of the desk 
(Kroemer & Grandjean, 1997 ). In the adjusted setup, the screen 
height was specifically adjusted to each individual and the key -
board was externally connected. A commercially available height 
adjustable riser (Cooling Pad, 638 (A), china; a maximum of 
18 cm in five steps) was placed underneath the laptop, and the 
screen height was adjusted so that the top edge of the laptop 
screen was at the level of the participants’ eye. The distance 
deemed between the riser and the table edge was some 22 cm. 
An external keyboard (XP -product, XP-8004, China; keyboard 
dimension: 38.7 × 12.2 × 2.3 cm) was located 10 cm away from 
the table edge (to get the same distance between keyboard and 
the table edge in both setups), and was used by the individual in 
the adjusted setup ( Figure 1. (A,B )).
Prior to the laptop work, EMG was recorded from the 
muscles (as mentioned in the next section) over an instructed 
10-s period rest (a reference-sitting posture) where 
participants sat in a relaxed position with their hands resting 
on the laps. Each muscle activity was used during the refer -
ence sitting in order to calculate a resting threshold to be used 
in computing the relative rest time of each muscle (Samani 
et al., 2009a ).
As for in each setup (i.e., conventional and adjusted), the 
computer tasks contained two 8-min segments with an 
1–2 min in-between break to score discomfort and save 
EMG and posture data in a computer. One of the segments 
involved computer mouse work, and the other included 
typing on the laptop ( Figure 1. C). In the typing task, the 
participant typed a text about body discomfort and sitting 
posture (McGill et al., 2003 ; Messing et al., 2008 ; Woo et al., 
2016 ) displayed in the upper part of the screen (as a pdf file 
on the desktop) for 8 minutes with a self-selected pace. 
Throughout the typing task, the participants were not 
allowed to use the mouse or to return the written words 
to correct them (Xie et al., 2015 ). As with the mouse work 
tasks, the participants copied a certain section of the text 
(about body discomfort and sitting posture) contained in 
the word file on the desktop screen, and pasted into a word 
table already prepared for this task. The text piece to copy 
and paste was highlighted green, and numbered to indicate 
the pasting location in the table. In each cell of the table, 
they received a textual command to change the font size, 
weight, and color or highlight the text. The participants 
were required to make changes only with the mouse. They 
placed their inactive arm on the desk, and were not allowed 
to lean on the inactive arm during the mouse work tasks. As 
for both tasks, no limitation was imposed on trunk move -
ments and leg splay but the participants were not allowed to 
cross their legs or change their sitting position on the seat 
pan. The orders of performing the mouse work and the 
typing task in each setup, and of adjusting the setups were 
randomly chosen and counter-balanced across the partici -
pants. There were five-minute breaks between the two 
setups.
2.3. Data recordings
The unilateral electromyographic (EMG) activities of cervical 
erector spinae (CES), anterior deltoid (AD), upper trapezius 
(UT), lumbar erector spinae (LES) and multifidus (MF) mus -
cles as well as angular displacements of cervical, thoracic and 
lumbar spines and shoulder joint in sagittal plane were 
recorded in the first and last 2 minutes of each segment but 
the participants were not informed of the recording initiation. 
The recording operations were initiated following the 
first minute to provide the participants with a minute to be 
acclimated. Having immediately completed each task, the 
participants scored their level of neck, back, and general 
discomfort on a 0–10 scale. Discomfort was rated using an 
adapted body map and discomfort scale with end-point 
anchors of ‘no discomfort: 0' and 'unbearable discomfort: 10' 
(Antle et al., 2015 ; Messing et al., 2008 ). Having completed 
the laptop work in both setups, furthermore, the participants 
were asked to indicate which the workstation setup was gen -
erally more comfortable (as participants' setup preference to

A wireless surface EMG Noraxon DTS system (Noraxon 
Inc., USA) was used to collect electromyographic activities of 
CES, AD, UT, LES and MF muscles. Self-adhesive, pregelled, 
surface disposable Ag/AgCl electrodes (F-RG1, Skintact, 
Austria) were used for the signal collection. Inter electrode 
distance was set to 2 cm. Electrodes were then connected to 
wireless EMG sensors (DTS EMG sensors; Noraxon) by EMG 
pinch lead wires (CMRR > 100 dB, Gain: 500). The signals 
were transmitted to a 16-bit analogue to digital (A/D) con -
verter receiver (Noraxon DTS receiver), and saved to 
a computer at a rate of 1500 Hz using the MR3 software 
(Version 3.10, Noraxon). The same investigator placed the 
electrodes on all the participants. The placement over the 
CES was approximately 1 cm to the right side of the C5 
vertebra. Specifically, the electrodes were placed for UT at 
the midpoint between C7 and the acromion. As for AD, the 
electrodes were located at 2 cm distal and anterior to the 
acromion, and as for LES, the electrodes were placed at 
4 cm lateral from the L1 spinous process. As with MF, the 
electrodes were aligned with a line from posterior superior 
iliac spine to the interspace between L1 and L2, and they were 
placed at the L5 spinous process level (i.e. about 2– 3 cm from 
the midline) (Basmajian & Blumenstein, 1980 ; Hermens & 
Freriks, 2017 ).
The angular displacement was captured by Noraxon’s 3D 
MyoMotion analysis system consisting of a set of (1 to 16) 
sensors using Inertial Sensor technology (MyoMotion 
Clinical, Noraxon U.S.A. Inc.). In the present study, five 
motion sensors were located on the bony landmarks of the 
external occipital protuberance, C7, T12, the bony area of 
the sacrum, and distal section of upper arm according to the 
manufacturer’s recommendations to measure cervical, thor -
acic, lumbar, and shoulder angular displacements. The pro -
cedure details of angular displacement measurement have 
been described (Noraxon U.S.A. Inc, 2014 ; Xie et al., 2018 ). 
Each sensor could provide a quaternion indicating its own 
spatial orientation. As a further matter, Noraxon’s MR3 
Software automatically converts the quaternion data into 
angular displacement using a rigid body model with the 
joint segments having a sensor attachment. The angular 
displacement corresponded to shoulder, cervical, thoracic 
and lumbar flexion/extension was measured. Prior to the 
experiment start, a resting trial was recorded as the partici -
pant standing upright and looking straight ahead to capture 
reference (null) positions of the sensors.
2.4. Data analysis
EMG signals were digitally band-pass filtered (Butterworth, 
4th order, 20–500 Hz). Furthermore, a second order 
Butterworth digital filter of 30 Hz (high pass) was applied to 
remove the cardiac signal (Montes et al., 2017 ). To avoid any 
transient effect, the first and last 10 seconds of mouse work 
and typing tasks were removed before processing. Thus, 
a 100-second EMG recording was used to analyze the dis -
cussed tasks.
Root mean square (RMS) was computed across 500-ms 
non-overlapping epochs on EMG signals from each muscle, 
and then the RMS values were averaged across the epochs (St- 
Onge et al., 2017 ). As a further matter, relative rest time 
(RRT) was computed across 10-second non-overlapping 
epochs, and averaged across the epochs. RRT is reckoned 
a value between 0 and 100% representing the percent time 
that the RMS of the EMG computed over a 100 ms was below 
a set threshold for a minimum of 250 ms (Hermens & 
Vollenbroek-Hutten, 2004 ; Samani et al., 2009a ). The resting 
threshold was obtained based on signals recorded during the 
reference sitting, and adjusted with increase or decrease in 5% 
steps such that RRT was at least 99% in the rest period (St- 
Onge et al., 2017 ). The mean (average) cervical, thoracic, 
lumbar, and shoulder flexion/extension angles were calculated 
across 100 seconds of the posture recording at the beginning 
(2–3 minutes) and at the end of each task (7–8 minutes) to 
assess the postural configuration during the tasks (Straker 
et al., 1997a ; Yang & Cho, 2012 ; Yu et al., 2018 ).
2.5. Statistical analysis
SPSS version 23.0 (IBM, USA) was used to conduct the 
statistical analysis. A linear-mixed model (LMM) was applied 
to fit the outcome measures, i.e., RMS, RRT, posture and 
discomfort score (West et al., 2014 ). To find the most parsi -
monious and the best fit model, a “top-down” modeling 
strategy (West et al., 2014 ) was applied. Moreover, worksta -
tion setup (conventional and adjusted), tasks (mouse work 
and typing) and time (beginning and end) for RMS, RRT and 
average postural angles as well as workstation setup (conven -
tional and adjusted) and tasks (mouse work and typing) for 
the discomfort rate were introduced as within-subject factors 
of the LMM. Having found out the fitting model, the residual 
of the model was checked to verify its normality. When 
a significant interactive effect was indicated, a Bonferroni 
adjustment was performed to compare the stratified dataset. 
In all the tests, significance was assumed at P < .05. In order 
to better compare the significant interaction results of setup × 
task, the percentage difference was calculated in RMS/RRT/ 
postural angles of the same task (mouse work or typing) 
between conventional and adjusted setups according to the

3. Results
3.1. Muscle activities
The workstation setup had a significant effect on the RMS of 
CES and MF muscles ( Table 1 ). In the adjusted setup, the 
RMS of CES and MF was significantly lower as compared to 
the conventional one, but the LES indicated a tendency 
toward a slight higher RMS in the adjusted setup ( Table 1 
and Figure 2 ). Additionally, the task type significantly affected 
the RMS of all muscles ( Table 1 ). RMS of all muscles was 
deemed significantly higher in the typing task as compared

with the mouse work task ( Figure 2 ). Interaction of setup × 
task had a significant effect on the AD ( Table 1 ). RMS of 
the AD was much less in adjusted setup than the conventional 
setup during typing [−21.11%]. However, it did not have any 
marked sign of a difference [5.02%] in the mouse work task 
( Figure 2 ).
RRT was significantly higher for the CES in the adjusted setup 
compared with the conventional one ( Table 1 and Figure 3 ). As 
for all muscles, RRT was significantly reckoned lower in the type 
task as compared with the mouse work one ( Table 1 and Figure 3 ). 
Interaction of setup × task indicated a significant effect on RRT of 
the CES and AD muscles ( Table 1 ). RRT was reckoned much 
more in the CES during typing in adjusted setup as compared 
with the conventional [118.84%], but this difference was modest 
in the mouse work task [59.26%] ( Figure 3 ). Finally, RRT was 
reckoned much more for the AD in the adjusted setup compared 
with the conventional setup [24.68%] during typing. However, 
this difference was insignificant in the mouse work task [−8.87%] 
( Figure 3 ).
3.2. Posture
The workstation setup indicated a significant effect on the cervi -
cal and thoracic flexion angles ( Table 2 ). Furthermore, the task 
type was demonstrated to have a significant effect on the 
shoulder, neck, thoracic and lumbar flexion angles ( Table 2 ). 
The absolute posture difference between workstation setups 
(conventional-adjusted) and tasks (typing-mouse work) has 
been shown in Figure 4 . As with the adjusted setup, the cervical 
and thoracic segments of the spine were positioned in a less 
flexed posture compared with the conventional setup ( Table 2 
and Figure 4 , A). The cervical, thoracic, and shoulder flexion was 
significantly reckoned more in the type task as compared with 
the mouse work one. However, the lumbar flexion was less in the 
type task ( Table 2 and Figure 4 , B). Interaction of setup × task 
had a significant effect on the cervical, thoracic, and shoulder 
flexion ( Table 2 ). The cervical and thoracic flexion was reckoned 
much more in the conventional setup than the adjusted setup 
[6.80 and 4.75 deg, respectively] during mouse work task. 
However, this very difference was modest in the typing task 
[2.27 and 1.52 deg, respectively]. During typing, the shoulder 
flexion was much more in the conventional setup as compared 
with the adjusted one [4.66 deg] whereas it did not have any 
marked sign of a difference [0.52 deg] in the mouse work task.
3.3. Discomfort
Laptop work has increased discomfort in the neck and back in 
both workstation setups. Nonetheless, the neck discomfort 
tended to be lower in the adjusted setup compared with the 
conventional setup ( Table 3 and Figure 5 ). Further, 84% of the 
participants reported that the adjusted setup was generally 
more comfortable, and they preferred this setup for work

4. Discussion
As compared with the conventional setup, the results indicated 
that the activity level of CES and MF muscles as well as the cervical 
and thoracic flexion angles were lower (and CES resting was 
higher) during the laptop work in the adjusted setup, but its LES 
indicated a slightly higher activity level. As for all the muscles, the 
activity level as well as the cervical, thoracic, and shoulder flexion 
was reckoned higher (and RRT of all muscles was deemed lower). 
However, the lumbar flexion was lower in the typing task as 
compared with the mouse work. In the adjusted setup, neck 
discomfort tended to be lower compared with the conventional 
setup, and adjusted setup was generally less discomforting to 
participants’ neck and back.
For supporting the hypothesis and according to Villanueva 
et al. ( 1997 ) findings, the higher activity level (and lower 
resting) of CES is concomitant with the higher neck flexion 
angle in the conventional setup as compared with the adjusted 
setup. Villanueva et al. ( 1997 ) examined the influence of 
adjusting screen height in video display terminals. They 
found lower neck and shoulder muscle activities with 
increased screen height during mouse work. They have also 
reported a high correlation coefficient between the extent of 
neck flexion and the neck extensor muscle activities. Further, 
Yu et al. ( 2018 ) have shown that, compared to the desktop 
computer, using tablet, and laptop resulted in increased neck 
flexion and shoulder elevation. Hence, it seems that prolonged 
laptop work may further increase the risk of developing neck 
disorders in the conventional setup, as the participants experi -
enced a more flexed neck and an increase in the mechanical 
load imposed on the neck (Kleine et al., 1999 ; Kusumawati & 
Muslim, 2019 ; Reinert et al., 2018 ; Sommerich et al., 2002 ; 
Szeto & Lee, 2002 ; Yu et al., 2018 ).
One of the important and interesting findings obtained as 
a result of the present study was that MF muscle activities were 
lower but LES muscle activities tended to be slightly higher in the 
adjusted setup as compared with the conventional one. 
Nevertheless, no significant difference was found out in the lum -
bar flexion angle between the conventional and adjusted setups. 
However, the thoracic flexion angle was reckoned higher in the 
conventional setup compared with the adjusted setup ( Figure 3 ). 
Therefore, it appears that the discrepancy between the trend of 
MF and LES muscle activities can be attributed, across the work -
station setups, to the difference of the thoracic posture in work -
station setups (Aarås et al., 2000 ; Kamil & Dawal, 2015 ; Keyserling 
et al., 2005 ) and likely the functional role of these muscles. Even

though LES and MF synergistically counter-act the spinal flexion, 
MF has the most advantageous lever arm for posterior sagittal 
rotation as it mainly inserts at the spinous processes whereas LES 
inserts at the transverse processes. Thus, it is expected that MF 
plays a more active role in holding the flexed trunk (Mork & 
Westgaard, 2009 ). A study conducted by Kamil and Dawal ( 2015 ), 
indicated that a significant correlation was observed among the 
pelvic, trunk angles and back muscles activities. Also, the findings 
revealed that the participants demonstrated the least level of MF 
muscle activities when sitting with an extended upper trunk 
posture (Kamil & Dawal, 2015 ). As compared with adjusted 
setup, the observed longer period of postures with increased 
thoracic spine flexion in the conventional setup may have 
a negative effect on the thoracic spine musculoskeletal structures 
and other related structures such as shoulder joint (Briggs et al., 
2007 ; Clark & Lucett, 2010 ; Kleine et al., 1999 ; Klussmann et al., 
2008 ). Also, Excessive lumbar spine flexion may increase the 
tension in the lumbar passive structures during sitting, and it 
may result in back pain in the long term (Jia & Nussbaum, 
2018 ; Mork & Westgaard, 2009 ; Mörl & Bradl, 2013 ). Thus, 
considering the improvement in ergonomic workplace (Janneck 
et al., 2018 ; Kaliniene et al., 2016 ; Klussmann et al., 2008 ), using 
appropriate interventions are required to reduce thoracic and 
lumbar spine flexion during laptop work. Although the adjusted 
setup reduced the thoracic flexion and decreased MF muscle 
activities, this setup has not significantly affected the lumbar 
spine posture. Observing a flexed lumbar spine in both work -
station setups may be due to the participants’ a motor habit in 
performing computer work, rendering workstation setup adjust -
ment ineffective in altering the lumbar spine posture.
The activity level of all muscles was reckoned higher (and 
muscle resting of all muscles was lower) in the typing task as 
compared with the mouse work task in both setups. As 
expected, the higher shoulder flexion was concomitant with 
a higher activity of the AD muscle during the typing task 
compared with the mouse work task (Hermans et al., 1998 ). 
During typing task, the neck was more flexed and lumbar 
flexion was lower than the mouse work task. This can likely 
be due to the fact that the participants typed with both hands, 
but they did the mouse work with one hand; this may likely be 
because of the higher shoulder flexion during typing task. In 
addition, the activity of the CES, LES and MF muscles might be 
enhanced to stabilize the spine and counteract the torque 
created by hand movements (Borghuis et al., 2008 ; McGill 
et al., 2003 ). As for the mouse task time period, it is worth 
noting that holding the full arm supporting on the work surface 
may have further highlighted the difference between the two 
tasks. During the typing task compared with mouse work task, 
higher activity level of CES and UT muscles is consistent with 
the findings of Sommerich et al. ( 2001 ) and Szeto et al. ( 2009 ) 
studies. In the study conducted by Sommerich et al. ( 2001 ), 
neck flexion as well as left levator scapulae, left and right 
sternocleidomastoid, trapezius and neck extensor muscle activ -
ities were higher during typing compared with mouse work 
task (Sommerich et al., 2001 ). Yang and Cho ( 2012 ) have also 
reported higher neck and shoulder flexion and upper trapezius 
muscle activities during typing task compared with mouse 
work task (Yang & Cho, 2012 ). Unlike the present study, in 
a study conducted by Dennerlein et al. ( 2002 ), shoulder posture 
was deemed the most neutral for Keyboard-intensive tasks 
whereas the shoulder was flexed, abducted, and externally 
rotated during the mouse-intensive task in order to reach out 
and interact with the mouse. In a pilot study, Cooper and 
Straker ( 1998 ) have reported a higher activity of the upper 
trapezius and lower activity of the anterior deltoid during key -
board task compared to mouse task. That study also showed 
individual differences between muscle activity for mouse and 
keyboard task (Cooper & Straker, 1998 ). The discrepancies 
between the present study results and the abovementioned 
studies may be due to differences in content of task, typing 
style, and the location of the keyboard and mouse 
(J. T. Dennerlein & Johnson, 2006 ). Even though the neck 
and shoulder posture and muscle activities can be affected by 
the type of tasks, trunk posture, workstation setup including 
the position of the mouse and dimension of keyboards and 
other computer components, typing style, as well as anthropo -
metric characteristics of participants affect the posture and 
muscle activity (Arne Aarås et al., 2000 ; Cooper & Straker, 
1998 ; J. Dennerlein et al., 2002 ; J. T. Dennerlein & Johnson, 
2006 ; Otto et al., 2018 ; Sommerich et al., 2002 ; Won et al., 2009 ; 
Yang & Cho, 2012 ). Therefore, it seems that typing task, parti -
cularly in the conventional setup, may be associated with 
higher physical exposure, and may increase the likelihood of 
associated adverse effects of physical exposure on shoulder. 
Thus, the attempts to redesign the tasks are deemed important 
to reduce the required duration of typing in computer 
workstation.
The significant interactive effect of setup and task implies 
that flexion of the cervical and thoracic spine was much 
higher in the conventional setup during mouse work task 
than the adjusted setup. However, this difference was modest 
in the typing task. Due to the difference reckoned in the 
screen height in two workstation setups, the cervical and 
thoracic spine was positioned in a less flexed posture during 
the mouse work. Nonetheless, the role of screen height may 
not probably be influential during typing task because the 
participants may have frequently kept their gazing eye on 
the keyboard (Kamil & Dawal, 2015 ; Schüldt, 1988 ). 
Therefore, it seems that adjusting the workstation ergonomi -
cally may be more effective in improving the neck and thor -
acic posture during mouse work than typing. The CES muscle 
resting (RRT of the CES) was much higher during typing in 
the adjusted setup than the conventional setup. But this dif -
ference was reckoned modest in the mouse work task. This 
was likely due to frequent flexion and extension of the neck to 
shift the gaze between the screen and the keyboard in the 
typing task whereas the gaze would likely be kept on the 
screen during the mouse work. As for the typing task, the 
observed higher neck flexion would also support this explana -
tion. Also, the AD muscle resting was much higher during the 
typing task in the adjusted setup (as well as the activity level 
was much lower) as compared with the conventional setup. 
However, such the difference was modest in the mouse work 
task. Further, shoulder flexion angle during typing in the 
adjusted setup was much lower than the conventional setup, 
but this difference seemed modest in the mouse work task. 
These observations are most likely related to the need for 
hand movements over the entire keyboard surface. As

Neck discomfort tended to be lower in the adjusted setup 
as compare with the conventional setup. This difference is 
deemed to be due to a higher CES muscle resting and less 
cervical and thoracic flexion in the adjusted setup compared 
with the conventional one (Berkhout et al., 2004 ; Chiou et al., 
2012 ; Villanueva et al., 1997 ; Yu et al., 2018 ). Based on 
biomechanical principles, one may assume that an upright 
neck position will result in less mechanical load on the neck. 
Furthermore, a more flexed neck results in higher activity of 
the cervical extensor muscles in the conventional setup 
(Seghers et al., 2003 ; Villanueva et al., 1997 ). Berkhout et al. 
( 2004 ) have shown that, during continuous laptop work, the 
access to adjusting screen height, distance, and the use of an 
external keyboard and mouse will improve user comfort and 
productivity, and reduce the mechanical load on the neck. 
Moreover, greater increase in the neck flexion, head tilt, and 
neck discomfort were reported during laptop work as com -
pared with working on a desktop computer (Bubric & Hedge, 
2016 ; Straker et al., 1997a ; Yu et al., 2018 ). Thus, prolonged 
laptop use has a greater potential to lead to over-exert the 
neck musculature in the conventional setup.
One of the limitations faced in this study was the use of the 
chair without backrest. Considering that the lumbar erector 
spinae and multifidus muscles sensors were placed on the back 
and regarding the participants were not able to lean on the 
backrest, a chair without backrest was used in this experiment 
as it has also been used in previous studies (Callaghan & 
McGILL, 2001 ; Jia & Nussbaum, 2018 ; Waongenngarm et al., 
2016 ). As a further matter, the external keyboard was placed in 
front of the laptop in the adjusted setup. Thus, the distance 
between the laptop screen and the edge of the table was slightly 
more than the conventional setup. Although this may affect the 
interface between the user and the laptop, it is not expected to 
cause a bias in reduced estimation of physical exposure in favor 
of the adjusted setup. In the present study, only male students 
were recruited, and female students were not included. This was 
firstly to keep the subject pool homogenous in this study, and it 
was secondly caused due to legal prohibition of the engagement 
of male researchers in physical contacts with female participants 
in our country. Noting that the incidence of neck and shoulder 
complaints are more frequent among females than males, one 
should be cautious about generalizing our results to a female 
population (Heidari et al., 2019 ; Won et al., 2009 ; Yang & Cho, 
2012 ), and further researches are necessary to verify whether the 
result will be reproduced in females. Any information was not 
collected on the participants’ history of laptop use as compared 
to desktop computer use, even though most of them were 
frequent laptop users. It is conceivable that the participants’ 
history of computer use would affect their response to the 
given task, and might affect how they perceive the level of 
discomfort. In spite of this limitation, our study is concerned 
with within-subject changes across the two conditions, and is not 
particularly sensitive to inter-subject variability.
We opted for a fixed-size workstation setup across all the 
participants to only change the studied characteristic of the 
workstation setup (i.e., screen height), and keep the rest of 
variables controlled and identical across the conditions. 
However, it cannot be denied the advantage of tailoring the 
workstation setup for each individual even though it could 
have made the implementation more time consuming and 
open for further discussions about the proper choice of set 
limits. Another important consideration when adjusting the 
height of a laptop screen during laptop work in the adjusted 
setup was the allowance of trunk movements. The screen 
height was initially adjusted in an upright seating posture. 
But the screen height may have been placed above the level 
of the participants’ eye due to the trunk flexion throughout 
the task, and it may have led to non-neutral posterior head 
tilt. Despite such a limitation, subtle trunk movement may 
also preclude postural fixity as a positive effect during the 
task. However, the influence extent of such subtle postural 
variation is difficult in long term to precisely be assessed.
Our results do not suggest that improving the workstation 
setup may alone alter the extent of lumbar flexion during 
laptop work. Thus, a cognitive and physical training protocol 
may be useful to change the motor habits of sitting in 
a specific position (Dockrell et al., 2010 ; Kaliniene et al., 
2016 ).
5. Conclusion
The results of this study indicated that the use of the ergonomi -
cally adjusted setup (using a riser under the laptop and external 
keyboard) compared with the conventional setup, reduced the 
cervical, thoracic, and shoulder flexion angles as well as the 
activity level of cervical erector spinae and multifidus muscles. 
Cervical erector spinae muscle resting also increased in the 
adjusted setup. Further, the adjusted setup was generally less 
discomforting for participants’ neck and back. The activity level 
of all muscles as well as the cervical, thoracic, and shoulder 
flexion was higher in the typing task compared with the mouse 
work task in both setups. These findings emphasize the impor -
tance of matching the ergonomic design of the laptops to match 
with anthropometric scales of individuals. Given the prevalence 
of laptops and the high rate of laptop use, it is recommended that 
users who use laptop in a long-term and sedentary positions 
should use a riser and external keyboard as using the laptop to 
improve the neck and back condition, and reduce the likelihood 
of musculoskeletal problems. In addition, these findings imply 
that manufacturers and industrial designers of the laptops should 
consider designing laptops with adjustable screen height and 
keyboard placement. The typing task may be associated with 
higher physical exposure, and it may increase the likelihood of 
associated adverse effects of physical exposure on shoulder and 
neck in the long run. Thus, it is advisable to reduce the extent of 
typing task in the construct of computer tasks. Although the 
adjusted setup reduced the thoracic flexion and decreased MF 
muscle activities, this setup alone did not alter the lumbar flexion. 
A cognitive and physical training protocol may be useful along 
with improving the workstation setup to change the motor habits 
of sitting in a specific position.

About the Authors
Mohammad Yadgaripour received the PhD degree in corrective exer -
cise and sports injuries from Kharazmi University of Tehran, Iran, in 
2019. He is a Teacher and Adjunct Professor in Farhangian 
University – pardis of Allameh Tabatabai of Khorramabad, Iran. His 
research interests include exercise therapy, core stability, and work- 
related musculoskeletal problems.
Malihe Hadadnezhad holds a BSc in physical therapy from Shahid 
Beheshti University, MA and PhD in corrective exercise and sports 
injuries from Tehran University and is Assistant professor in 
Department of Biomechanics and sport injuries in Kharazmi 
University. Her researches focus on exercise therapy, rehabilitation 
and corrective exercises.
Ali Abbasi is an Assistant Professor in Sports Biomechanics at Kharazmi 
University. He focuses his research on the Nonlinear Dynamic Analysis 
in Sports Biomechanics, coordination, coordination variability in repeti -
tive human movements, Neuromechanics and Gait analysis.
Fereshteh Eftekhari holds a PhD in sports biomechanics from 
Kharazmi University of Tehran. She is an assistant professor at Shiraz 
University. Her current research focuses on neuromuscular control and 
biomechanical analysis of sport and basic movement.
Afshin Samani received the PhD degree in biomedical engineering 
and science from Aalborg University, Denmark, in 2010. He is cur -
rently an Associate Professor in sports science and ergonomics. His 
research interests include the methods of quantification of work 
exposure in relation to musculoskeletal disorders.
