Abstract 
Continuous seated postures may increase the risk of adverse health outcomes such 
as low-back pain, and this risk may be influenced by several modifying influences.  In 
the present study, we aimed to quantify the effects of continuous sitting and 
psychosocial stress under an unsupported sitting condition.  Fourteen participants 
completed continuous, 40-min. periods of computer-based tasks, involving both low 
and higher levels of psychosocial stress, while using a laptop computer without a 
desk.  Continuous sitting significantly increased perceived discomfort (particularly in 
the upper and lower back), trunk flexion, and metrics of localized muscle fatigue.  A 
higher level of psychosocial stress increased estimated lumbosacral compression 
forces (by ~12%).  Only weak correlations were found between subjective and 
objective measures, while various fatigue metrics showed a good level of 
correspondence with each other.  These results could support the future evaluation or 
design of diverse seated work configurations. 
 
Keywords: Continuous sitting; Psychosocial stress; Perceived discomfort; Muscle 
Fatigue; Lumbosacral kinematics; Spine Biomechanics; Laptop Computer Use; 
Unsupported Sitting

1 Introduction 
Sedentary behavior, especially prolonged continuous sitting, presents emerging 
health risks in both occupational and non-occupational settings (Owen et al., 2014).  
Recent estimates indicate that many adults maintain seated postures in over 90% of 
their leisure time (Chau et al., 2012) and over half of their working hours (Thorp et al., 
2012; Bronwyn et al., 2015).  Furthermore, individuals who work mainly in a seated 
posture are more likely to experience or aggravate low back pain (LBP: NIOSH, 1997; 
Dankaerts et al., 2006; O'Sullivan et al., 2006; Womersley and May, 2006).  Sitting 
behaviors can also be affected by diverse job-related factors, such as the working

===== PAGE 5 =====

environment (Magnusson and Pope, 1998; Delleman et al., 2004), seated work 
duration (Keyserling, 1991; Pitman and Ntuen, 1996; Stricker et al., 2003), or 
psychological task stress (Ellegast et al., 2007; Taib and Yun, 2014).  
 
Due to the flexibility of using mobile devices, such as smartphones, laptops, or tablets, 
many users choose to use these portable devices in situations without a desk. In 
addition, many places involve situations in which unsupported seating is all that is 
available (i.e., non-desk conditions with or without a backrest). For example, flexible 
library and activity-based, open-plan office concepts turn many workplaces into a 
more flexible environment, but with no designated workstation assigned to any user. 
To maximize occupancy, unsupported seating environments are also common 
solutions provided in public areas, such as airport or coffee shops.  These 
environments often provide a mix of benches, chairs, booths, and lounge areas 
(Figure 1) for various collaborative activities, but they can also require individuals to 
work in unsupported seating conditions (Rianne et al. 2011, Asher 2017). The current 
study employed particular scenarios involving unsupported sitting, simulating 
continuous working conditions with limited in-chair movements. Using mobile devices 
under such sitting conditions could increase exposure to postures that may increase 
the risk of musculoskeletal disorders (Kim 2015, Yassierli and Juraida 2016).

Prolonged sitting often results in discomfort and/or pain in the lower back (Fenety et 
al., 2000; Beach et al., 2005; Dunk and Callaghan, 2005; Gregory et al., 2006).  
However, the effects of a single continuous period of sitting on worker performance 
are not well studied, and there remains a lack of evidence quantifying the 
development of discomfort and associated risks. In addition, high mental workloads 
and stressful working schedules are often characteristics of modern jobs that require 
continuous sitting.  Psychosocial stress describes non-physical aspects of work, such 
as time pressure and high information processing demands, each of which has been 
found to contribute to the risks of developing work-related musculoskeletal disorders 
(Bongers et al. 1993, Hartvigsen et al. 2004). More specifically, psychosocial stress 
has been related to the development of work-related musculoskeletal disorders in 
upper extremities (Hughes et al., 2007; Thiese, et al., 2015), shoulder (Blangsted et 
al., 2004; Mehta and Agnew, 2012), and back (Hartvigsen et al., 2004; Marras et al., 
2000; Kato, et al., 2013; Taib and Yun, 2014). However, the combined effects of 
psychosocial stress and continuous sitting on LBP risks have not been quantified.

There is also some inconsistency in reported evidence regarding the relationship 
between these factors and the risk of sitting-related LBP (Leboeuf-Yde, 2004; 
Womersley and May, 2006; Lis et al., 2007), which may be due, at least in part, to the 
insensitivity of the evaluation methods employed.  The effects of continuous sitting on 
lumbosacral loads may be caused by time-dependent changes in muscle fatigability 
and/or viscoelastic behavior of passive trunk components (Bigland-Ritchie et al., 1995; 
McLean et al., 1997; Keller and Nathan, 1999; Beach et al., 2005).  Localized muscle 
fatigue has been measured via muscle EMG to assess the effects of continuous 
sitting on the lumbosacral region (McLean et al., 1997; Seghers et al., 2003), yet 
EMG-based assessments of muscle fatigue can be relatively less effective while 
muscle activities are less than 15% of maximal muscle capacity (Öberg et al., 1992; 
Öberg et al., 1994). Other more sensitive methods, such as based on external muscle 
stimulation, provide an alternative approach to reliably detect muscle fatigue during 
low-level muscle contractions (Johnson, 1998, Jia et al., 2015; Jeremy et al., 2015; 
Garcia, et al., 2015).  An accurate estimation of lumbosacral loads during prolonged 
sitting is also reliant on appropriately modeling viscoelastic tissue behaviors.  No 
existing study, to our knowledge, has quantified potential time-dependent changes in 
lumbosacral loads resulted from continuous sitting, with consideration of both the 
active (via muscle fatigue) and passive (via viscoelastic behaviors) contributions of 
the lumbar tissue. 
 
Therefore, this study was designed to determine the effects of continuous sitting and 
psychosocial stress under an unsupported sitting condition, using both subjective 
(discomfort) and objective (lumbosacral kinematics and kinetics, muscle activity, and 
muscle fatigue) measures.  Participants completed two unsupported, laptop-based 
Accepted Manuscript

===== PAGE 8 =====

seated tasks, intended to represent a continuous, unsupported working condition with 
limited in-chair movements.  We hypothesized that continuous sitting and 
psychosocial stress would have significant adverse effects that are reflected in both 
subjective and objective measures, and that there would be a correspondence 
between these different measures.  A longer-term goal of this effort was to expand 
our understanding of the potential adverse consequences of continuous sitting and 
psychosocial stress under unsupported sitting conditions, and to provide information 
to develop practical interventions to reduce sitting-related discomfort and injury risks.    
 
2 Methods 
2.1 Participants 
Fourteen participants (gender balanced) completed the experiment and were 
recruited from the local student population and community.  Mean (SD) age, stature, 
and body mass were 24.5 (4.1) yrs, 174.5 (9.1) cm, and 72.3 (11.1) kg, respectively.  
After a demonstration of the experimental protocols, all participants completed an 
informed consent process approved by the Institutional Review Board.  No 
participants had any self-reported musculoskeletal injuries or neurological diseases 
within the past year, and individuals with body mass index >30 kg/m2 were excluded 
due to the potential difficulties in obtaining surface EMG measures and evoking 
reliable muscle stimulation responses.   
 
2.2 Experimental Design  
Participants completed two tasks in separate trials that each involved continuous (40 
min) use of a laptop in a seated posture without a desk (as shown in Figure 2).  While 
some individuals may sit longer, a 40-min period was thought to represent a typical 
Accepted Manuscript

===== PAGE 9 =====

uninterrupted period of laptop usage without a desk.  Each trial involved participants 
sitting on a stool with no backrest.  Stool height was adjusted prior to the first trial for 
each participant, so that both thighs were horizontal and with a 90° included knee 
angle.  The stool height, laptop placement, and participants’ postures were recorded, 
and the same setup was used in the second trial.  To represent a continuous static 
sitting condition while using a laptop away from a desk (Moffet et al., 2002; Asundi et 
al., 2010; Gold et al., 2012), participants were instructed to complete the tasks while 
maintaining a relaxed kyphotic posture without large ranges of body movement or 
communicating with the experimenters. Participants were still allowed to alleviate any 
postural stresses by switching their posture between the relaxed kyphotic posture and 
a more upright sitting posture. Participants were also allowed to withdraw from the 
study at any time due to discomfort or other reason (though no participants withdrew 
from the study).

These tasks were done in two conditions involving different levels of psychosocial 
stress (low vs. higher).  In the LS condition, participants engaged in relaxed internet 
browsing, which involved performing self-directed “surfing” (e.g., reading news, 
streaming video).  A HS was introduced by asking participants to complete a typing 
task in a situation involving both additional mental workload and time pressure.  
During the typing task, participants duplicated several paragraphs from a typing test 
program (Typing Test TQ, Giletech e.K., Germany).  To induce additional mental 
workload, pairs of two-digit numbers were displayed between each paragraph of text, 
and participants were asked to mentally calculate the product or summation of these 
paired numbers, similar to approaches used by others (Kirschbaum et al., 1992; 
Hughes et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2011).  To add time pressure, participants were 
asked to type as fast as they could, and they were offered a $10 bonus, as an 
incentive, if they completed the typing task at a rate ≥ 115% of their baseline speed. 
This baseline was measured initially during a 1-min typing task completed at an 
individual’s normal typing speed.  The order of LS and HS conditions was 
counterbalanced across participants, and at least four days were provided between 
each trial to minimize carry-over effects (Edwards et al., 1977; Jones et al., 1989).   
 
The experimental procedures are summarized in Figure 3. The experiment started 
with a calibration procedure that included measures of resting and maximal voluntary 
muscle contractions as described earlier (Jia and Nussbaum, 2016). After a brief rest 
period, in which participants were allowed to move and stretch as desired, the FR 
angle of the trunk was measured, then SR of the lumbar extensor muscles were 
collected in a constrained but relaxed upright sitting position as described earlier (Jia

et al., 2015). Participants subsequently completed a 40-min continuous seated task 
under one of the two stress conditions.

At the beginning of each task, participants were also asked to rate their perceived 
discomfort. Upon completing each task, PD scores, SR and FR angles were collected 
again. Prior to the seated exposures, FR responses were collected first, since SR 
exposures were in a seated posture and thus could have impacted FR measures.  
After the seated exposures, SR responses were collected first, to minimize fatigue 
recovery that may have occurred if FR measures were obtained prior to these. During 
the 40-min tasks, EMG, trunk kinematics, and lumbosacral moments and forces were 
measured continuously. Data from the first minute of each 40-min task were 
discarded, since participants often needed this time to obtain a stable posture. 
Further details regarding each measure are described below.

2.3 Trunk Flexion-Relaxation Angle  
Participants were asked to bend forward from an upright standing position to reach 
their end range-of-motion in trunk flexion (Shirado et al., 1995; Callaghan and Dunk, 
2002; Solomonow et al., 2003).  FR angle was then determined by the relative silence 
of lumbar extensor muscle activity, using procedures described earlier (Hendershot et 
al., 2011).  For each trial, post-task FR angle was normalized to the initial value 
collected before the task.   
 
2.4 Muscle Stimulation Responses  
Stimulated responses of the lumbar extensor muscles were measured as an indicator 
of localized muscle fatigue, following the setup and protocols described in Jia et al. 
(2015).  In brief, a dual-channel system – consisting of a stimulator (Grass S88) in 
series with a stimulus isolation unit (SIU5) and a constant current unit (CCU1; all from 
AstroMed, Inc., West Warwick, RI) – was used to electrically stimulate the lumbar 
extensors bilaterally at the L3 level.  After a conditioning train, stimulation responses 
were collected (at 1000 Hz) during three, 16-second stimulation trains at 2 Hz.  Post-
task SR responses were normalized to initial values.   
 
2.5 Electromyography 
Surface EMG signals were collected continuously during the 40-min seated tasks.  
Two eight-channel, telemetered EMG systems (TeleMyo 900, Noraxon, AZ) were 
used to collect surface EMG bilaterally from 12 lumbar muscles, which included three 
lumbar extensors [EL group, ET group, and MF], and three lumbar flexors [IO, EO, 
and RA].  As illustrated in Figure 4, placements of disposable bipolar Ag/AgCl

Raw EMG signals were sampled at 1000 Hz and then amplified, band-pass filtered 
(20 - 400 Hz), and root-mean-square converted with a moving window of 50-
millisecond duration in hardware. Root mean squared EMG signals were 
downsampled to 100 Hz to match the sampling rate for trunk kinematics (see below). 
Median power frequency was determined from the raw EMG of each muscle over 1-
second windows, from the first five (i.e., 2-6 min) and the last five min. of each task; 
MPF was calculated using Fast Fourier Transforms as described in van Dieën et al. 
(1993). Mean values of EMG MPF over these 5-min samples were then obtained, and 
subsequently normalized to initial values. 
 
2.6 Localized Muscle Fatigue 
Localized muscle fatigue was assessed using two methods.  The first method used 
raw EMG obtained during the trials, as described above.  Mean EMG MPF obtained 
from the first five and the last five min of each task were calculated, and changes 
between these mean values were used as a fatigue index in each muscle.  The 
second method used mean stimulation responses, with muscle fatigue indicated as a 
decrease in stimulation responses after vs. before the 40-min tasks (Edwards et al., 
1977; Binder-Macleod and Snyder-Mackler, 1993; Johnson et al., 1995a, Jia et al., 
2015).   
 
2.7 Perceived Discomfort 
Perceived discomfort, for the whole body and eight local body parts (neck, left/right 
shoulders, upper/lower back, buttocks and left/right thighs), was measured using a 
Discomfort Rating Scale (Figure 5).

2.8 Trunk Kinematics 
Trunk angles were also collected continuously during the 40-min trials.  Trunk angles 
were defined as the relative angles between the trunk segment and pelvis, and the 
latter were monitored using two inertial measurement units (IMUs: MVN, Xsens Inc., 
Los Angeles, CA) that were attached to the dorsal aspect of the trunk at the T10 and 
S1 levels (Figure 4).  Both IMUs were aligned with their respective Y axes pointing 
superiorly, and trunk angles were calculated using Xsens software. Since participants 
were required to maintain a generally symmetric posture and movement in the 
sagittal plane was the main focus of this study, off-plane movement was assumed to 
be negligible. IMUs were sampled at 100 Hz and subsequently low-pass filtered at 4 
Accepted Manuscript

===== PAGE 16 =====

Hz (4th order Butterworth; bidirectional).  Mean (SD) values of trunk angles were 
determined from the first five and the last five min. of each task.  
 
2.9 Lumbosacral Joint Moments and Forces  
An EMG-based trunk model (Jia and Nussbaum 2016) was used to estimate 
lumbosacral kinetics (forces and moments).  Both EMG and trunk angles were used 
as inputs to the model, which was calibrated to each individual.  Specific model 
outputs were lumbosacral moments (MLAT, MAXIAL, and MFLEX) and lumbosacral 
reaction forces (FANT, FCOMP, and FLAT).   
 
2.10 Statistical Analyses 
MANOVA (using Wilks’ Lambda) was used to determine the effects of continuous 
sitting (first vs. last five minutes), psychosocial stress, and their interaction on the 
dependent measures as a whole.  Specific dependent measures were triaxial 
lumbosacral forces and moments, FR angle, trunk flexion angle, stimulation response, 
muscle EMG and MPF, and PD.  In the event of a significant MANOVA effect, 
univariate ANOVAs were performed for each dependent variable where relevant.  
Effects were considered significant when p<0.05.  To assess whether there was any 
underlying relationship among perceived discomfort from the eight body segments, 
PCA was performed on the PD scores.  For the same purpose, PCA analysis was 
also performed on EMG MPF from all measured muscles.  The level of 
correspondence between objective measures [trunk flexion angles, compression 
forces, flexion/extension moments, muscle EMG MPF (principal components), and 
stimulation responses] and subjective measures [perceived discomfort (principal 
components)] was evaluated using bivariate coefficients of correlation (ρ).  To assess

the consistency between the two fatigue measurement methods used, ρ values were 
also obtained between EMG median frequencies (principal components) and 
stimulation responses.   
 
3 Results 
MANOVA results indicated that both continuous sitting (p<0.01) and psychosocial 
stress (p=0.04) had significant effects on the set of dependent measures, but there 
was not a significant interaction effect (p=0.90).  Results of univariate ANOVAs (Table 
1) indicated that continuous sitting had significant main effects on a number of 
dependent measures, which included trunk flexion angles, stimulation responses, 
MPF from most muscles, and perceived discomfort in all body regions evaluated.  In 
contrast, psychosocial stress had relatively few significant effects.

4 Discussion 
The objective of this study was to determine the effects of continuous sitting and 
psychosocial stress, in the context of unsupported sitting, on lumbosacral kinematics 
and kinetics, perceived discomfort, and localized muscle fatigue.  Only the main 
effects of continuous sitting and psychosocial stress on selected dependent 
measures were significant, which suggests that the effects of different level of stress 
were consistent across the 40-min sitting periods examined.  It is unclear, though, if 
this lack of an interaction effect will generalize to a larger range of exposure 
conditions or longer sitting durations.   
 
Continuous sitting significantly increased the magnitude of lateral shear forces 
(p<0.01).  As noted earlier, absolute values were analyzed, though these forces 
varied over a wide range (from - 55.17 N to 111.01 N).  The tasks here were 
completed in largely symmetrical postures, for which the major trunk rotations and 
major lumbosacral kinetics were in the sagittal plane.  Previous evidence indicates 
that participants have less effective motor control, especially when muscles are 
fatigued, in the coronal and transverse planes when performing a task mainly in the 
sagittal plane, and which results in substantially different muscle recruitment patterns 
for identical tasks (Parnianpour et al., 1988).  As such, the observed changes in 
predicted lateral shearing forces may have been caused by underlying changes in 
muscle recruitment during continuous sitting.   
 
While the impacts of psychosocial stress were significant for only a limited number of 
the current dependent measures, the higher psychosocial stress condition (HS) did 
increase compression forces by 12%.  Previous work has indicated that increased 
Accepted Manuscript

===== PAGE 25 =====

psychosocial stress could contribute to increased muscle activation, especially during 
conditions involving relatively low physical demands (Marras et al., 2000; Hughes, 
2004; Hughes et al., 2007); such increased muscle activity was found here (Figure 8), 
though the differences were not significant. These increased muscle activations, 
however, likely contributed to the increased predicted compression forces (Cholewicki 
et al., 1995; Granata and Marras, 2000).   
 
There were significant increases in trunk flexion angles at the end of both tasks.  
Participants were asked to maintain a static, kyphosed sitting posture, with minimal 
posture adjustments or in-chair movement.  Both creep and fatigue in the low back 
can develop with static trunk flexion (Kazarian, 1975; Keller et al., 1987; Adams and 
Dolan, 1996; Kanlayanaphotporn et al., 2002), and creep may occur after only five 
minutes of static flexion (Adams and Dolan, 1996; Kanlayanaphotporn et al., 2002).  
FR angle is often used as an indicator of creep deformation in the spinal structure 
under flexion exposures, with results from such studies indicating that continuous 
trunk flexion increases FR angle (Olson et al., 2004; Olson et al., 2006; Shin et al., 
2009).  Here, the increase in trunk flexion angles indicated a potential trunk 
deformation over the 40 min of continuous sitting, which was further supported by the 
increase (albeit not statistically significant) in FR angles.   
 
Localized fatigue was measured using both muscle stimulation and EMG MPF.  
Continuous sitting resulted in significant decreases in stimulated responses and 
declines in EMG MPF for several flexor and extensor muscles at the end of the 40-
min periods in both psychosocial stress conditions. These decreases suggest that 
localized muscle fatigue did occur as a result of completing the continuous, 
Accepted Manuscript

===== PAGE 26 =====

unsupported seated task in a kyphosed sitting posture.  Some of the inconsistencies 
between muscles may be attributed to functional differences between the trunk 
flexors and extensors.  During continuous sitting, trunk extensors are usually 
considered as postural muscles, and which are required to be continuously activated 
to stabilize the sitting posture (van Dieën et al., 2001; Mork and Westgaard, 2005; 
Mork and Westgaard, 2009).  Consistent with this functional muscle role, we 
observed declines in EMG MPF among three trunk extensors (EL, ET, and MF).  The 
observed decrease in EMG MPF in the bilateral IO also supports the role of the IO 
muscle in stabilizing the trunk during sitting, consistent with some existing evidence 
(Snijders et al., 1995).   
 
The correspondence between two distinct fatigue measurement methods was 
determined. Only a weak positive correlation was evident between EMG MPF and 
stimulation responses (Figure 9E), though with indications in both responses of 
localized fatigue developing by the end of the continuous seated tasks.  Consistency 
between these different measures supports that fatigue indeed developed during 
these tasks, though the lack of a strong correspondence between them suggests that 
they may be reflecting different underlying phenomena and that both approaches 
have potential value in assessing muscle fatigue during continuous sitting. 
 
Perceived discomfort also significantly increased at the end of 40 min of continuous 
sitting.  A continuous static sitting posture, such as that imposed by the non-desk 
setup in this study, has been shown to lead to discomfort in the neck, shoulders, and 
back (Grandjean et al., 1983; Stokes et al., 1987; Gay et al., 2006), and which 
increases over time during uninterrupted tasks at work (Marras et al., 1995; Ostensvik 
Accepted Manuscript

===== PAGE 27 =====

et al., 2009). To minimize potential confounding effects of in-chair movement, 
participants here maintained a kyphosed sitting posture, as one example of a sitting 
posture adopted in non-desk conditions, and which can increase intradiscal pressures 
(Wilcox and Keselman, 2003), stretch the posterior spine ligaments (Owen et al., 
2010), and restrict nutrient supplies to nerves (Hallal et al., 2012). These factors may 
have contributed to the observed increases in discomfort.   
 
While subjective measures (e.g., perceived discomfort) provided direct assessment of 
sitting discomfort, objective measures can potentially provide unbiased estimations 
that are related to sitting discomfort (Lee et al., 1993; Kyung et al., 2008).  Support for 
objective measures as useful additions to subjective measurements of sitting 
discomfort would be provided if a good correlation existed between these two types of 
measures.  Several studies have been completed to identify the relationship between 
subjective measures (e.g., perceived discomfort) and a range of objective measures 
including trunk posture, EMG, and interface pressure (Eklund and Corlett, 1987; Lee 
et al., 1993; Salewytsch and Callaghan, 1999; Vergara and Page, 2000).  Here, the 
first principal component of PD scores was used as a representation of overall 
perceived discomfort across all measured body parts and the whole body, and 
correlations were obtained between PPC1 and several objective measures (trunk 
flexion angle, muscle EMG MPF and stimulation responses, Figure 9).  Although no 
statistically significant (p>0.23) correlations were found between subjective and 
objective measures, trunk flexion angle was positively associated with perceived 
discomfort, specifically with higher trunk flexion angles corresponding with higher 
perceived discomfort, a relationship consistent with previous evidence (Eklund and 
Corlett, 1987).  Declines in both fatigue measures (EPC1 and stimulation response) 
Accepted Manuscript

===== PAGE 28 =====

were also found to be associated with an increase in perceived discomfort.  This 
association suggests that participants may have perceived higher discomfort, at least 
in part, as a result of localized muscle fatigue, as has been described earlier 
(Rohmert and Luczak, 1978; Zhang et al., 1996).   
 
Four potential limitations of this work need to be acknowledged.  First, the effects of 
continuous sitting and psychosocial stress were evaluated under a constrained sitting 
condition.  Each experimental task was completed with participants sitting in a single, 
fixed posture, and with minimal in-chair moments.  In realistic sitting situations, 
participants are free to make postural adjustments or take microbreaks to reduce 
body discomfort (Salewytsch and Callaghan, 1999; McLean et al., 2001).  Therefore, 
the results of the current study may have limited ability to generalize to the broader 
use of a laptop or other mobile device in various unsupported sitting conditions.  
Future work is needed to test the effects of continuous sitting and psychosocial stress 
under more complex task conditions that include a wider range of trunk movements 
and sitting postures.  Second, the duration of the continuous task was determined 
based on the results from our pilot studies, where muscle fatigue was observed after 
30 min of quiet sitting.  There is a lack of evidence, to our knowledge, regarding 
typical exposure durations for laptop use without a desk, so the applicability of the 
current work, in terms of the duration of actual use, is unknown.  In addition, 40 min of 
continuous sitting may not be of sufficient duration to detect potential time-dependent 
changes in passive trunk components (via viscoelastic changes).  Additional study is 
needed to identify the common exposure durations of sitting under different 
conditions and a longer duration could be also expected to detect the time-dependent 
changes associated with passive and active trunk components.  Third, only two 
Accepted Manuscript

===== PAGE 29 =====

psychosocial stress levels were evaluated in this study, which may not reflect the 
spectrum of psychosocial stress situations.  Including more stress conditions may 
help to better identify the effects of psychosocial stress over time. Fourth, while 
significant increases in whole-body and localized discomfort were found as a result of 
the 40-min. seated exposures, the final values were relatively low overall (Figure 7) 
and it is unclear if these values represent practically-meaningful level, and the lack or 
more substantial discomfort may have precluded identifying stronger correlations 
between discomfort and objective measures (Figure 9). 
 
In summary, unsupported continuous sitting was found to have important impacts on 
lumbosacral kinematics, and to lead to the development of muscle fatigue and 
perceived discomfort.  The current findings suggest that sitting for moderate 
continuous periods (i.e., 40 min.) can lead to higher body discomfort, internal spinal 
loads, and localized fatigue, which could further increase risks of developing LBP.  
Though more limited effects of psychosocial stress were found, the higher-level 
stress imposed here did lead to an increase in lumbosacral compression.  These 
outcomes were evident after 40 min of sitting under experimental conditions involving 
laptop use without a desk, which could be of particular concern for some conditions of 
mobile device use (e.g., in an airport).  This study highlighted the potential influence 
of sitting-related moderating factors on individual behaviors and provided evidence to 
facilitate a better understanding of sitting-related risks.  Further research is still 
needed, however, to assess the effects of continuous sitting and psychosocial stress 
in more complex occupational and non-occupational sitting conditions through more 
detailed analysis, such as estimation of time-sensitive lumbosacral loads using 
advanced spine model.
