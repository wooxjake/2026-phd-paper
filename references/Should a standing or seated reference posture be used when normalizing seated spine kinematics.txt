Should a standing or seated reference posture be used
when normalizing seated spine kinematics?

abstract
Currently in the literature there is no consensus on which procedure for normalizing seated spine
kinematics is most effective. The objective of this study was to examine the changes in the range of
motion (ROM) of seated posture trials when expressed as a percent of maximum standing and seated
ROM. A secondary purpose was to determine whether the typical maximum planar calibration
movements ( ﬂexion, lateral-bend, and axial twist) elicited the respective maximum ROM values for
each spine region versus postures with speci ﬁc movement instruction. Thirteen male participants
completed seven different movement trials. These consisted of the maximum planar movement trials,
with the remaining four postures being combinations of speci ﬁc lumbar and thoracic movements. Global
and relative angles for the upper-thoracic, mid-thoracic, lower-thoracic, and lumbar regions were
calculated and normalized to both a seated and standing reference posture. When normalizing both
global and relative angles the standing reference appears optimal for ﬂexion, twisting and lateral bend
angles in all spine regions, with the exception of relative ﬂexion angle in the mid-thoracic region. The
maximum planar movement trials captured the greatest ROM for each global angle, relative lower-
thoracic angle and relative lumbar ﬂexion angle, but did not for all other relative angles in the upper-
thoracic, mid-thoracic, and lumbar regions. If future researchers can only collect one reference posture
these results recommend that a standing reference posture be collected for normalizing seated spine
kinematics, although a seated reference posture should be collected if examining relative ﬂexion angles
at the mid-thoracic region.

1. Introduction
A recent study by Bauman et al. (201 1) found that the typical
Canadian spends on average ﬁve hours seated every weekday. With
the average individual spending a large portion of their workday
seated, there has been an increase in research examining the effects
of sitting on spinal postures. The literature suggests the occurrence,
or reoccurrence, of low back pain can be provoked through sitting
(Sjolie, 2004 ), with occupations requiring seated work for greater
than 95% of the total shift being associated with low back pain
(Hoogendoorn et al., 2000 ). As more studies quantify the spine's
movement and postures while seated, such as Claus et al. (2009) ,i t
is important to consider individual differences when examining and
interpreting the results, and the use of a suitable normalization
procedure (i.e. as a percent of maximum range of motion (ROM)). It
has been suggested that postures and movements be expressed in
terms of maximum range in order to make comparison between
individuals and between groups ( Edmondston et al., 2007b ). To
date, few studies have normalized lateral bend or axial twist
postures in either the thoracic or lumbar spine. Furthermore, seated
lumbar spine ROM during ﬂexion is typically normalized to a
maximum standing reference posture ( Callaghan and Dunk, 2002;
Callaghan and McGill, 2001; Dunk and Callaghan, 2010 ), and it
remains unclear whether this is appropriate or if a maximum
seated reference posture should be used (i.e. the maximum angles
achieved during sitting and standing differ).
While seated lumbar spine postures are often normalized using
maximum standing ﬂexion, there is no consensus on the most
effective way to report seated thoracic spine kinematic data.
Typically, works investigating seated thoracic spine motion have
reported their ﬁndings as angles in degrees ( Edmondston et al.,
2007a; Gilleard et al., 2002 ), with few studies reporting normal-
ized thoracic angles. Strahan et al. (201 1)examined the kinematics
of certain rowing techniques by quantifying the lower thoracic and
lumbar position during the rowing stroke, for all three axes, as the
percent of the seated rowing positions' ROM. Recently Nairn et al.
(2013), investigated the thoracic spine during upright sitting,
slumped sitting, and seated maximum ﬂexion when normalized

During analyses of seated spine kinematics it is important to

this study was to examine the changes in the maximum ROM of
seated posture trials when expressed as a percent of maximum
standing (%STAND) or a percent of maximum sitting (%SIT) for
ﬂexion, lateral bend, and axial twist motion. A secondary purpose
was to determine whether the maximum ROM was occurring in
the intended maximum planar movement trial as opposed to one
of the four seated movement trials.
2. Methods
2.1. Data collection
The study consisted of 13 right-hand dominant male participants with no
history of neck, back and shoulder pain for at least one year prior to collection. The
mean 7SEM age, height, and weight for the study was 23.6 70.6 years,
1.8270.02 m, and 84.3 73.9 kg, respectively. Informed consent was obtained for
each participant prior to data collection and the institution's Of ﬁce of Research
Ethics approved all protocols prior to the study's commencement.
Kinematic data were collected at 50 Hz using a seven camera three-
dimensional motion capture system (Vicon MX, Vicon Systems Ltd., Oxford, UK)
to track the position of 31 passive-re ﬂective markers. As shown in Fig. 1, 24 of the
reﬂective markers were placed on eight rigid plates (three markers per plate),
which were attached to the skin over speci ﬁc vertebrae (T
1,T 4,T 5,T 8,T 9,T 12,L 1, and
the posterior– superior iliac spines (PSISs)) as per Nairn et al. (2013) . The remaining
markers were placed over C7, greater trochanters, and the iliac crests and anterior –
superior iliac spines of the pelvis.
Participants were asked to perform seven different movements which were
performed unilaterally to the right where appropriate (i.e. axial twist and lateral bend).
Three of these movements were maximum planar movement trials: maximumﬂexion
(MaxFlex), maximum axial twist (MaxTwist), and maximum lateral bend (MaxBend),
which were completed both while sitting (-Sit) and standing (-Stand). The remaining
four movements were performed while seated only and used as the comparison
postures with focus on thoracic twist orﬂexion, with the lumbar spine being in a neutral
or at a mid-range ﬂexion posture. These combinations included: thoracic twist with a
neutral lumbar (Twist – Neutral), thoracic twist with a ﬂexed lumbar (Twist – Flex),
thoracic ﬂexion with a ﬂexed lumbar (Flex– Flex), and thoracic ﬂexion with a neutral
lumbar (Flex– Neutral). Participants were asked to begin in an upright posture, move to
the speciﬁed end posture in approximately four seconds, hold the posture for ﬁve
seconds (hold-phase), then to return to their starting position in another four seconds.
The timing was assisted by both a metronome and researcher cues. For the maximum
planar movements, each participant was told to ﬂex/bend/twist as much as possible
without speciﬁc instruction on how to move the different spine regions. In addition to
the overall trunk movement, speci ﬁct oe a c hp o s t u r e ,s t a n d i n gﬂexion required the
knees to remain straight; all lateral bend trials had the ear bent to the shoulder; and for
the twist trials the head/neck was rotated so the participant was looking over their right
shoulder. The speciﬁc head movement instructions were given to facilitate the full spinal
range of motion and were similar to those used by Willems et al. (1996).D u r i n gt h e
sitting trials, the chair height was adjusted so the knee and hip angles were at
approximately 901. Each movement was repeated for three trials consecutively and each
block of three was presented in a random order.
2.2. Data processing
The data were ﬁltered using a fourth-order dual-pass, low pass Butterworth
ﬁlter, with a cut off frequency of 2.5 Hz, as determined through a residual analysis
(Winter, 2009). The spine was partitioned into four regions based on the location of
the rigid plates: upper-thoracic (T
1– T4), mid-thoracic (T5– T8), lower-thoracic (T9– T12),
and lumbar (L1-PSIS) ( Nairn et al., 2013 ) as shown in Fig. 1. Angles for each region
were expressed as both relative and global angles, with a regional relative angle
being de ﬁned as the movement of a segment relative to the one below it, with the
lumbar segment being relative to the pelvis. Global regional angles were taken as
the particular segment with respect to the laboratory space. The three-dimensional
rotation angles were calculated using an X– Y– Z (Flexion– Lateral Bend– Axial Twist)
Cardan sequence. Given the mathematical nature of the analysis it is possible slight
variations in results may have arisen based on the rotation sequence used.
However, this order has been used previously during three-dimensional, multi-
segmented trunk analyses of seated motion and follows orthopedic convention
(Preuss and Popovic, 2010 ), and thus was deemed acceptable.
For each movement, the maximum ROM of each region was taken as the absolute
difference between the maximum angle during the hold-phase of the posture and the
participants' upright starting position. The upright starting position was determined
for each participant and each region by averaging theﬁrst one second of data from all
seated and standing trials separately, as trials began seconds before participants
commenced their speciﬁed movements. For each participant a single starting position
for sitting and one for standing was obtained for each of the four regions, in each of
the three planes of movements. This resulted in 01 (0%Max) at each spine region being
equivalent to upright. From each plane, the maximum angle of each region from any of
the three repeats of the maximum planar movement trials (MaxFlex, MaxTwist,
MaxBend) was recorded for each of the four regions, and this was done for both the
maximum sitting and maximum standing trials separately. These values were then
used for normalizing the regional angles from the speciﬁc seated posture movements
to a percent of maximum ROM both while sitting (%SIT) and standing (%STAND).
2.3. Statistical analysis
The normalized ROM of each region (global and relative angles) for ﬂexion,
axial twist and lateral bend, were analyzed separately using 2 /C2 6 repeated
measures ANOVAs, with the two factors being: reference posture (%SIT or %STAND)
and Movement trial (Flex – Neutral, Flex – Flex, Twist – Neutral, Twist – Flex, Max – Sit,
and Max – Stand). For each plane, all of the comparison movements as well as the
speciﬁc seated and standing maximum planar movement trials for that plane were
analyzed. For all analyses, α¼0.05 and all signi ﬁcant omnibus F-tests were further
analyzed pairwise, and the p-value was adjusted using a Bonferroni correction. All
statistical analyses were completed using a statistical software package (IBM SPSS
Statistics, SPSS Inc., v.20.0, Chicago, IL).

The reliability of the maximum planar trials was assessed with

variable (relative upper-thoracic sitting lateral bend, r¼0.645).

while either a standing or sitting reference posture is adequate for
global lateral bend angles. However, the decision on whether a
standing or sitting reference posture should be used for normalizing
relative angles must be approached for each level of the spine and
each direction individually. Flexion was the axis where the choice of
reference posture varied the most between regions. Again brieﬂy, for
the lumbar, upper-thoracicand lower-thoracicregions, relativeﬂexion
angles should be normalized to a standing reference posture, as
angles as a %SIT were always larger or no different than angles as
%STAND (Fig. 2a, c, d). The mid-thoracic was the only region where
relative angles as %SIT were always smaller than the same angle as
%STAND, suggesting a larger ROM while sitting ( Fig. 2 b). Relative
lateral bend and twisting angles for all regions can be normalized as
a percent of maximum standing, as the MaxBend trials expressed as
%SIT were always larger or no diff erent in comparison to %STAND.
Ideally both reference postures would be collected, regardless of
plane or region, with the largest angle from either reference posture
being used for normalizing. If it is not feasible to collect both
reference postures, due to time constraints or special population
considerations, it appears acceptable to normalize relative three-
dimensional spine kinematics to a standing reference posture,
however a seated reference posture is recommended when examin-
ing relative ﬂexion at the mid-thoracicregion.
When normalizing kinematic data it is crucial to determine if
the maximum movement trials are resulting in the largest angle.
Across the four spine regions, the maximum movement standing
trials produced global ﬂexion, lateral bend, and twisting angles
signiﬁcantly greater than or equal to the maximum angle achieved
during the sitting maximum movement trials ( Table 1). While the
maximum planar movement trials resulted in reference postures
with the absolute largest angle when examining global angles, this
was not the case when examining relative angles. With regards to
the lumbar region, the MaxFlex-Stand trial resulted in greater
relative ﬂexion angles than all other trials ( Fig. 2 d); whereas in
twisting and lateral bend, the comparison trials which called for
twisting resulted in relative angles similar to those seen in both
MaxBend and MaxTwist trials. For the lower-thoracic region,
MaxFlex-Stand resulted in relative percentages greater than all
other trials, while no difference was found between both MaxTw-
ist, and MaxBend trials done while sitting compared to standing,
although they were found to be signi
ﬁcantly greater than all other
movements. Examining the upper-thoracic and mid-thoracic
regions, the maximum movement trials did not produce a ROM
that was found to be signi ﬁcantly greater than all comparison
movements. As comparison trials were able to elicit a greater
magnitude of motion than the standard, or traditional, maximum
planar movements, future research should investigate which
sitting and standing postures result in the greatest absolute
ROM, looking at each segment and plane of motion individually.
As individual differences are present when examining the entire
spine, as well as each spine region's ROM, it may be necessary to
have participants complete a variety of movements attempting to
capture their true maximum, with the trial resulting in largest
ROM for each segment, being used for normalizing.

The additional external support provided by a chair while
sitting, and not just the position the spine adopts while seated,
could partially explain the results of this study. While it was not
observed during collection, to explain the lumbar twist data it was
speculated that some participants used the chair and their feet as
leverage when they attempted to maximally rotate. This method of
producing “anchored” motion may have resulted in the observed
twisting of the lumbar region in the opposite direction to the
intended movement and could be responsible for the large lateral
bend angles that occurred during twisting trials, as found in the
Twist– Neutral and Twist – Flex trials. Of the thirteen participants,
three participants may have used this anchoring technique during
the comparison trials involving twist (Twist – Flex, Twist – Neutral),
and one during the MaxTwist-Sit trials (produced global and
relative twist angles to the left, noted during data analysis).
Regardless, the magnitude of the movement for these participants
was within the range of the other ten participants whose spines
rotated in the intended direction of the movement. As a result of
this, the absolute maximum angle achieved during the movement
was used as the maximum angle, regardless of direction. The
participant's global angle range over the three MaxTwist-Sit trials
averaged to /C24 5.341, showing how little motion actually occurs at
the lumbar region during seated twisting. The chair also seemed to
affect relative ﬂexion of the upper-thoracic angle, as the Flex –
Neutral and Flex – Flex trials resulted in percentages greater than
175%STAND, similar to the ﬁnding of
Nairn et al. (2013) . This could
be a result of the dif ﬁculty in maintaining balance when attempt-
ing to fully ﬂex both the lumbar and thoracic spine at the same
time while standing, which is less evident while sitting due to the
support of the chair. As a result the overall largest movement from
each region was taken as the ROM, in order to counteract any
inﬂuence the chair may have had on the direction of the motion.
Similar to any study requiring motion capture, limitations were
present in the procedure. The use of surface markers in motion
analysis is a common concern as skin motion artefact can occur. As
such, care was taken to ensure the rigid plates were adhered as best
as possible and were constantly monitored for any gross-movement
artefacts. Although our spine marker setup has been previously
reported in the literature (Nairn et al., 2013)a n dw a sa d a p t e df r o m
Preuss and Popovic (2010), it is possible that factors such as muscle
contractions and marker conﬁguration could have had an effect on
the recorded movement. Sex has been identiﬁed as a factor that can
affect an individual's spine ROM (Sullivan et al., 1994), however these
differences may be accounted for through the use of the proper
normalization technique. Based on the work by Van Herp et al.
(2000) and Edmondston et al. (2007a) ,i ti se x p e c t e dt h a tt h e

conﬁned to the maximum planar trials as observed for the male

required marker placement con ﬁguration and the required female
attire, only a male population could be used in this study. Also, it is
possible that other variations of combined movement patterns not
included in this study could produce a larger maximum than
presented; and it is possible tha t an external device such as a bar
could help facilitate the motion ( Johnson et al., 2012 ). Considering
this study was the ﬁrst attempt to investigate the difference in using
seated or standing normalizatio n postures on seated movement
trials, the aforementioned limitations were considered acceptable;
and future work will be able to expand on the current ﬁndings.
5. Conclusion
This study illustrated the need to consider both the reference
posture and type of trial used when normalizing seated spine
kinematics across multiple levels of the spine. When investigating
global angles in any direction it appears adequate to normalize
angles as a percent of maximum ROM while standing. Regarding
relative angles, a standing reference posture appears acceptable
for normalizing spine kinematics, with the exception of the mid-
thoracic region. Examining the different movement trials, global
angles across all spine regions and planes indicated that the
standard maximum planar movements consistently resulted in
the largest global rotations compared to the comparison trials.
Conversely, the standard maximum planar movement trials only
resulted in the largest relative angle for the lower-thoracic angles
and relative ﬂexion in the lumbar spine. These ﬁndings highlighted
the importance of considering not only the region of the spine, but
how the data will be presented (as a global or relative angle),
when normalizing seated spine kinematics. In conclusion, the
ideal normalization procedure recommended for seated spine
kinematics is to collect both seated and standing reference
postures, and use the overall maximum angle attained for each
spine segment. If it is only possible to record and/or have
participants perform one reference posture, a standing reference
posture would suf ﬁ
ce, but caution should be exercised when
interpreting the relative mid-thoracic ﬂexion angles.
