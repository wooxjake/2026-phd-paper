ABSTRACT 
Prolonged sitting may involve several mechanisms that make it a risk factor for low back pain. 
The aim of this study was to investigate lumbar-pelvic kinematics and multifidus muscle (MF) 
activity during squatting and forward bending in relation to the sedentary behaviour of physic -
ally active students. Sixty-three students were divided into two groups according to the time 
spent in a sitting position during the day: ‘high’ (>7 h/day); ‘low’ (�7 h/day). Lumbar-pelvic 
ratios, ranges of motion, angular velocities, and MF flexion-relaxation phenomenon were investi -
gated. Data were obtained using the optical motion analysis system, and surface electromyog -
raphy. The results indicated that lumbar-pelvic ratios during both tasks and velocity of lumbar 
spine during squatting were significantly greater in the ‘high’ than in the ‘low’ sitting group. 
Muscle activity showed no differences between groups. Prolonged sitting can be considered a 
factor that slightly, but statistically significantly influences the lumbar-pelvic kinematics in phys -
ically active people.  
Practitioner summary: Lumbar-pelvic kinematics can be altered by prolonged sitting in physic -
ally active students. Lumbar-pelvic ratios during squatting and forward bending and lumbar 
spine velocity during squatting were significantly greater in the ‘high’ than in the ‘low’ sitting 
group. Sedentary behaviour should be considered during an assessment of movement patterns.

1. Introduction 
Sedentary behaviour and physical activity (PA) are con -
sidered as determinant factors of health and life span 
(Stamatakis and Gill 2019; Chau et al. 2013). Sitting 
time can elevate cardiovascular disease risk even in 
those who are highly physically active, and PA does 
not eliminate the increased risk of death associated 
with high sitting time (Ekelund et al. 2016; Stamatakis 
Emmanuel et al. 2019). Sedentary behaviour is also 
related to low back pain (LBP) (Hanna et al. 2019). 
Previous research on sitting posture and muscle activa -
tion suggests that habitually sustaining flexed sitting 
postures for long periods of time may result in the 
deconditioning of spine muscles and lead to a 
greater risk of back injury or a provocation of existing 
LBP (Bontrup et al. 2019; Dankaerts et al. 2006; 
P. O’Sullivan et al. 2006; P. B. O’Sullivan et al. 2006). 
Sedentary behaviour is defined as any waking 
behaviour that is characterised by an energy expend -
iture of 1.5 metabolic equivalent of tasks (METs), while 
in a sitting, reclining, or lying posture (Gardner et al. 
2016). Self-report sitting behaviours were previously 
defined in the literature as sedentary when the total 
time of sitting (transport, work leisure activity) during a 
day exceeded 7 h (Heneghan et al. 2018; van Uffelen 
et al. 2013; Park et al. 2018). Sufficient PA, according to 
the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), 
is characterised by 5 or more days of any combination 
of walking, moderate intensity or vigorous intensity 
achieving a minimum total PA of at least 600 MET 
minutes a week (Biernat 2013).

The increased time spent in sitting is a global prob -
lem and office work is a major contributor to sedentary 
behaviour (Kazi et al. 2019). However, long sitting is 
also particularly characteristic of young people continu -
ing their education (Matusiak-Wieczorek et al. 2020). 
Students are one of the most sedative groups in 
European populations (Loyen et al. 2016; Jelsma et al. 
2019). With interest in a healthy life style growing 
among young adults, more and more of them meet 
the PA recommendation (Matusiak-Wieczorek et al. 
2020). Therefore, high PA does not exclude sitting 
behaviours, which are associated not only with educa -
tion but also with leisure activities like watching televi -
sion, playing computer games or reading. At the same 
time one person can be considered physically active 
and spend a lot of time sitting. It seems unclear how 
long sitting times can influence persons who meet PA 
guidelines. Higher amounts of daily total sitting time 
have been shown to be associated with greater risk of 
all-cause mortality and moderate or vigorous physical 
activity appears to attenuate this hazardous association 
(Chau et al. 2013). 
During trunk flexion postures the lumbodorsal fascia 
and spinal ligaments provide necessary support for the 
extensor muscles, reducing the necessity of contraction 
to maintain the fully flexed posture. Observation of 
electrical silence of the back extensors muscles at full 
trunk flexion is defined as flexion-relaxation phenom -
enon and calculated as flexion-relaxation ratio and/or 
extension-relaxation ratio (McGorry and Lin 2012; Laird 
et al. 2019). During flexed postures the transition of 
load moves towards other active and/or passive struc -
tures close to the end range of flexion (P. O’Sullivan 
et al. 2006). A seated posture can be affected by the 
parameters of a chair, but in general a sitting posture 
causes the pelvis to rotate backward, reduce lumbar 
lordosis and increase intervertebral disc pressure 
(Harrison et al. 1999). Long-duration sitting can be a 
contributory factor in hamstring tightness (Waqas et al. 
2016; Fatima et al. 2017). Reduced hamstrings flexibility 
has been reported as a factor altering lumbar-pelvic 
mobility during functional tasks like forward bending or 
reaching tasks (Gajdosik, Hatcher, and Whitsell 1992; 
Zawadka et al. 2018). Previously lumbar-pelvic motion 
was wildly examined to demonstrate the relationship 
between LBP and lumbar-hip coordination during 
many activities (Larivi �ere, Gagnon, and Loisel 2000; 
Esola et al. 1996; Shojaei, Salt, et al. 2017). One of the 
parameters described in the literature is the lumbar- 
pelvic ratio defined as the ratio of total lumbar motion 
to pelvis motion in the sagittal plane (Zawadka et al. 
2018; Kasahara et al. 2008). Lumbar-pelvic motion has 
been shown to be connected to LBP by alternations in 
lumbar-hip coordination, slower task pace, decrease 
in lumbar contribution in overall movement and a 
compensatory increase in mobility of other spine seg -
ments and pelvis (Larivi �ere, Gagnon, and Loisel 2000; 
Esola et al. 1996; Shojaei, Salt, et al. 2017). Changes in 
the trunk flexion-extension movement pattern may be 
the result of a neuromuscular adaptation to minimise 
deformation in the lower back passive tissues and 
avoid pain (Shojaei, Salt, et al. 2017). However, a cause- 
and-effect relationship has not been well established so 
far. Due to the high functional character of movement, 
the biomechanics of the spine and pelvis during squat -
ting and forward bending were often studied (Zawadka 
et al. 2020; Shojaei, Vazirian, et al. 2017). However, the 
relationship between sitting behaviour and lumbar 
kinematics in standing among students is not well 
documented in the literature. 
Due to the fact that prolonged sitting involve sev -
eral mechanisms that make it a risk factor for LBP (Park 
et al. 2018) and that LBP is clearly related to biomech -
anical alternations during trunk flexion-extension tasks, 
we decided to investigate the linkage between sitting 
time and lumbar-pelvic kinematics. Understanding lum -
bar-pelvic kinematics in an asymptomatic group of 
long-sitting young people could provide useful infor -
mation about the missing link between sitting behav -
iour and biomechanical changes during dynamic 
activities observed in LBP patients. 
Until recently biomechanical alternations of dynamic 
activities as a consequence of prolonged sitting posture 
have not been widely investigated. Understanding the 
influence of prolonged sitting on lumbar-pelvic coordin -
ation during trunk flexion in dynamic tasks has import -
ant clinical implications in assessing and preventing LBP 
among students. Therefore, the hypothesis was that an 
increased time of sitting would alter lumbar-pelvic kine -
matics and muscle activity during squatting and forward 
bending compared to less sitting time. The aim of this 
study was to investigate lumbar-pelvic kinematics and 
multifidus muscle (MF) activity during squatting and for -
ward bending in relation to the sedentary behaviour of 
physically active university students.

they had any musculoskeletal pain or an orthopaedic

2.2. Procedures and measurements 
Two tasks were investigated in this study: forward 
bending and squatting. Participants performed five tri -
als of each task without additional load. They were not 
trained for these tasks prior to the investigation. Before 
recording the trials participants were instructed in how 
to do the tasks, and the execution of tasks was demon -
strated to them. The initial position of both tasks was 
the same: standing looking straight ahead with arms 
forward, parallel to the floor and with feet at shoulder’s 
width apart. Forward bending was executed to the 
maximal depth with straightened knees, and squatting 
was executed to the maximal depth maintaining heel 
contact throughout the task. The pace of movement 
was self-selected; however, participants were instructed 
not to do any unpredictable or sudden movements. 
Three seconds after reaching the final position of for -
ward bending/squatting, participants were asked to 
return to their initial positions at a similar pace. An 
average of three middle trials (without the first and the 
last) was statistically analysed. 
Kinematic data were obtained using the VICON 
motion analysis system (Oxford Metrics Ltd, Oxford, UK) 
with 8 infra-red cameras operating at a sampling rate of 
100 Hz. A set of reflective markers was placed on spe -
cific anatomical landmarks. Markers have been attached 
to the following locations to build the spine and pelvis 
model: tenth and twelfth thoracic spinous process (Th 
10, Th 12), posterior and anterior superior iliac spine 
and second and third sacral spinous process (S2, S3). 
The system registered three-dimensional trajectories of 
passive markers. Markers trajectories were filtered and 
segments of the lumbar spine and pelvis were modelled 
using software (Vicon, Nexus; Oxford Metrics).

The pelvis angle was defined as the angle of the 
sacral bone relative to the vertical line, and the lumbar 
spine angle was defined as the angle of the thoracic 
spine relative to the sacral bone. The angles were 
computed in the sagittal plane (Figure 1). The range 
of motion (ROM) was defined as the difference 
between the maximal and minimal angle obtained by 
each segment in the sagittal plane during a task. The 
lumbar-pelvic ratio describes the coordination during 
spine flexion-extension tasks (also called the lumbar- 
pelvic rhythm) (S. Shin and Yoo 2020). In this study 
ratios were calculated by dividing the total lumbar 
ROM by the pelvis ROM. Angular velocity of segments 
was measured as an angular displacement of seg -
ments (spine, pelvis) in sagittal plane over the change 
in time. Average and peak velocities were analysed 
during two phases of movement: descent and ascent. 
Surface electromyography (sEMG) integrated with 
the motion capture system (wireless EMG; Myon) was 
used to record superficial lumbar multifidus muscle 
activity at the sampling rate of 1000 Hz. Electrodes 
were placed on the back along the axes of the muscle 
fibres, according to the SENIAM protocol: on and 
aligned with a line from caudal tip of posterior super -
ior iliac spine to the interspace between L1 and L2 
interspace at the level of L5 spinous process (i.e. about 
2–3 cm from the midline) (‘Sensor Locations’ 2021). 
Two pairs of self-adhesive disposable Ag/AgCl disc sur -
face electrodes (SORIMEX, Toru �n, Poland) with an elec -
trical contact surface of 1 cm
2 
were placed bilaterally, 
with a 2 cm interelectrode distance. The subjects’ hair 
was shaved when it was necessary and the skin was 
cleaned with alcohol before the sEMG electrode 
was affixed. 
The EMG signals collected during the movements 
were band-pass filtered at 10–450 Hz using a fourth- 
order Butterworth filter with zero lag and then rectified. 
EMG data were smoothed using a 100 millisecond mov -
ing average window. Based on the methodology of pre -
vious studies, the peak amplitude of EMG was 
determined within the ascent and descent phases, and 
the mean for a 200 ms window about the peak was 
taken as the sEMG amplitude value (McGorry and Lin 
2012; Neblett et al. 2003). A flexion-relaxation ratio (FRR) 
and extension-relaxation ratio (ERR) of the activity dur -
ing the forward bending and full squat was calculated 
to determinate the presence of the flexion-relaxation 
phenomenon (FRP) and muscle silence (McGorry and 
Lin 2012). For this purpose, we have divided tasks into 
the descent phase, the static position phase and the 
ascent phase. The value for the FRR was calculated by 
dividing the mean of a 200 ms window about the peak

2.3. Statistics 
Statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistica 
software (ver. 13.1 TIBCO Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). 
Continuous data were described in tables by mean (M), 
median (Me) and standard deviation (SD). Box and whis -
ker plots show median, upper and lower quartiles and 
maximum and minimum vales. The level of statistical 
significance was set at p ¼ 0.05. Normal distribution of 
data was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the 
nonparametric statistical method was used for kinematic 
and EMG analyses. The Chi-square test was used for 
comparing the number of females and males in groups 
and two-way ANOVA with partial eta squared (g
p
2
) was 
used for comparing group characteristics (Tables 1 and 
2). In order to compare lumbar-pelvic kinematics 
between ‘low’ and ‘high’ sitting groups and males and 
females we used the Mann–Whitney U-test. The intra- 
class correlation coefficients (ICC) show good to excel -
lent reliability of the kinematic data (ICC ¼ 0.90–0.97 for 
squatting and ICC ¼ 0.78–0.97 for forward bending). The 
Glass rank-biserial correlation was used to calculate 
effect sizes (ES) of statistically significant comparisons, 
and they were interpreted as small (0.2), medium (0.5), 
and large (0.8) effect sizes (Tomczak and Tomczak 2014; 
Fritz, Morris, and Richler 2012).

4. Discussion 
The aim of this study was to investigate lumbar-pelvic 
kinematics and MF activity during squatting and for -
ward bending in relation to the sedentary behaviour 
of physically active young adults. The results of this 
study are generally in agreement with the stated 
hypothesis. Increased time in sitting significantly alters 
lumbar-pelvic kinematics during squatting and forward 
bending compared to less siting time. This conclusion 
can be applied to people who meet PA guidelines. 
4.1. Lumbar-pelvic ratios and ROMs 
Greater values of the lumbar-pelvic ratio in the ‘high’ 
sitting group indicates that the domination of lumbar 
spine motion over pelvic motion during tasks is 
increased as a result of prolonged sitting. Previous 
research links greater lumbar-pelvic or lumbar-hip 
ratios with sex and age. The lumbar contribution to 
movement has been identified as smaller in females 
than in males and smaller in older than in younger 
participants (Vazirian et al. 2017; Pries et al. 2015). 
However, in this study groups were balanced with 
respect to sex and age. Patients suffering from LBP 
also have been reported to have decreased lumbar 
motion in forward bending tasks (Larivi �ere, Gagnon, 
and Loisel 2000). On the other hand, Jandre Reis and 
Macedo (2015) have shown that lumbar mobility was 
greater while at the same time pelvic mobility was 
restricted in LBP patients comparing with controls. 
Lumbar-pelvic movement patterns in LBP patients still 
remain unclear. It may be that people with LBP should 
be classified into subgroups by the pain provoked by 
movement (flexion/extension), As was suggested by 
Kim et al. (2013) and Laird et al. (2014) lower move -
ment speed is commonly seen in people with LBP and 
has been linked to a fear of movement (Thomas 
et al. 2008). 
We consider two possible explanations of our cur -
rent results. First, thigh hamstrings can restrict pelvis 
anterior rotation and lead to compensation resulting in 
increased lumbar flexion. Second, deconditioning of the 
lumbar muscles and excessive loading of passive tissue 
in a sitting position can lead to changes in the trunk 
movement pattern. Previous studies of Hasebe et al. 
(2014) suggest that subjects with tight hamstrings have 
decreased pelvic mobility. A compensatory increase in 
the lumbar ROM during the forward bending move -
ment can be the result of a slightly, but not statistically 
significant decreased pelvic mobility in the ‘high’ sitting 
group. In clinical practice, limitation of hamstring flexi -
bility and greater lumbar spine ROM should be an indi -
cative of low back stabilisation exercises and hamstring

stretching of hamstrings significantly improves maximal 
forward bending and pelvic mobility, which decreased 
the lumbar-pelvic ratio (Hasebe et al. 2016). Long-dur -

tightness (Waqas et al. 2016; Fatima et al. 2017). 
On the other hand, continuous sitting also resulted in 
higher trunk flexion angles (Jia and Nussbaum 2018). 
Sustained trunk flexion during sitting can lead to creep 
of back viscoelastic tissues which can increase the risk 
of LBP (Little and Khalsa 2005). Creep of lower back 
extensor muscles may occur due to prolonged passive 
stretching in a sitting position. That may lead to a 
reduced contribution of passive tissue in maintaining 
spinal stability and posture. Thus, as a result of com -
pensation, lower back extensor muscles are required to 
generate more active forces during static and dynamic 
tasks and cause fatigue. 
4.2. Angular velocities and EMG 
The reduced ability of muscles to generate strength 
may be a significant risk factor for low back pain (G. 
Shin, D’Souza, and Liu 2009). P. O’Sullivan et al. (2006) 
observed decreased activity in lumbar MF while mov -
ing from upright sitting to slump sitting, suggesting 
the occurrence of relaxation of the spinal stabilising 
muscles. However, the muscle activity pattern was 
highly variable. In our study there were no statistically 
significant differences between groups. However, FRR 
of the left multifidus during forward bending was 
greater in the ‘high’ than in the ‘low’ sitting group. 
That finding may be the result of greater lumbar spine 
ROM during forward bending in the ‘high’ sitting 
group. However, the sEMG results are rather inconclu -
sive which is in line with O’Sullivan’s previous study. 
With regard to the large data scatter our results of 
sEMG should be interpreted with caution. 
Greater angular velocity of the spine observed in 
the ‘high’ sitting group could be considered a risk fac -
tor in back muscles injuries. Yoon, Shiekhzadeh, and 
Nordin (2012) suggests that faster movement during 
loading tasks significantly affects passive spinal ele -
ments due to the viscoelastic characteristics of tissues. 
Greater acceleration of movement requires coordi -
nated muscle recruitment to initiate and continue 
the movement. 
The different FRP levels found in participants with 
different flexibilities can be explained by the change 
of lumbar curvature. Previously, it has been demon -
strated that participants with low flexibility exhibited 
more evident FRP (Chen et al. 2018). Contrary, we 
have noticed that high sitting group, which showed 
greater lumbar-pelvic ratio, demonstrated greater FRP, 
nevertheless this finding was not statistically signifi -
cant and needs further investigations. 
4.3. Limitations and future research 
Our study has a few limitations. First, the effect sizes 
of statistically significant comparisons between groups 
are rather small (0.30–0.37). Thus, current results 
should be interpreted with caution and need further 
investigation. 
Second, we did not investigate hamstring extensi -
bility and we consider this a main limitation of our 
study. Previously, angular tests measured by goniome -
ters. such as active knee extensions, have been 
recommended as criterion measures (Mayorga-Vega, 
Merino-Marban, and Viciana 2014). An analysis of the 
relationship between hamstring flexibility, sitting time, 
and lumbar-pelvic kinematics may justify our reason -
ing about the causes of the current results. 
According to the authors’ knowledge, this is the 
first study investigating the relationship between sit -
ting behaviour and lumbar-pelvic kinematics during 
squatting and forward bending. Due to the fact that 
we have investigated only physically active people, it 
should be worth investigating whether the association 
between sitting time and lumbar-pelvic kinematics is 
stronger in non-physically active persons or 
office workers. 
4.4. Practical applications 
This study has practical implications for students and 
other young people continuing their education who 
spend a lot of time sitting but are trying to meet the 
PA recommendation. Our study shows that lumbar 
pelvic kinematics can be affected by sitting time even 
when physical activity level is sufficient or high. 
Therefore, sedentary behaviour should be taken into 
account by physical therapists, coaches and other pro -
fessionals when evaluating squats and forward flexion 
movements. Higher education is a critical period of 
life when health behaviours can be formed in young 
adults. Thus, education for reducing sedentary behav -
iours among students could be a public health strat -
egy for LBP prevention (Vainshelboim et al. 2019). 
According to Heneghan et al. (2018) it is probable 
that young adults are a ‘risk’ group for developing 
future musculoskeletal complaints like LBP, and it is 
very likely that they will work in occupations where 
substantial periods of time will be sitting.

Understanding lumbar-pelvic kinematics can provide 
new information about not clear relationship of seden -
tary behaviour and risk of LBP. Several biomechanical 
studies have been conducted to clarify the relation -
ship between sitting and LBP (Tor �en 2001; Mork and 
Westgaard 2009). Our investigation can be considered 
as a starting point for making more specific research 
about how sitting time is associated with biomechan -
ics during functional dynamic tasks to those accrued 
through engaging in moderate-to-vigorous phys -
ical activity. 
5. Conclusions 
Lumbar-pelvic kinematics are significantly altered by 
longer sitting times in physically active people. 
Lumbar-pelvic ratios during squatting and forward 
bending and spine velocity during squatting were 
greater in participants who have been sitting longer 
than 7 h/day. That suggests that sitting time can be 
considered one of the factors influencing lumbar- 
pelvic biomechanics during dynamic tasks. Thus, it 
should be taken into account during an assessment of 
squatting and forward bending movements. Moreover, 
our study extends our understanding of sitting behav -
iours and tries to explain the relationship between 
longer sitting time and potential LBP risk.
