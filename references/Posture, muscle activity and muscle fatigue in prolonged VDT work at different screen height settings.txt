Posture, muscle activity and muscle fatigue in prolonged VDT
work at diﬀerent screen height settings
JAN SEGHERS*, A RNAUD JOCHEM and A RTHUR SPAEPEN
Laboratory of Ergonomics and Occupational Biomechanics,
Department of Kinesiology, Katholieke Universiteit Leuven, Tervuursevest 101,
3001 Leuven
Keywords: Computer terminals; Electromyography; Muscle fatigue; Neck
muscles; Shoulder; Posture.
With the increasing use of video display terminals (VDTs), there is growing
concern over the corresponding increase in the number of health problems
reported. Although much research has focused on identifying the optimal screen
height, there is to date no consensus. This study aimed to investigate the eﬀect of
prolonged (89 min) VDT work at four diﬀerent screen heights on head – neck
posture, muscle activity and the development of muscle fatigue. The results show
that lowering screen height, starting from 15 cm above the baseline (i.e. top of the
screen level with eye height while sitting), decreased the ear – eye angle, increased
the viewing angle, increased the viewing angle relative to the ear – eye line, and
increased the muscle activity of the neck extensor muscles. There were also some
signiﬁcant time eﬀects on postural angles and muscle activity. In this study there
were only rare occurrences of muscle fatigue, deﬁned as a simultaneous increase in
EMG amplitude and a shift of the EMG power spectrum to lower frequencies.
Muscle activity increased signiﬁcantly in some muscles and for certain screen
heights.
1. Introduction
The use of visual display terminals (VDT) has become an activity in daily work for
an increasing number of individuals. Although this implies a decrease in traditional
forms of manual labour and the associated discomforts, new health problems arise.
Musculoskeletal discomfort, for example, has been found to occur and increase
during a working day among VDT workers (Hagberg and Sundelin 1986). Another
health concern is the visual discomfort while working with a computer screen
(Bergqvist et al . 1995). Much research has focused on identifying the ideal VDT
screen height considering musculoskeletal problems of the shoulder and neck region
and visual strain. The requirements to decrease musculoskeletal strain while also
decreasing visual strain may conflict, and the feasibility of this remains debatable.
Several authors have studied the effect of screen height and document position on
musculoskeletal strain. Hamilton (1996) found that the lowest levels of tension in the
neck muscles were experienced with the smallest deviations in head position.
Villanueva et al . (1997) found a strong significant correlation between posture
changes caused by different screen heights and muscle activities. Decrease in neck

due to the support of the forearms (Laursen and Jensen 2000). Trapezius muscle load

fatigue when working at their screens for several hours (Hagberg and Sundelin
1986). In general, these authors agree that a higher monitor placement is required to
reduce musculoskeletal strain. Most studies describe a change in muscle activity, but
research into muscle fatigue is less common. A longer duration is required to
produce and identify muscle fatigue, while most authors have only used a 5 – 30 min
computer task. Turville et al . (1998) used a 2 h reading task, but could not detect a
significant shift in median frequency (MDF) in the EMG signal, so no fatigue was
identified for any of the screen heights studied. An exact screen height, which reduces
muscle fatigue, has not yet been established. Further research is therefore required to
determine whether the recommendation for musculoskeletal problems and low
muscle activity can also be applied to reduce muscle fatigue.
Recommendations for prevention of visual fatigue, however, confuse the issue.
The resting point of the eyes is considered to be 15 8 below the horizontal when the
head is upright, so to reduce visual strain a VDT screen placement lower than the
horizontal line of sight is recommended. Several authors have described the effects of
a downward gaze and a downward viewing angle on eye comfort. Sotoyama et al .
(1997) reported a higher tear volume while gazing 30 8 downwards than in the 0 and
158 positions. The proposed cause was a decrease in ocular surface. Larger ocular
surface has been found to increase visual strain due to increased tear evaporation.
Other variables that affect eye discomfort are vertical gaze inclination and viewing
distance (Lie et al . 1997). Villanueva et al . (1996) reported a correlation between
neck angle and screen height and suggested that the neck seems to be the most
important component in adjusting the position of the head to find the preferred
viewing angle. In their study, significant changes of viewing angle and vertical gaze
direction were found for different screen heights. Lower monitor settings gave larger
viewing angles and larger vertical gaze directions. Generally it was found that high
monitor placement caused more visual strain than lower monitor placement
(Villanueva et al . 1996, Saito and Sotoyama et al . 1997, Sotoyama et al . 1997,
Burgess-Limerick et al. 1998, Psihogios et al. 2001). Jaschinski et al. (1998) reported
that high monitor placement could provoke eye discomfort, especially when the
monitor was placed closer to the eyes than the subject’s preferred distance. However,
viewing distance and gaze inclination did not change significantly with the time of
day (Jaschinski et al. 1998). Jaschinski et al. (1997) reported that during close vision,
the ocular muscles are loaded to a lesser extent when vertical gaze is directed
downwards. Ankrum (1997) also reported that screen-tilt influences eye discomfort.
This study showed that a monitor with the top tipped forward caused more postural
and visual discomfort. Taking all factors into consideration these studies seem to
recommend a VDT screen height lower than the horizontal. Postural changes with

al. (1998) did find a significant increase in postural shifts in a 2 h computer task, but
change in head tilt was not examined in their study. More research is needed on this
issue to find how working time influences changes in head and body posture.
The effect of different screen sizes on posture was studied by Villenueva et al .
(1996). They suggested that the use of larger computer screens might induce an
upward gaze and a more erect posture. Burgess-Limerick et al . (1998) questioned
whether the results for normal sized monitors found in their study would hold for
different monitor sizes. Sommerich et al. (2001) reported lower activity in all muscles
using a larger monitor, though for some muscles there was a restriction on this effect,
for which no clear explanation was apparent. Previous research has aimed to
examine the effects of changing screen height, but in several studies muscle fatigue
was not detected. Most authors compared means and did not look for time effects
within one specific screen height setting.
Considering the findings described on musculoskeletal and visual strain, the study
presented here was designed to compare signs of fatigue in both musculoskeletal and
visual aspects for different screen height settings. The main aim was to determine the
onset and degree of muscle fatigue within a certain time period while working at a
specific screen height, and then to compare these for different screen heights. This
same method was also used to examine time effects in postural and visual changes.
2. Method
2.1. Subject details
Sixteen subjects (eight male and eight female; 14 right-handed and two left-handed)
gave their informed consent to participate in this study. No exclusion criteria were
used. The subjects were asked to complete a form providing information of possible
relevance to the study results. Seven subjects had a history of musculoskeletal neck
and shoulder symptoms during VDT work, and the remaining nine reported no
problems. Glasses or contact lenses were used by 10 of the subjects, none of whom
used bifocals. The subjects formed a rather heterogeneous group, for whom the
demographic data are presented in table 1.
2.2. Materials
The VDT workplace was fully adaptable to each individual subject, comprising a
table with adjustable height, an adjustable office chair, and a monitor arm. A 17-inch
cathode ray tube (CRT) screen was set up at three screen heights, and a 15-inch Thin
Film Transistor (TFT) flat panel display for a laptop computer was used for the
lowest placement. A keyboard and mouse were used at all screen heights. EMG data
were gathered with a telemetric EMG-device (model ME3000P8, Mega Electronics
Ltd., Kuopia, Finland). The pre-amplified EMG signal (analogue differential
amplifier) was measured at a sample rate of 1000 Hz. The signal bandwidths of the
high- and low pass filters were 8 and 500 Hz respectively. Posture was analysed with
a Sony Hi 8 video camera, which digitized the data to measure several angles
between reflective markers placed on the subjects.
2.3. Task
All subjects worked for about 89 min at each of the four different VDT screen
heights. The test sessions were spread over 2 days due to the length of each session.
Subjects had to work at two screen heights in 1 day and were asked to return 1 week

later, on the same day and at the same time. Between screen heights, subjects rested
for at least 45 min. To neutralize the effect of the sequence of the different test
situations, the order of the change in screen height was completely randomized. The
work task included an experimental part (E) and an Internet based search task (I). A
schematic representation of the experimental protocol is shown in figure 1. During
the experimental part of the task, the subjects played a standardized keyboard
computer game for 5 min. The game required visual monitoring and control of the
arrow keys on the keyboard, using the right hand only. Subjects rested their left hand
on the table. During this part, the raw EMG signal and video images were recorded
and subjects were asked to make no abrupt movements. The Internet based search
task lasted 9 min and subjects were allowed to reposition themselves in the chair and
to move neck and shoulders if they preferred to do so. During this part of the
experiment, the subjects used the keyboard and computer mouse. The intention was
to create a situation similar to their normal VDT working practices.

Three types of data were collected: electromyography, video images and

2.4.1. Electromyographic analyses : The raw EMG signal was recorded with a
portable EMG device (model ME3000P8, Mega Electronics Ltd., Kuopia, Finland)
for 5 min, seven times within the 89 min of each session. Bipolar Silver-Silver
Chloride surface electrodes (Nikomed, Denmark) were placed on eight muscles (see
table 2) situated around the neck – shoulder region. The inter-electrode distance was
20 mm and placement of the electrodes conformed to Seniam recommendations
(Hermens et al . 1999) and recommendations by De Luca (1997).
Two EMG parameters were extracted from the raw EMG signal. The averaged
rectified EMG (AEMG) was calculated (time window = 0.5 s) to quantify the
muscle activity. Myoelectrical signs of muscle fatigue are characterized by a
simultaneous increase in AEMG and decrease in MDF (Luttmann et al . 2000).
Therefore, the median frequency (MDF) was calculated using a Fast Fourier
Transformation (FFT). The slopes (% change per time unit) of the EMG
parameters, determined using linear regression techniques, quantified the EMG
changes over time.
Comparison between subjects and test sessions was possible by normalizing all
EMG parameters as a percentage of the RVC. The RVC activity for the trapezius
muscle (TRP) was a 90 8 abduction of the arm with a 1 kg weight in the hand of
the arm being tested. The RVC activity for the deltoid muscle (DLT) was a 90 8
flexion of the arm in the sagittal plane with a 1 kg weight in the hand of the arm
being tested. The RVCs for the sternocleidomastoı ¨ deus muscles (SCM) and the
neck extensor muscles (NXT, splenius capitus) were tested with the subjects lying
on a treatment couch in supine and prone positions respectively, with the neck
and head extending beyond the end of the couch, and therefore supported by the
neck muscles alone. In this way the weight of the head acted as an external load
for the neck muscles. All RVC tests were taken before the sessions, and
measurements were recorded for 5 s for each test. The RVC tests for the TRP
and DLT muscles were separate, so that each arm could be tested separately,
while the SCM and NXT muscles were tested in one 5-s test session.

2.4.2. Posture analyses with video images : During EMG measurement, posture and
workstation settings were captured by a video camera (Sony Hi 8 video camera).
These images were taken on the ﬁrst, fourth and seventh 5-minute EMG
measurement period and digitized on the 30th, and 150th and 270th second within
the 5-minute period (300 s), a method used previously by Turville et al . (1998).
Diﬀerences between screen heights were tested by taking the data from the 4th EMG
measurement on the 45th minute of each session.
The following angles were analysed (see figure 3):
. the Ear – Eye Angle (EEA), between the horizontal and a line joining the outer
cantus of the eye and the trages of the ear;
. the Viewing Angle (VA), between the horizontal and a line joining the outer
cantus of the eye and the middle of the computer screen;
. the Viewing Angle relative to the Ear – Eye line (VArel).
Angles below the horizontal plane are indicated with a minus sign.
Repeated digitizing of the same captured video image, revealed variations in VA,
EEA and VArel of about 0.4 8,3 8 and 3 8 respectively depending on the distance
between the markers. The use of more precise instruments might lead to more
accurate measurements. The results presented in 3.2 show that the method was
sufficiently accurate to detect the influence of VDT screen height on the calculated
angles.
2.4.3. Measurements of subjective feelings of discomfort : To give an indication of
the subjective feelings of discomfort of the eyes, the neck and both shoulders, the
Borg CR-10 rating scale (Borg 1982) was used. This scale has ratio properties using
numbers and verbal expressions. The Borg scale has been used by several authors to
describe both discomfort and fatigue (Hagberg and Sundelin 1986, Sundelin and
Hagberg 1992, Hermans and Spaepen 1995, Swanson et al . 1997, Liao and Drury

2.4.4. Statistical analyses : Pearson correlation and Student’s t-tests were used to

were tested using a Friedman ANOVA test for non-parametric data. The level of
signiﬁcance was chosen as p 4 0.05 for all statistical tests. Unless otherwise stated,
all data are expressed as the mean + SD.
3. Results
3.1. Subjects
Study of the subjects showed that the group with a history of shoulder or neck
problems had worked for a significantly longer period behind VDT screens than the
group with no complaints ( p 5 0.01). In this sample there was an obvious
correlation between age and time spent behind the computer per day ( r = 0.69,
p 5 0.01), probably because the subjects above 24 years of age had computer-task
related jobs.
3.2. Posture
Table 3 shows the mean + SD values of postural angles with the different screen
heights. Change in screen height significantly affected Viewing Angle (VA), Ear –
Eye Angle (EEA) and Viewing Angle relative to the Ear – Eye line (VArel). In
particular, the VA decreased when the VDT screen was in a higher position
( p 5 0.01) and the eyes needed to be directed more upwards. There was a greater
EEA with higher screen heights ( p 5 0.01), so the head was more erect. The VArel
also showed significant differences between the different heights, being larger for
lower monitor height settings ( p 5 0.01).

4. Discussion
The heterogeneous sample in this study could make external validity rather high,
taking into account the fact that the test group was small ( n = 16) and mean age was
24 years.
4.1. Posture
The observed mean postural angles were similar to those reported in previous
studies. The EEA determined by Jampel and Shi (1992) was 15 8 above the horizontal
line of the eyes. Grey et al . (1966) found an EEA of 14.5 8 in erect posture. In the
present study a similar 14.6 8 EEA was determined. The results show a significant
change in posture when screen height was altered. The VA, EEA and VArel differed
significantly between all screen height settings. VA and VArel increased with lower
monitor positions while EEA decreased with the VDT screen in the low placement,
confirming previous research (Villanueva et al . 1996, 1997, Burgess-Limerick et al .
1998, 2000, Psihogios et al . 2001, Sommerich et al . 2001). Time affected posture in
VA and EEA (Table 3). VA significantly decreased with time in the high, baseline
and low VDT screen positions. There was no difference in the laptop condition. EEA
increased only in the high monitor position (21 8 –2 48). The time effects in VA could
not be attributed to a change in head tilt in the sagittal plane, because EEA did not
change significantly with time for the other screen heights. Therefore, the change in
VA was probably due to subjects slouching in their chairs during the 89-min test
sessions. This slouching would cause the eyes to be positioned at a lower level,
relative to the VDT screen, so VA appeared to decrease. In the highest screen
condition the EEA did change significantly with time because the eyes could not
compensate in the high screen placement. This possibility is logical, as upward gaze
is not recommended (Sotoyama et al . 1997).
A fourth angle, which could not be measured in this study, is the gaze angle. Gaze
angle is defined by the position of the eyes and can be determined by measuring the
ocular surface (Villanueva 1996). However, only part of the changes in VA can be
attributed to gaze angle changes, because EEA does not fully account for the
changes in VA (Villaneuva et al . 1996, Burgess-Limerick et al . 1998). Sommerich et
al. (2001) found a 13 8 compensation for gaze angle in a total 35 8 change of VA. In
our study a total gaze angle compensation of 9 8 was found within a 27 8 change of
VA. Although significance between screen heights was not tested, the highest
contribution (38%) of gaze angle to the change in VA was found between baseline
and low VDT screen heights. Between the low VDT screen height and laptop
monitor height the contribution of the gaze angle was only 27% while between the
highest and baseline position 31% was found. This means more flexion of the neck
was required, and less change in gaze angle was used below a VA of 7 178. These
results for low monitor placement confirm research by Sommerich et al . (2001).
Since screen heights higher than the baseline were examined in this study, it is
concluded that more head tilt and less change in gaze angle (31%) are used when
working with VDT screens above baseline, and this is consistent with the resulting
time effects on posture described above.

in the NXT muscles ( p 5 0.01). With the lowering of the computer screen from
15 cm above baseline to table height, there was a 50% increase in NXT muscle

differences (see table 4). The highest and the baseline screen heights showed a
significantly lower activity in the right NXT muscle compared to the laptop
condition ( p 5 0.01). The left NXT muscle showed significantly lower muscle
activity between the highest screen height and the low and the laptop conditions
( p 5 0.01), and in the baseline screen height compared to the low and the laptop
conditions ( p 4 0.05). Comparable effects were reported by Hamilton (1996),
Villanueva et al . (1997), Turville et al . (1998) and Sommerich et al . (2001). The
differences with the three VDT screen heights and the laptop screen height were also
comparable to results on NXT muscle activity found by Villanueva et al . (1998).
However, it was not the aim of the present study to make a comparison between
CRT and flat panel displays, but to determine how muscle activity was affected when
a laptop was placed on the table surface, as often practiced in many work
environments. In conclusion, the highest level of NXT muscle activity occurred in
the laptop condition, particularly caused by the extreme flexion in the neck.
There was much variation in the activity of the other muscles (see table 4), so that
a clear trend could not be identified, possibly explained by the low activity levels that
were measured. In particular the very low activity in the anterior deltoid muscle is
remarkable but understandable, since it is the muscle that is expected to be the least
affected by changes in screen height. This muscle was included in the study because it
has rarely been investigated elsewhere, and it was of interest to know whether
prolonged use of the keyboard and mouse would affect its activity level. However,
deltoid muscle activity was hardly affected in this study. Another reason for the low
levels of muscle activity could be the fact that the workstation was customised to the
subjects to ensure good support and comfort. In this study, the adjustment of the
arm rests of the office chair and the set up of the table height provided a full support
for the forearm (see figure 2). This argument is supported by Aara ˚ s et al. (1997) who
reported that trapezius muscle activity was significantly lower when forearm support
was provided. Laursen and Jensen (2000) also reported that the lack of significance
in their study was probably due to the low trapezius muscle activity caused by
forearm support. Nevertheless, a significantly higher level of trapezius muscle
activity was detected in the right trapezius muscle compared to the left muscle
( p 4 0.05), probably attributable to the right-handed control of the cursor keys
during the experimental part of the study.
4.3. EMG changes over time
The myoelectrical signs of muscle fatigue are characterized by a simultaneous
increase in AEMG and decrease in MDF (De Luca 1997, Clarys 2000, Luttmann et
al. 2000). These indications of muscle fatigue were hardly apparent for any of the
muscles and screen heights and can probably only be detected with longer test
periods. Turville et al . (1998) even suggested recording the EMG signal during a
whole working day. Nevertheless, a significant increase in muscle activity over the
course of the test session, quantified by the slope in AEMG, could be detected for
some muscles and screen heights. However, an increase in EMG amplitude can be
caused by several factors including an increase in the force production of the muscle,
postural changes or muscle fatigue (Luttmann et al . 2000). Muscle fatigue based on

an increase of the EMG amplitude is only possible when the force production during
the task is kept constant in combination with minor changes in working posture. In
this study EMG parameters were extracted from the measurements where the
subjects played a standardized computer game, to ensure a comparable workload of
the task during each of the seven measurement periods. Additionally, the time-
dependent changes in the position of the head were minimal. This is reflected in the
absence of time effects in the EEA, except for the highest VDT placement condition.
For this condition, it is doubtful whether the increase in AEMG can be seen as an
indication of muscle fatigue. Table 5 shows the muscles for which the electrical
activity (AEMG) changed with time. The largest increase in muscle activity was in
the highest monitor position (baseline + 15 cm). At this screen height, AEMG
showed a significant ( p 4 0.05) or almost significant ( p 4 0.10) increase during the
89-minute test session for four of the eight recorded muscles (right TRP, right NXT,
left NXT and right SCM). Irrespective of the screen height, the most remarkable
increase in electrical activity occurred in the TRPr muscle. After 89 min of VDT
work, the muscle activity of the TRPr muscle increased by about 5%. If this increase
continued linearly over an 8-h working day, a quarter more muscle activity would be
required to perform the same task at the end of the day. This finding reinforces the
work of Hermans and Spaepen (1995), who also found an increased EMG activity of
the trapezius muscle at the end of the working day. Although no postural changes of
the shoulder were analysed in the present study, elevating the right shoulder while
manipulating the cursor keys with the right hand during the computer game may
have caused the detected increase over time.
The simultaneous increase in muscle activity of the right NXT and SCM muscle is
also noteworthy. The increase in both muscles might be explained by the increase in
EEA with time. Adjustments of the office chair prevented subjects from being able to
lean backwards. The centre of gravity of the head may therefore have been
positioned in front of the rotation axis. Based on this possibility, it could be assumed
that neck extension was caused by a contraction of right and left NXT muscles (see
table 4). The increase in muscle activity of the right SCM could be due to co-
contraction. Therefore, prolonged VDT work at this high monitor position might be
harmful.
4.5. Visual discomfort
Although not statistically significant, a tendency towards more eyestrain was found
in the laptop condition. More eye discomfort being reported while working in the
laptop condition could be related to a combination of several factors. Firstly, the size
of the laptop screen was somewhat smaller than the CRT monitor (Villanueva et al.
1998). However, the visible diagonal size of a CRT monitor is always smaller than
the tube’s actual diagonal size. A 17-inch CRT monitor has an edge area and the
visible diagonal is approximately 16-inches. TFT monitors do not have an edge area,
which means that a 15-inch TFT monitor is almost the same as the 17-inch CRT
monitor. Secondly, a lower brightness of TFT monitors was noticed but the
resolution was kept constant. Additionally, the absence of flickering, geometry and
convergence errors due to the technical nature of TFT displays might even improve
the visual comfort for the user. Finally, a greater horizontal distance from the eyes to
the middle of the screen in the laptop condition (mean + SD distance = 76 + 5 cm),
compared to the 60 cm with the other screen heights, may have contributed to the
reports of more eye discomfort.

5. Conclusions
From this study, we can conclude that the height of the VDT screen has an influence
on VA, EEA, and VArel and muscle activity of the left and right NXT muscles. In
addition, the most remarkable increase in muscle activity over the course of the 89-
minute test session was with the highest screen height (baseline + 15 cm). Despite
the occurrence of some postural changes with time at this screen height position, the
EMG activity increase is interpreted as a sign of muscle fatigue, although further
research is needed to clarify this.
Finally, with respect to physical and visual comfort, a VA between 8 8 and 16 8
below the horizontal plane is recommended from the results. These recommenda-
tions should be generalized with care. Results from this study, using larger VDT
screen sizes ( 4 15 inches), are comparable to previous findings using 14-inch or 15-
inch monitors, although less significance was found in the other muscles tested in the
study. This can be attributed to the careful arrangement of the workplace according
to the subject’s individual anthropometry.
In future studies longer test periods, sensitive EMG equipment and postural
control may be needed to detect muscle fatigue during VDT work. However, it
is not possible to regulate all the confounding factors. From a practical point of
view, it is may be better not to disturb people’s working practices. It is possible
that VDT users even try to alleviate musculoskeletal discomfort and/or prevent
fatigue by making non-work related body movements (Karwowski et al . 1994).
The myoelectric signs of muscle fatigue in low-level, long duration contractions
are also still unclear. Individual differences in fiber type distributions and/or
force control strategies might make a per son more or less sensitive to
musculoskeletal disorders.
Muscle activity, muscle fatigue, physical and visual comfort should all be taken
into account in recommending a general screen height for a group of VDT workers,
and workstations need to be adjusted to the needs of each individual to regulate
musculoskeletal and visual discomfort.
