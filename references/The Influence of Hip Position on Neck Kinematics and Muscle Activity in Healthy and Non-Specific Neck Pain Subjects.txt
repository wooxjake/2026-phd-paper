DUDLEY‡1,2  1School of Allied Health Professions, Loma Linda University, Loma Linda, CA, USA; 2Department of Physical Therapy, Loma Linda University, Loma Linda, CA, USA; 3Department of Physical Therapy, University of St. Augustine for Health Sciences, Austin, TX, USA  †Denotes graduate student author, ‡Denotes professional author  ABSTRACT International Journal of Exercise Science 17(1): 1280-1293, 2024. Neck pain is a widespread problem in society with many variables influencing its cause. The angle of the hip may influence the kinematics of the neck in addition to the myoelectrical activation of the surrounding musculature that contributes to the development of neck pain. The purpose of this study was to investigate the changes in spinal inclination angles and muscle activity in the neck using a 10 deg wedge in a forward slope, neutral and rear slope seated position. Forty subjects were recruited for this study. Each subject sat at a workstation under the 3 different sitting positions for ten minutes for each condition. During this time, the subjects typed a prompt for seven minutes and rested for the remaining 3 minutes. This was repeated for each position. Inclination angles and electromyography data were collected at different intervals during each trial. The results showed a significant difference in mean inclination angle among the three positions in the pain group (p<0.01). The head and neck inclination angles were higher in the rear sloped position compared to the forward slope (p<0.001). In the pain group, there was no significant change in the mean muscle activity in the UT in the forward slope as compared to the rear slope and neutral positions (p ≤0.01). The results of this study suggest that the forward sloped position provides a favorable body alignment compared to the other positions which may then lead to less unwanted muscle activation.  KEY WORDS: Myoelectrical, cervical spine, ergonomics, biomechanics  INTRODUCTION  Neck pain is a prevalent issue in contemporary society, affecting a considerable proportion of the general population. An exact percentage of the world's population that has neck pain is difficult to provide given the differences in the populations studied. However, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 187 studies reported a one-year prevalence rate of neck pain of 4.9% to 77.8% across all age groups and regions of the world (12). Another study indicated that the prevalence of neck pain in the general population can reach up to 40% (2). The range of neck pain in occupational settings can vary between 6-76% (2). This high prevalence is associated with significant an economic burden. In the Netherlands, the estimated cost of neck pain has reached $686 million dollars annually and is expected to increase worldwide (5). Thus,

understanding the scope and impact of neck pain is essential to develop effective strategies to mitigate its effects on individuals and society.  The etiology of neck pain is multifactorial and can be affected by numerous factors such as age, gender, occupation, lifestyle, and comorbidities (12). One of the main factors associated with neck pain is working in a seated position (13). Sitting at work for more than 95% of the time is considered a risk factor for developing neck pain (2). Additionally, there is a positive correlation between neck flexion in a seated position and neck pain(2). Forward head posture (FHP) was not only found to be associated with neck pain but also with causing spasms of the upper trapezius (UT) (7). Furthermore, holding the head in a forward posture while performing a computer task resulted in increased cervical spine loading and muscle activity compared to a neutral head posture (22). Neutral positions are shown to reduce cervical erector spinae (CES) activity and load on the cervical spine (9). While sitting kinematics are linked to the development of non-specific neck pain (NSNP), its multifactorial nature suggests that the cause cannot be solely attributed to static seated neck posture.  Beyond their influence on muscle activation, certain postures significantly affect spinal intradiscal pressure (22, 25, 28). The lumbar spine experiences peak pressure during sitting and standing, while sustained cervical flexion increases pressure in the cervical spine relative to a neutral posture (25, 28). These findings suggest the importance of maintaining a neutral posture, particularly during prolonged sitting when performing computer work, to potentially reduce the risk of spinal discogenic problems.  Kinematics of the neck are correlated to the trunk and position of the head (7). Winkel and Westgaard stated that a curvature change in one spinal region will result in alterations in curvature in other regions of the spine (29). The loss of lumbar lordosis and forward inclination of the head during sitting has been found to produce significant mechanical stress on the spine especially during prolonged postures or repetitive cervical movements (4). This increased mechanical stress may lead to spinal degeneration and related symptoms over time, if not corrected.  Quek et al. reports that there was a significant negative association between thoracic kyphosis and cervical range of motion in older adults (23). Therefore, to optimize sitting posture, it is important to consider the alignment and movement of both the neck and trunk.  A popular clinical assumption is that by decreasing the degree of posterior pelvic rotation and thus the tendency for the lumbar spine to flatten, the mechanical stresses of sitting can be minimized. The influence of sitting posture on head and neck posture and muscle activity is discussed in detail by Caneiro et al. (6). The authors investigated the effects of different sitting postures, including the slumped position (6). Their findings revealed that the slumped posture was associated with increased thoracic spine flexion, neck flexion, and anterior head translation kinematically (6). These results suggest that postural adjustments may be necessary to reduce the risk of developing neck pain or other musculoskeletal disorders associated with prolonged sitting. Regarding myoelectrical activation, there was increased CES and thoracic erector spinae (TES) activity while sitting in the slumped position (6). When sitting in a neutral posture, there

was neutral head alignment and decreased myoelectrical activity of the CES and TES (6). However, the study did not account for the hip joint.   In addition to the spine, the hip joint is an essential variable for the ideal sitting posture. There are studies that investigated the effect of hip angles on lumbar spine kinematics and muscle activity but not many related to hip angles and cervical spine kinematics and muscle activity (30). In a study conducted by Yim et al., a 10-degree wedge was used to examine the relationship between hip angle and spinal kinematics, and cervical muscle activity. The researchers found that a forward sloping wedge (decreased hip angle) resulted in a decreased FHP and a reduction of CES myoelectrical activity as compared to two other chair seat options (30). However, no significant changes in UT muscle activity were observed (30).  These findings suggest that adjusting the hip angle using a wedge may be an effective approach for improving sitting posture. One limitation of the study is that the participants were all healthy individuals. Therefore, the influence of the hip angle on spinal kinematics and neck muscle activity remains inconclusive, especially in the NSNP population. A study by Johnston et al. (2008), investigated neck movement and muscle activity characteristics in female office workers with neck pain. The investigators found that the pain group showed less rotational range of motion (ROM), increased CES myoelectrical activity and inability to reduce UT and CES myoelectrical activity after the task was concluded (16). These results provide a different perspective compared to the results of the Yim et al. study. Other studies show that there is a positive association between increased UT activity and neck pain (7, 11). However, in these other studies, the hip angles were not considered.   Because of the current gap in literature, the purpose of this study is to investigate the changes in cervical muscle activity and kinematics in various hip angle seated positions in subjects with NSNP and healthy individuals.  We hypothesized that individuals with NSNP will demonstrate more inclination in sagittal plane motion in head, neck, and trunk angles as the hip moves into more flexion and an increase in muscle activation, particularly in the UT muscle, over time during rear slope and neutral sitting position but not in the forward slope position.  METHODS  Participants Forty subjects (20 healthy, 20 neck pain) between the ages of 25-45 were recruited for this investigation. Of the recruited subjects, 23 were female and 17 were male. The sample size was based on an a priori power analysis of 0.8, effect size of 0.25 and type I error of 0.05. Healthy subjects were included if they had no reports of neck pain, injury and met the age range. Neck pain subjects were included if they have been experiencing pain for more than 3 months, neck mobility deficits and neck pain with headaches. Neck pain subjects were screened for inclusion by a licensed physical therapist to ensure each subject met the criteria. Those with a history of neck injury, spinal surgery, neck pain with radicular symptoms below the elbow, hip pathology, osteoporosis, malignancy, and pregnancy were excluded in both groups. Prior to any testing, participants read and signed an Informed Consent Document and Protected Health Information (PHI) release form approved by the Loma Linda University Institutional Review Board (IRB#:

Protocol  The Neck Disability Index (NDI) is an outcome measure in clinical and research settings that measures self-related disability in patients with neck pain (26, 31). The questionnaire consists of 10 items and is rated on a six-point scale from 0 (no disability) to 5 (complete disability) with a maximum possible score of 50 points (27, 31). The raw score can then be expressed as a percentage of disability which was used for this study.   All subjects self-reported demographic info including age, gender, occupation, sitting hours and medications. Pain was reported using the numeric pain rating scale (NPRS) (8).   The Flexion, Abduction and External Rotation test (FABER) was used to assess general mobility between the left and right hip for each subject (24). The subjects were placed supine on the plinth and the tested leg was passively taken into a “Figure 4” position with the foot resting on the opposite tibia (3, 24). Then a measurement was taken to assess the distance from the tested leg to the plinth. This was measured by assessing the distance from the lateral femoral epicondyle to the plinth in centimeters and was assessed bilaterally (10). A comparison was done between the pain and control group. Figure 1 illustrates the positioning and measurement technique for this test.

Statistical Analysis Data was analyzed using the Statistical Package of Social Sciences (SPSS) version 28.0.  Mean ± standard deviation (SD) was computed for quantitative variables and frequency (%) for categorical variables. The normality of the quantitative variables was examined using Shapiro Wilk tests and Boxplots. To compare gender and occupation by study group, Chi-square of independence was used. For quantitative variables, the two study groups were compared using independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test when the distribution was not approximately normal. In the pain and control groups, mean inclination angle by sitting condition (FS versus N versus RS) were compared using one way analysis of variance (ANOVA). If the results were statistically significant, post hoc comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni test to determine which sitting conditions were significantly different.   A mixed 3x2 factorial analysis of variance was used to examine changes in inclination angles by sitting condition (FS versus N versus RS) and group. In each study group, mean EMG for CES, UT, TES, and SCM muscle activity pre and post typing activity by sitting condition were compared using one way analysis of variance (ANOVA). In addition, mixed factorial analysis of variance was used to examine changes in muscle activity by sitting condition over time. Within each sitting condition, changes in EMG CES, UT, TES, and SCM muscle activity pre versus post typing was compared using paired t-test for each study group. Analysis of covariance was used to examine changes in muscle activity by study group over time while controlling for the number of hours of work. The level of significance was set at p≤0.05. DISCUSSION  The primary aim of this study was to investigate the changes in different hip angle positions in a seated position on cervical muscle activity and kinematics in subjects with non-specific neck pain and contrast their findings to asymptomatic controls. Under the assigned conditions, the hypothesis was supported, finding significant myoelectrical activity in the UT muscles, as well as CES, in subjects with neck pain after office-simulated prolonged sitting for 10 minutes during RS and N postures but not FS seated position. Additionally, there was more inclination in sagittal plane motion in head, neck, and trunk angles as the hip moved into more flexion as seen in the RS posture.   According to our results, using a reclined seat (RS) led to increased head and neck inclination, potentially resulting in heightened muscle activity in the CES and UT, which could raise the risk of neck pain (22). Furthermore, the pain group exhibited less trunk inclination in the RS position compared to the FS position, indicating a decreased lordosis in the RS. In a study by Yim et al. (30), a 10-degree angled FS seat surface yielded the highest cervical vertebral angle and the least forward head posture (FHP), closely resembling the ideal neutral alignment. This suggests that an FS chair seat may promote better postural alignment during seated work in asymptomatic adults. The same study also revealed that a RS seat reduced lumbar lordotic angles and increased thoracic kyphotic angles more than a flat or forward-sloping seat. This suggests that prolonged sitting in an RS chair may not maintain a neutral spinal posture in asymptomatic office workers. Consequently, the findings endorse the FS position as more favorable for individuals with neck pain.  The findings in this study suggest that regardless of neck pain, subjects experienced increased myoelectrical activation of CES and UT muscles over time during typing, except in the FS position for those with pain. This suggests that the FS position may be beneficial for UT activity in subjects with pain. Additionally, both groups showed increased TES and SCM activity after the task in the RS position in comparison to before the task. This increase in muscle activation could be due to the sustained and repetitive nature of typing, leading to muscle fatigue and stress. As a result, prolonged typing may increase muscle activity in the neck, thoracic, and shoulder regions, potentially contributing to or worsening neck pain.  Our findings align with those of the Johnston et al. study (16), which reported an inability to reduce UT myoelectrical activity after a specific sequence of movements while seated in female office workers with neck pain. Similarly, our results show increased UT myoelectrical activation over time in our subjects with pain when seated in the reclined and neutral postures. This is comparable to the findings of the Yim et al. study (30), which observed increased UT activity in healthy, asymptomatic adults in all positions. However, for our pain subjects, FS posture did not result in increased UT activity. Overall, these findings suggest that individuals with neck pain experience increased UT activation during seated office tasks in the RS and NS postures but not in the FS posture. This may indicate that a forward-sloping wedge may be beneficial for office workers with neck pain and low tissue reactivity. The results also offer the notion that the FS position might be preferable for some patients or could be used as a reinforcement

intervention. This change in hip angle may increase lumbar lordosis while reducing thoracic kyphosis, leading to a more ideal cervical posture and less required muscle activity from the UT. Similarly, a systematic review of 35 studies found a consistent association between FHP and neck pain (23). The review also found that FHP is linked to increased activity in the UT and SCM muscles, which are often related to neck pain. However, their study did not include specific hip angles.   Another important finding was that the pain group demonstrated less hip mobility compared to the control group. This is similar to what Lee et al. (18) found in their study, where the cervical pain group had asymmetrical hip mobility deficits compared to the control group (18). However, they did not consider a sitting wedge. Currently, there is no standard slope for office chair seats. The angle of the seat slope can vary depending on the design of the chair, its intended use, and user preferences (1). According to ergonomic experts, a common range for the seat slope angle of office chairs is between 0 and 5 degrees (1). A slight forward tilt of the seat can promote a neutral spine posture, reduce pressure on the back of the thighs, and improve blood flow to the lower extremities (1). Additionally, a FS chair seat can potentially help reduce neck pain and discomfort by increasing lumbar lordosis, promoting a more upright posture that aligns the head, neck, and spine. When sitting in a chair with a forward-sloping seat, the pelvis rotates slightly forward, naturally increasing lumbar lordosis and promoting a more upright sitting posture (14). This anterior rotation of the pelvis shifts the center of mass forward, increasing support through the floor (more than 25% of body weight) (14). This alignment can help reduce excessive activation of the posterior spine and cranioscapular muscles, alleviating strain on the neck muscles. This may be especially beneficial for those who spend long periods sitting at a desk or workstation.  The present study has some limitations. The subjects with NSNP had mild to moderate tissue reactivity/irritability (e.g., 3.5/10 mean resting pain) and mild to moderate disability (median = 16%) which may not be generalized to individuals with neck pain with high tissue reactivity. Future research should investigate subjects with neck pain with moderate to high tissue reactivity. The subjects were relatively young, with a mean age of 31.6 years. Although there was a good mixture of females (60%) and males (40%) in the present study, the sample size was too small to compare gender differences. Therefore, a larger sample size should compare gender and age differences. Thirteen or 65% of the subjects in the pain group were office workers which is a high percentage, but perhaps a stronger subject pool would have been one that only included sedentary office workers. Subjects with moderate to severe disability will need to be further investigated in future studies. This study did not consider seat depth, seat pan contours, or seat cushioning. The arm rests and seat pan were removed from a standard office chair and replaced the seat pain with non-contoured firm wedge which could affect generalizability of our findings. So, another consideration would be to investigate the effect of arm rests on myoelectrical activation and kinematics. Lastly, a more comprehensive assessment of the hip joint should be considered to thoroughly associate it and its effect on neck kinematics and muscle activity.
