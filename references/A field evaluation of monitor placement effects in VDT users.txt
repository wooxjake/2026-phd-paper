2. Methods
2.1. Participants
Twentysoftwarecompanyemployeesvolunteeredfor
the study (8 women, 12 men; 11 software developers, 5
quality assurance analysts, 3 managers, and one
technical support person). Averages for age, height,
and weight were 36.6yr (/C68.9), 173.3cm (/C66.8), and
70.1kg (/C612.6), respectively. No participants wore
bifocals. All looked primarily at the VDTduring work,
usingfewornosourcedocuments.VDTuseaveragedat
least 6h/day. Monitors were either 43 or 48cm in size
(17 or 19in, for 2 and 18 subjects, respectively).
Employees were invited to participate in the study if
their monitors and keyboards were aligned with the
mid-sagittal plane of their trunks, and if gaze angle
(GA-h) as they normally worked approximated either
eye level (08) or mid-level (/C017.58). This normal GA-h
would have been a function of anthropometry, work-
station design, and/or personal preference, as their
employer readily accommodated requests for work-
station adjustments.
Participants were evenly dichotomized, based on
normal GA-h. Subject grouping and experimental
manipulation are depicted in Fig.2. All participants
workedwiththeirmonitorspositionednormallyforthe
ﬁrsttwoweeks ofthe study. Forthesecondtwoweeks,
two participants in each group continued to work with
their monitors positioned normally (these two sub-
groupsofcontrolsubjectswerelabeledHi–HiandMid–
Mid), while the remaining 16 participants had their
monitors relocated so that they would experience the
alternatemonitorlocationfortwoweeks(thesetwosub-
groups of test subjects were labeled Hi–Mid and Mid–
Hi). Screen heights were changed by either adding or
removing risers beneath the monitors, the typical
customized intervention the employer normally pro-
vided. During data analysis, one participant from each
test group was excluded because viewing angles mea-
sured during the ﬁrst two weeks of the study were not
consistent with screening measurements made prior to
the start of the study.
2.2. Experimental design
A quasi-experimental, within-subjects, counterba-
lanced design (Campbell and Stanley, 1963) was used
totesteﬀectsofmonitorlocationonuserposture,visual
andmusculoskeletaldiscomfort,andpreference.Posture
measures included eye–ear line relative to horizontal
(EEL-h), neck angle relative to vertical (NA-v), trunk
angle (TA), thoracic bending (TB), and gaze angle
relativetohorizontal(GA-h;seeFig.1).Thesemeasures
were directly comparable to those in Sommerich et al.
(accepted). Screen tilt and distance were self-selected.
Duetothelargenumberofreportslinkingpsychosocial
workfactorswithvisualandmusculoskeletaldiscomfort
(BergqvistandKnave,1994;FaucettandRempel,1994;
Smithetal.,1981),thejobcontentquestionnaire(JCQ)
(Karasek et al., 1985) was administered to participants,
toaccountforpotentialconfoundingfromwork-related
stress. Work organization factors, including hours
worked per week, were also assessed.
2.2.1. Posture assessment
A video camera was placed orthogonal to the
participant’s workstation at a height matching the
center of rotation of the shoulder. Reﬂective markers
were placed at the canthus, tragus, C7, inferior wing of
the scapula, and center of rotation of the hip (Fig.1).
Marker location and camera height and distance were
measuredtoensureconsistentplacementoneachﬁlming
day. No restrictions were placed on the participants;
they were free to meet with visitors, answer the phone,
or leave their oﬃces as needed.

of the study with monitors in the alternate locations.

3. Results and discussion
The participants and conditions in which they
normally worked were favorable for the purposes of
this study. Participants were a homogenous group, all
professionals, most with graduate degrees. They per-
formed similar types of work and worked similarhours
per week (35–40h, considered full-time and average for
the company). Rest break occurrence was found to be
similar among participants, throughout the study.
Results from the JCQ showed that participants experi-
enced high job control and low psychosocial stress,
typical of other previously studied computer program-
mersintheUSA.Thisreducesthelikelihoodthatvisual
or musculoskeletal strain would be due to psychosocial
stress,andmorelikelythatstrain,ifwork-related,might
be due to workstation set-up.

3.3. Location preference
At the end of the study, participants were asked to

revealed no changes to these selections.
A number of factors are likely to inﬂuence a VDT
user’s viewing angle preference. One hypothesis regard-
ing preference is that users choose a viewing angle that
allows them to achieve reasonably low levels of visual
and musculoskeletal strain, simultaneously, while not
interfering with job performance, and within the
physicalconstraintsoftheworkstation.Table7contains
subjective preference comments from each participant,
which, in their variety, provide some support for this
hypothesis. A number of factors that could have
inﬂuenced the participantsinthis study wereexamined,
and are discussed below.
3.3.1. Visual strain
A preference for lower targets to be placed nearer
than higher targets is consistent with much of the gaze
anglepreferenceliterature,thoughMenozzietal.(1994)
found no correlation between perceived visual exertion
and viewing distance. In the current study, however,
gaze angle (GA-h) did not correlate with viewing
distance to the center of the screen, either at the
beginning (normal location) or end of the study
(preferred location). Viewing distances for the Hi and
Mid locations were 56.8cm (/C67.5) and 57.1cm
(/C67.9), respectively. Viewing distances to the top and
bottomofthescreenarediﬀerentiallyaﬀectedbyscreen
tilt. When tilt is less than the GA-h, the bottom of the
screenisfurtherawaythanthetop.Thismightbemore
likely to occur for a Mid (or lower) placement, in an
attempt to avoid glare from overhead lighting. In the
current study, however, self-selected screen tilt for the
Hi location averaged 3.18 (/C64.5) tilted back from
vertical, and was 10.18 (/C65.0) for the Mid location,
both similar to their respective GA-h. Screen tilt
averaged 108 in the Mid location, both for those who
preferred that location and for those who chose the Hi
location.
Glare from windows or overhead lights can cause
problemsforVDTusers.Inthecurrentstudy,however,
there was no pattern between the presence, absence, or
location of an oﬃce window and monitor placement
preference.Theonlytwotestsubjectswhoworkedwith
overhead lighting both preferred the Hi placement,
along with six of the twelve who worked without
overheadlighting;oneHi–HicontrolandoneMid–Mid
control also worked with overhead lighting. No
participants commented on the inﬂuence of lighting on
their choice. In summary, then, no patterns were
identiﬁed regarding the workstation factors viewing
distance, screen tilt, or glare that would demonstrate a
trend with regards to placement preference. It may be
the case, then, that monitor placement preference in
these participants reﬂected, in part, individual diﬀer-
ences in viewing angle preference. This is supported by
some of the subjective comments in Table 7, as well as
the individual and average GA–EELs in that table.
3.3.2. Musculoskeletal strain
Mann–Whitney tests were performed to examine the
inﬂuence of body part discomfort on gaze angle
preference. Only those body partsfor whichdiscomfort
wasmorefrequentlyregisteredweretested,andofthose
tested only the test of neck discomfort approached
signiﬁcance (p50.1). The test showed that the partici-
pant’slocationpreferencegenerallycorrespondedtothe
location in which he or she experienced less neck
discomfort (Table 8).
With the possible exceptions of the trends in neck
discomfort and GA–EEL, no group-level explanations
for monitor preference appeared in this data set. This
ﬁnding supports the hypothesis presented earlier,
describing preference as inﬂuenced by a number of
factors,particularlywhennoegregiousconditionsexist.
Inthepresenceofanegregiouscondition,itislikelythat
preference within a group would be homogenous.
However, when a number of factors exercise inﬂuence,
yet none dominate, a heterogeneous response is more
likely to result, as in this study. Several authors have
concluded that optimal monitor location is that which
meets the needs of the individual user (Tyrrell and
Leibowitz, 1990; Jaschinski et al., 1998; Mon-Williams
et al., 1999; Burgess-Limerick et al., 1998; Sommerich
et al., accepted). This conclusion suggests a need for
employee training in ergonomics which includes
recognition of individual responses to visual and

4. Conclusions
When considered jointly, this study and previous
research in the areas of gaze angle preference and
monitorplacementsupportlocationofthecenterofthe
monitor at mid-level or somewhat above, rather than
below, as a reasonable starting location, based on
objective and subjective research outcomes. However,
individual diﬀerences in visual capabilities (such as the
use of bifocals), physical make-up, work tasks, and
otherworkstationdesignelementssignalthatﬁnetuning
of placement is required to meet individual needs. As
such,optimalplacementforsomeindividualsmayoccur
below mid-level.
