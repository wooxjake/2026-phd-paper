Abstract
Purpose Prolonged sedentary behavior and slump sitting posture may impair neuromuscular function; however, 
acute effects on postural sway and potential differences between athletes and non-athletes remain unclear. Therefore, 
this study aims to examine the acute effects of prolonged slump sitting on postural sway in female athletes and 
non-athletes.
Methods In this study, 24 females (12 athletes, 12 non-athletes; age 18–30 years) completed balance assessments 
using the Biodex Balance System, followed by a 30-minute standardized slump sitting protocol. Outcomes included 
static postural stability test (PST), Modified Clinical Test of Sensory Interaction on Balance (mCTSIB), and limits of 
stability (LOS). Group differences were analyzed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA).
Results Prolonged slump sitting significantly influenced selected postural sway outcomes. Athletes demonstrated 
greater post-intervention sway compared with non-athletes in the Overall Stability Index (p = 0.01) and the eyes-
closed firm-surface mCTSIB condition (p = 0.02). A significant group difference was also observed in static left LOS 
performance (p = 0.01).
Conclusion In a nutshell, the present study demonstrated that prolonged slump sitting may influence postural 
sway in female athletes and non-athletes, with specific differences emerging between groups under certain sensory 
and stability conditions. By highlighting the potential impact of slump sitting on postural stability, this research 
contributes to the fields of sports science, ergonomics, and rehabilitation, emphasizing the need for strategies that 
mitigate the negative effects of prolonged sedentary behavior.

Introduction
Sedentary behavior has emerged as an essential public 
health concern, with prolonged sitting increasingly rec -
ognized as a contributor to musculoskeletal dysfunction 
[1], impaired neuromuscular control, and decreased pos -
tural stability [ 2]. Modern occupational and academic 
environments often require extended periods of static 
sitting, frequently in suboptimal postures such as slump 
sitting, a posture characterized by posterior pelvic tilt, 
thoracolumbar flexion, and forward head positioning 
[3]. Prolonged slump sitting has been shown to induce 
muscle fatigue [ 4], reduce lumbar lordosis, alter spinal 
loading patterns [5], and decrease activation of deep sta -
bilizing muscles, including the transversus abdominis 
and multifidus [ 6]. Furthermore, sustained flexed pos -
tures may influence sensorimotor integration through 
changes in mechanoreceptor sensitivity and neuromus -
cular recruitment patterns [ 7, 8]. These biomechanical 
changes may impair proprioceptive feedback from spi -
nal and pelvic structures [ 9], disrupting postural control 
strategies during subsequent standing tasks [10].
Postural control is a complex process involving the 
integration of sensory input from visual, vestibular, and 
proprioceptive systems with motor responses to main -
tain balance during static and dynamic tasks [ 11]. A 
commonly used quantitative indicator of postural con -
trol is postural sway, typically assessed through center-
of-pressure (COP) displacement, velocity, and variability 
during quiet standing [ 12]. Increased postural sway has 
been associated with impaired neuromuscular coordina -
tion [13], fatigue [14], reduced proprioceptive acuity [15], 
and elevated injury risk. In sports science, postural sway 
metrics have been widely used to evaluate injury risk, 
rehabilitation progress, and training adaptations [ 16], 
with evidence suggesting that athletes often exhibit more 
efficient balance strategies compared to non-athletes [ 17, 
18]. In athletic populations, optimal postural control is 
crucial not only for performance but also for injury pre -
vention [ 19], as deficits in balance and neuromuscular 
stability have been linked to conditions such as anterior 
cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries [ 20], ankle sprains [ 21], 
and chronic low back pain [22]. A study showed that indi-
viduals who underwent ACL reconstruction appeared to 
exhibit a persistent postural balance deficit, particularly 
in dynamic double-leg conditions [23].
Despite increasing research on sedentary behavior and 
postural control, important gaps remain. Existing stud -
ies have largely emphasized chronic musculoskeletal 
outcomes of sitting rather than the effects of prolonged 
slump sitting on postural sway [ 24]. Direct comparisons 
between athletes and non-athletes following standard -
ized sedentary postures are limited, leaving unclear 
whether athletic conditioning mitigates or amplifies tran-
sient balance disturbances. Female-specific investigations 
are also underrepresented despite known sex-related dif -
ferences in neuromuscular control [ 25], balance strate -
gies, and injury susceptibility. Furthermore, variability 
in sitting protocols, posture standardization, and sway 
assessment methods has restricted the development of 
clear recommendations that integrate ergonomic, sports 
performance, and rehabilitation perspectives. Therefore, 
the present study aimed to investigate the effects of pro -
longed slump sitting on postural sway in female athletes 
and non-athletes. It was further hypothesized that the 
effects of prolonged slump sitting on postural sway would 
be condition-specific and more pronounced under sen -
sory-challenged conditions.
Materials and methods
The present study employed a causal–comparative with 
pre-test and post-test design. Participants were recruited 
using convenience sampling from the Faculty of Sport 
Sciences and Health at the University of Tehran. Based 
on a study [ 26], 24 participants were chosen using G. 
Power software, ensuring a 0.80 test power, an effect size 
of 0.84, and a significance level of 0.05. They were divided 
into athletes (N = 12) and non-athletes’ groups (N = 12). 
Participants were eligible for inclusion if they met the fol-
lowing criteria: female sex; age between 18 and 30 years; 
body mass index (BMI) between 18.5 and 25 kg/m²; no 
history of fracture or surgical intervention involving the 
shoulder, lumbar spine, or lower extremities within the 
previous six months; absence of neurological disorders, 
stroke, or functional dysfunction in the neck or shoul -
der regions; and ability to safely perform all testing pro -
cedures. Also, the athlete group consisted of individuals 
with a minimum of three years of regular participation 
in organized sports activities, defined as structured and 
supervised training programs conducted within univer -
sity teams or sports clubs. Participants were engaged in 
various disciplines, including team sports (e.g., volleyball, 
basketball) and individual sports (e.g., athletics). All ath -
letes trained at least three sessions per week, with each 
session lasting a minimum of 90 min. The majority of 
participants competed at a recreational to university level 
rather than elite or professional levels. Participants were 
excluded if they had engaged in intense physical activ -
ity within the 24 h preceding testing, were outside the 
specified age range, demonstrated non-compliance with 
study procedures, or chose to withdraw from participa -
tion at any point during the study. Based on the Decla -
ration of Helsinki, all stages of the study were explained 
to the participants, and then written informed consent 
was obtained. Secondly, individuals were informed that 
in the event of any issues during the tests, the exam -
iner, a sports science student, would take all necessary 
actions. Also, the participants were instructed on how 
to perform the tests. Demographic and anthropometric

Procedure
The test procedure was conducted as follows: initially, 
a pre-test assessment of postural sway using the Biodex 
Balance System was performed for both groups. In the 
next stage, participants were positioned in a slump sit -
ting posture (seated on a backless chair in a flexed posi -
tion, with the hip and knee joints at 90° flexion, and a 
posture in which the angle formed between the acro -
mion, L1 vertebra, and greater trochanter deviated by 
± 3° from 93°) [ 4]. This angle was established using ana -
tomical landmarks and measured with a handheld goni -
ometer, supported by visual alignment (Fig. 1). Prior to 
the intervention, the examiner adjusted each participant’s 
posture to meet the required criteria. Participants main -
tained the slump sitting position for 30 min [ 4], after 
which post-test measurements of postural sway were 
recorded. During this period, posture maintenance was 
continuously monitored by the examiner. Participants 
were instructed to maintain the defined slump position 
and avoid postural corrections. Verbal reminders were 
provided when necessary to ensure adherence, and any 
substantial deviations from the target posture were cor -
rected immediately.
Postural sway assessment
Postural sway was assessed using the Biodex Balance 
System. All participants completed the Postural Stability 
Test (PST), Modified Clinical Test of Sensory Interaction 
on Balance (mCTSIB), and Limits of Stability (LOS) test 
according to standardized manufacturer guidelines under 
the supervision of an experienced examiner. Reported 
reliability coefficients demonstrated the Biodex Balance 
System as a valid and reliable tool for assessing pos -
tural sway ( R = 0.94) [ 27]. Prior to testing, participants 
received a detailed explanation of procedures and per -
formed familiarization trials to minimize learning effects. 
For all assessments, participants stood barefoot on the 
Biodex platform in a standardized upright posture with 
feet positioned according to the device’s coordinate grid. 
Foot placement was recorded during the initial trial and 
replicated in subsequent measurements to ensure con -
sistency between pre-test and post-test sessions. Partici -
pants were instructed to maintain an upright standing 
posture with arms alongside the body unless otherwise 
specified and to focus on the visual feedback displayed on 
the monitor [28].
Postural Stability Test (PST)
To evaluate static postural control, participants com -
pleted the PST [ 28]. They were instructed to stand in a 
double-leg stance with eyes open (DLEO) and maintain 
the platform indicator at the center of the display while 
minimizing body sway. At the beginning of the test, 
the participant’s foot position and stance width were 
recorded. During each trial, once the device was acti -
vated, participants were required to keep the platform 
as stable and horizontal as possible for 15 s, preventing 
excessive deviations and disturbances. The stability level 
of the movable platform was adjustable; in this study, it 
was set between levels 3 and 6 during double-leg stance 
trials to provide moderate instability while maintaining

Modified Clinical Test of Sensory Interaction on Balance 
(mCTSIB)
The mCTSIB was conducted to assess the contribution 
of visual and somatosensory inputs to postural control 
under different sensory conditions. Each trial required 
participants to stand quietly while maintaining stability 
on the platform for the specified test duration accord -
ing to Biodex protocols. Participants were instructed to 
remain as still as possible and avoid excessive movements 
[28]. The device recorded sway indices and postural sta -
bility performance across sensory conditions, allowing 
evaluation of sensory integration and balance control 
mechanisms. Participants completed four standardized 
conditions: Eyes open with a firm surface (EO-firm), 
Eyes closed with a firm surface (EC-firm), Eyes open with 
a foam surface (EO-foam), and Eyes closed with a foam 
surface (EC-foam).
Limits of Stability (LOS) Test
Dynamic balance and voluntary control of the center of 
gravity (COG) were evaluated using the LOS test. Par -
ticipants stood on the platform at stability level 5 with 
feet positioned according to recorded coordinates and 
arms crossed over the chest. While viewing the monitor, 
participants were instructed to shift their body weight 
and move their COG toward randomly appearing visual 
targets in multiple directions (anterior, posterior, left, and 
right). Targets appeared sequentially and unpredictably, 
requiring controlled and accurate weight shifting without 
lifting the feet [28]. Participants were asked to move their 
COG toward each target as quickly and accurately as pos-
sible. The Biodex system calculated parameters related to 
movement time, directional control, and dynamic stabil -
ity performance. All balance assessments were performed 
before and immediately after the prolonged slump sitting 
intervention under identical testing conditions to evalu -
ate changes in postural sway and stability performance.
Statistical analysis
Data analysis was performed using SPSS software (ver -
sion 27). The normality of the data distribution was 
first examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences 
between athletes and non-athletes, while controlling for 
pre-test scores (covariate), were analyzed using analy -
sis of covariance (ANCOVA). For each comparison, 
F-values, eta squared (η²) as a measure of effect size, and 
p-values were calculated. Statistical significance was set 
at p ≤ 0.05.

Discussion
The purpose of the present study was to investigate the 
effects of prolonged slump sitting on postural sway in 
female athletes and non-athletes. The main findings indi-
cated that prolonged slump sitting influenced several 
postural stability outcomes, with significant differences 
observed between groups in OSI, the EC-firm condi -
tion of the mCTSIB, and the static left LOS test. Athletes 
demonstrated higher post-intervention sway scores in 
selected conditions compared to non-athletes, suggest -
ing that prolonged slump sitting may temporarily alter 
postural control mechanisms regardless of athletic sta -
tus. However, most dynamic LOS variables and other 
mCTSIB conditions did not show significant group dif -
ferences, indicating that the effects may be task-specific 
and more evident under certain sensory or stability 
demands.

sway is consistent with studies showing that prolonged 
slumped or flexed sitting postures may contribute to neu-
romuscular fatigue and altered postural control. Jung et 
al. (2021) reported increased trunk muscle fatigue after

prolonged slumped sitting [ 4], which may negatively 
influence postural stability during subsequent standing 
tasks. Similarly, de Groot et al. (2014) found that flexed 
posture is associated with impaired postural control, sup-
porting the notion that sustained spinal flexion affects 
balance regulation [10]. However, these findings contrast 
with research suggesting that athletes generally exhibit 
lower postural sway and more efficient stability strate -
gies than non-athletes. Donath et al. (2016) observed 
improved balance performance in trained individuals 
during challenging postural tasks [ 13], while Andreeva 
et al. (2021) reported superior postural stability among 
athletes [ 14]. The discrepancy may be attributed to the 
acute fatigue and proprioceptive alterations induced by 
prolonged slump sitting, which may temporarily reduce 
the neuromuscular advantages typically observed in 
athletic populations. Several mechanisms may explain 
the increase in OSI observed after prolonged slump sit -
ting. Sustained spinal flexion may reduce lumbar lordo -
sis and alter muscle activation patterns [ 29], particularly 
decreasing the engagement of deep stabilizing muscles 
such as the transversus abdominis and multifidus [ 30]. 
This may reduce trunk stiffness and impair the abil -
ity to control COP movements during standing tasks 
[31]. Additionally, prolonged static posture may disrupt 
proprioceptive feedback [ 9], leading to decreased sen -
sorimotor integration. The higher OSI values observed 
in athletes may reflect a temporary disruption of finely 
tuned motor control systems that rely on precise neuro -
muscular coordination. Athletes often employ adaptable 
and dynamic balance strategies [ 32], and acute fatigue or 
altered sensory input may result in increased sway vari -
ability rather than rigid stabilization. Within the broader 
field, these findings highlight that acute sedentary behav -
ior may transiently affect postural control even in physi -
cally trained individuals.
Concerning the results of mCTSIB, athletes demon -
strated higher post-test sway scores compared to non-
athletes, indicating greater postural instability when 
visual input was removed. A study showed that prolonged 
slumped sitting may compromise proprioceptive input 
and postural control when visual cues are unavailable 
[33]. Similarly, Peterka (2002) highlighted the importance 
of sensorimotor integration for maintaining balance [ 8], 
suggesting that disruptions in proprioceptive feedback 
can impair stability [ 34], particularly under eyes-closed 
conditions. Conversely, previous literature has often 
shown that athletes possess superior sensory integration 
and balance performance compared to non-athletes. For 
example, Gaerlan et al. (2012) reported enhanced pos -
tural control among trained individuals under sensory-
challenged conditions [ 11], and Donath et al. (2016) 
found that athletes generally demonstrate reduced sway 
during demanding balance tasks [ 13]. The contrasting 
results in the present study may reflect the acute neuro -
muscular and sensory alterations caused by prolonged 
slump sitting, which may disrupt the sensory weighting 
strategies typically advantageous in athletes. The signifi -
cant changes observed in the EC-firm condition likely 
reflect a disruption in proprioceptive processing and 
central sensory reweighting mechanisms following pro -
longed slump sitting [35]. When visual input is removed, 
the postural control system must rely primarily on 
somatosensory and vestibular information to maintain 
stability. Sustained spinal flexion may alter afferent input 
from paraspinal muscles [36], ligaments, and joint mech -
anoreceptors [37], thereby degrading the quality of pro -
prioceptive signals reaching the central nervous system 
(CNS). Prolonged slump sitting may also induce neuro -
muscular fatigue in deep trunk stabilizers and alter feed -
forward activation patterns necessary for anticipatory 
postural adjustments [38]. Reduced activation or delayed 
recruitment of stabilizing muscles may impair the ability 
to maintain equilibrium when sensory demands increase 
[39]. Furthermore, when vision is removed, individuals 
may demonstrate increased sway due to insufficient pro -
prioceptive compensation. The greater sway observed in 
athletes may be interpreted through the aspect of motor 
control flexibility and sensory dependence. Athletes often 
develop highly refined sensorimotor strategies optimized 
for dynamic movement and rapid environmental inter -
action [ 40]. However, these systems may be more sen -
sitive to acute perturbations such as fatigue or altered 
proprioceptive input, resulting in temporary instability 
under sensory-deprived conditions. Another possible 
explanation is that athletes utilize more exploratory pos -
tural strategies [ 41], reflecting adaptive motor variability 
rather than purely reduced stability.
Additionally, a significant difference between female 
athletes and non-athletes was observed only in the 
static left LOS parameter after the intervention, sug -
gesting increased sway during voluntary weight shifting 
toward the left side. The observed changes align with 
literature indicating that prolonged flexed postures can 
disrupt trunk stability and alter directional weight-shift -
ing performance. O’Sullivan et al. (2006) demonstrated 
that variations in sitting posture may influence spinal-
pelvic alignment and trunk muscle activation patterns 
[5], potentially affecting postural control during subse -
quent standing movements. Similarly, Windhorst (2007) 
emphasized the importance of proprioceptive feedback 
from spinal musculature in guiding directional bal -
ance responses [ 9], suggesting that altered afferent input 
could impair targeted stability tasks such as LOS. How -
ever, previous research has often shown that athletes 
possess superior directional control and LOS compared 
to non-athletes. Parker et al. (2008) reported more effi -
cient balance and motor control strategies in athletic

populations [ 42], and Andreeva et al. (2021) found that 
trained individuals generally demonstrate enhanced pos -

ferences observed in the present study may reflect the 
acute neuromuscular effects of prolonged slump sitting, 
which may override typical athletic advantages by dis -

The direction-specific impairment in the static left LOS 
may reflect complex interactions between biomechani -
cal alignment, neuromuscular fatigue, and hemispheric 
motor control mechanisms following prolonged slump 
sitting [ 43]. Sustained spinal flexion may induce asym -
metric loading patterns on the pelvis and lumbar spine 
[44], potentially leading to uneven activation or fatigue of 
lateral trunk stabilizers such as the quadratus lumborum 
and oblique abdominal muscles [ 45]. Because LOS tasks 
require controlled displacement of the COG toward the 
boundaries of stability, even subtle asymmetries in mus -
cle activation or proprioceptive signaling may influence 
performance in a single direction. In addition, slump sit -
ting may alter intersegmental coordination between the 
trunk and lower extremities [ 46], which could reduce the 
efficiency of anticipatory postural adjustments necessary 
for precise lateral weight shifts. From a neurophysio -
logical perspective, prolonged static posture may dis -
rupt cortical and subcortical motor planning processes 
involved in directional balance control [47]. It is also pos-
sible that lateralized control of movement, influenced 
by limb dominance and hemispheric specialization, may 
make one direction, such as the left, more vulnerable to 
fatigue-induced motor variability [ 48]; however, these 
mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present 
study and should be interpreted with caution. The greater 
changes observed in athletes may reflect their reliance 
on high-gain neuromuscular control strategies that are 
optimized for performance but sensitive to perturbations 
[49]. When proprioceptive input is altered or stabilizing 
musculature is fatigued, athletes may exhibit increased 
movement amplitude or exploratory sway as their motor 
system attempts to recalibrate balance boundaries [ 50]. 
Within the broader framework of postural control, these 
findings suggest that acute postural stressors can impair 
directional stability [ 51], emphasizing the importance of 
symmetrical trunk function and sensorimotor integrity 
for maintaining optimal limits of stability.
The existing literature presents inconsistent findings 
regarding postural control differences between athletes and 
non-athletes, which may be explained by several moderat-
ing factors. A recent review highlighted that although ath-
letes generally demonstrate superior balance performance 
compared to non-athletes, these differences are highly 
dependent on sport-specific demands, level of expertise, 
and testing conditions [52]. For example, athletes engaged 
in disciplines requiring high levels of static or dynamic 
balance (e.g., gymnastics, shooting, or martial arts) tend 
to exhibit reduced postural sway, whereas athletes from 
other sports may not demonstrate the same advantage 
[52]. Moreover, the influence of visual input is critical; dif-
ferences between athletes and non-athletes often become 
more pronounced under eyes-closed conditions, where reli-
ance on proprioceptive and vestibular systems increases [53, 
54]. Furthermore, fatigue and acute physiological stressors 
can temporarily impair postural stability, potentially dimin-
ishing the typical balance advantages observed in athletes 
[55]. In addition, training experience and competitive level 
appear to play a critical role in postural control perfor -
mance. Higher-level athletes generally exhibit more refined 
neuromuscular control and reduced sway compared to 
lower-level or recreational athletes [56]; however, this rela-
tionship is not always consistent across studies and may 
depend on the interaction between sensory conditions and 
task complexity [52].
The findings have practical implications for athletes, stu-
dents, and individuals engaged in prolonged sedentary 
activities. From a clinical perspective, minimizing sus -
tained slump sitting and incorporating regular movement 
breaks may help maintain optimal neuromuscular function 
and postural stability. For sports practitioners and coaches, 
awareness of the potential transient balance disturbances 
following prolonged sitting may inform warm-up strategies 
and injury prevention programs. Theoretically, the study 
contributes to the growing literature emphasizing the acute 
sensorimotor consequences of sedentary postures on bal-
ance control.
Several limitations should be considered when interpret-
ing these findings. First, the relatively small sample size may 
limit the generalizability of the results and reduce statistical 
power for detecting subtle effects. Second, the use of con-
venience sampling from a single university population may 
introduce selection bias. Third, the study examined only 
acute responses to a single 30-minute slump sitting period 
and, therefore, cannot address long-term adaptations. Addi-
tionally, only female participants were included, limiting the 
applicability of findings to male populations. Furthermore, 
BMI was used as a proxy measure for body status; however, 
it does not account for body composition. Future stud -
ies should incorporate direct assessments such as body fat 
percentage or lean mass to provide a more accurate evalu-
ation, particularly in athletic populations. The athlete group 
included participants from different sport disciplines and 
competitive levels, which may have influenced postural 
control outcomes. Finally, factors such as fatigue level and 
habitual posture were not controlled in detail and may have 
influenced balance outcomes. Incorporating electromyo -
graphic or kinematic analyses in future research may clarify 
the underlying mechanisms responsible for changes in pos-
tural sway.

Conclusion
In a nutshell, the present study demonstrated that pro -
longed slump sitting may influence postural sway in 
female athletes and non-athletes, with specific differ -
ences emerging between groups under certain sensory 
and stability conditions. By highlighting the potential 
impact of slump sitting on postural stability, this research 
contributes to the fields of sports science, ergonomics, 
and rehabilitation, emphasizing the need for strategies 
that mitigate the negative effects of prolonged sedentary 
behavior.
