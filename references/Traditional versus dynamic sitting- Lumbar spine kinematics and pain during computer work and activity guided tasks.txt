Traditional versus dynamic sitting: Lumbar spine kinematics and pain 
during computer work and activity guided tasks 
Jessa M. Davidson , Jackie D. Zehr , Paolo B. Dominelli , Jack P. Callaghan
* 
Department of Kinesiology and Health Sciences, University of Waterloo, Waterloo, Ontario, Canada   
ARTICLE INFO  
Keywords: 
Active sitting 
Dynamic seat pan 
Low back pain 
ABSTRACT  
Dynamic sitting may mitigate low back pain during prolonged seated work. The current study compared pelvis 
and lumbar spine kinematics, pain, and work productivity, in traditional and dynamic sitting. Sixteen partici -
pants completed three 20-min blocks of computer work and activity guided tasks in a traditional office chair or 
backless and multiaxial rotating seat pan while kinematics were measured from accelerometers on the low back. 
Pain ratings were recorded on a visual analogue scale every 10 min. Similar pelvis and lumbar kinematics 
emerged when performing computer work in traditional and dynamic sitting. Pelvis and lumbar sagittal and 
frontal plane shifts and fidgets were largest for dynamic sitting in the activity guided tasks. Buttocks pain was 
higher in dynamic sitting, but low back pain and work productivity were unaffected. Dynamic sitting increased 
spine movement during activity guided tasks, without negatively impacting lumbar kinematics, low back pain, or 
productivity during seated computer work.   
1. Introduction 
An association between prolonged sitting and low back pain has been 
observed in both epidemiological ( da Silva et al., 2019 ; Gupta et al., 
2015 ; Hanna et al., 2019 ; Levangie, 1999 ; Lis et al., 2007 ; Mendelek 
et al., 2011 ; Park et al., 2018 ; Thorbj ¨ornsson et al., 2000 ; Van Vuuren 
et al., 2005 ) and laboratory research ( De Carvalho et al., 2020 ; O ’ Keeffe 
et al., 2013 ; O ’ Sullivan et al., 2013 ; O ’ Sullivan et al., 2006 ; Schinkel-Ivy 
et al., 2013 ; Sheahan et al., 2016 ; Vergara and Page, 2002 ; Williams 
et al., 1991 ). Sustained lumbar flexion and decreases in spine movement 
relative to other activities of daily living are potential mechanisms for 
sitting-induced low back pain. Sitting situates the spine into flexion, 
typically between 30% and 80% of the maximum voluntarily lumbar 
flexion angle (%MaxFlex) ( Beach et al., 2005 ; Callaghan and McGill, 
2001 ; Dunk and Callaghan, 2005 ; Greene et al., 2019 ). Lumbar flexion 
may lead to the perception of pain as it alters the spine loading envi -
ronment such that the posterior passive tissues of the spine are engaged 
( Callaghan and McGill, 2001) and a greater proportion of the 
compressive load is supported by the intervertebral disc ( Adams et al., 
1994 ; Adams and Hutton, 1985 ; 1980 ; Hedman and Fernie, 1997 ; 
Nachemson, 1966 ). A relative decrease in spine movement while sitting 
may also contribute to the perception of pain as spine movement is 
necessary to redistribute loads, fluid, and nutrients throughout the joint 
( Adams and Hutton, 1985 ; Huang et al., 2014 ; Nachemson, 1966 ; Wilder 
et al., 1988 ; Wilke et al., 1999 ). Specifically, those with low back pain 
often exhibit larger and/or more frequent micromovements, indicating 
that increases in spine movement may be a mechanism to alleviate or 
combat low back pain ( De Carvalho and Callaghan, 2023a ; Dunk and 
Callaghan, 2010 ; Vergara and Page, 2002 ). Taken together, in -
terventions which decrease lumbar flexion and promote movement of 
the lumbar spine in sitting may assist in reducing the risk of low back 
pain with sitting. 
Multiple interventions targeting reduced spine flexion and/or 
increased spine movement in prolonged sitting have been proposed. 
Recent popular interventions include whole-body movement or hybrid 
seating such as taking standing or walking breaks to reduce the time 
spent sitting. A systematic review noted decreases in worker pain when 
utilizing a sit-stand workstation compared to sitting only ( Karakolis and 
Callaghan, 2014 ). Unfortunately, when sit-stand workstations are 
implemented in the workplace, individuals often do not stand enough, 
such that sitting time is not significantly reduced ( Graves et al., 2015 ; 
Pronk et al., 2012 ; Riddell and Callaghan, 2021 ; Robertson et al., 2013 ; 
Wilks et al., 2006 ). Further, taking a break from sitting (e.g., 2 min of 
walking every 40 min) only temporarily relieves sitting-induced low 
back pain ( De Carvalho and Callaghan, 2023a ). As an alternative to 
sit-stand rotation, hybrid sit-stand chairs have been proposed to avoid

end-range spine flexion in sitting ( Antle et al., 2015 ; Chester et al., 2002 ; 
Ebara et al., 2008 ; Kingston et al., 2016 ; Le and Marras, 2016 ; Sartika 
and Dawal, 2010 ). However, recommendations for appropriately posi -
tioning the spine in these hybrid chairs are more recent ( Buchman- -
Pearle et al., 2023 ; Noguchi et al., 2019 ) and there is little evidence that 
simply opening up the trunk-thigh angle in hybrid chairs will elicit less 
spine flexion than a traditional 90-90-90 sitting posture. Unlike hybrid 
chairs which primarily aim to decrease spine flexion in sitting, ‘dynamic ’ 
or ‘active ’ chairs aim to decrease spine flexion while also promoting 
movement. For instance, dynamic chairs use moveable or deformable 
seat pans which could tilt forward to decrease pelvic tilt and tilt 
side-to-side to increase lateral pelvis movement. Many dynamic chairs 
variations exist, such as stability balls, traditional office chairs with a 
split seat pan, and stools with a multiaxial rotating seat pan. In a 
multiaxial rotating seat pan without a back support (like that investi -
gated currently) pelvis, hip, and trunk-thigh angles and trunk muscle 
activation were not different from a traditional office chair, but buttocks 
pain was higher ( L ´eger et al., 2022 ). Spine flexion and fidget frequency 
or amplitude were also not different between dynamic and traditional 
sitting, but low back pain remained lower in dynamic sitting ( Frey et al., 
2021 ). Dynamic sitting on a different rotating seat pan also decreased 
mean lumbar flexion by ~25 %MaxFlex with no differences in lumbar 
flexion variability or pain ratings compared to a traditional office chair 
with the seatback removed ( O ’ Sullivan et al., 2012 ). Evaluations 
comparing the different types of dynamic chairs are limited, but they 
generally demonstrate similar responses across chair types. Trunk 
muscle activation was not different between a rotating and split seat pan 
but the rotating seat pan tended to promote greater pelvis range of 
motion in the sagittal and frontal plane ( L ´eger et al., 2022 ). Trunk 
muscle activation was also similar between a stability ball and rotating 
seat pan when completing core exercises ( Holmes et al., 2015 ). Addi -
tionally, while buttocks pain increased on both types of dynamic chairs, 
buttocks pain was higher on the split seat pan design ( L ´eger et al., 2022 ). 
Collectively, it appears that dynamic sitting, facilitated by a rotating seat 
pan, has the potential to alter lumbar posture and movement, but the 
results depend on the chair being assessed. Additionally, despite that 
some rotating seat pans have the capability to promote movement in 
both the sagittal and frontal plane (i.e., seat pan tilts forward and 
backward and side-to-side), studies have not typically assessed kine -
matic changes in the frontal plane. While the opportunity for lateral tilt 
of the seat pan may increase spine movement, asymmetry in spine 
postures could also increase the risk of pain ( Marras et al., 1997 ). 
However, lateral spine and pelvis kinematics have not been investigated 
in dynamic sitting contexts. 
The purpose of this investigation was to evaluate how dynamic 
sitting altered pelvis and lumbar spine kinematics in the sagittal and 
frontal plane and perceived pain. Dynamic sitting while performing 
computer work and activity guided was facilitated by using a chair with 
a multiaxial rotating seat pan and was compared to traditional sitting. 
Seated activity guided tasks can provide an opportunity to train in -
dividuals on how to use a dynamic chair and promote additional 
movement at the workplace. While active breaks usually involve a more 
extended period away from the workstation (e.g., 30-min walk), activity 
guided tasks performed at the workstation could provide supplemental 
micro or short breaks (e.g., performed as a break or in conjunction with 
non-visual tasks like a phone call). We hypothesized that compared to 
traditional sitting, dynamic sitting would demonstrate similar pelvis and 
lumbar postures and movement in the sagittal plane ( Frey et al., 2021 ); 
however, increases in frontal plane pelvis and lumbar postures and 
movement were expected due to the increased lateral mobility of the 
seat pan compared to a traditional office chair. Additionally, it was 
hypothesized that buttocks pain would modestly increase during dy -
namic sitting ( L ´eger et al., 2022 ), but that low back pain would not be 
different ( Frey et al., 2021 ; O ’ Sullivan et al., 2012 ). Secondary objec -
tives of this study were to assess differences in trunk-thigh and hip 
flexion postures between the seats, weight distribution on the lower 
limbs versus under the seat, and work productivity.

2.3. Instrumentation 
Triaxial accelerometers (ADXL 335, Analogue Devices, Norwood, 
MA) were used as inclinometers to measure pelvis and lumbar spine 
angles. The accelerometers were affixed to the skin overlaying the first 
sacral (S1) and lumbar vertebrae (L1). Calibration trials, namely upright 
standing, maximum standing forward flexion, and maximum lateral 
bending to the right and left, were performed to normalize the flexion- 
extension and lateral bending angles with respect to the participants ’ 
range of motion. Accelerometer data were sampled at 250 Hz. 
Rigid marker clusters, each containing four infrared markers 
(Northern Digital Inc., Waterloo, ON) measured trunk-thigh and hip 
angles. Marker clusters were secured to the skin overlying the sternum 
and the lateral aspect of the thigh. A separate rigid body cluster was 
temporarily secured directly on top of the S1 accelerometer during the 
calibration trials only. A static upright standing calibration trial was 
recorded, and the proximal and distal ends of segments were digitized 
and tracked relative to the segments of interest. Functional joint trials 
were bilaterally performed to identify the position of the hip joint center 
( Camomilla et al., 2006 ; Schwartz and Rozumalski, 2005 ). The hip joint 
centers were tracked with the respective thigh segment during the 
seated conditions to facilitate construction of the pelvis segment ( Zehr 
et al., 2021 ). Marker position data were sampled at 25 Hz. 
Individuals completed pain ratings bilaterally for the upper back, 
low back, buttocks, and thighs using a 100 mm visual analogue scale 
(VAS) ( Tennant and Lab, 2023 ). A rating of 0 mm indicated “ No Pain ” 
and a rating of 100 mm indicated “ Worst Pain Imaginable ” . In each 
20-min block, pain ratings were obtained at: 0, 10, and 20 min. 
2.4. Data processing 
All kinematic data were padded ( Howarth and Callaghan, 2009 ) and 
then smoothed using a dual-pass second order low-pass Butterworth 
filter with an effective cutoff frequency of 1 Hz ( Buchman-Pearle et al., 
2021 ; Dunk and Callaghan, 2010 ; Greene et al., 2019 ). Accelerometer 
voltage data were calibrated to gravitational acceleration (g) and con -
verted to degrees using standard four-quadrant trigonometric equations. 
Pelvis anterior-posterior tilt and left and right obliquity were derived 
from the inclination of the S1 accelerometer. Lumbar flexion-extension 
and left and right lateral bending were derived from the relative incli -
nation between the S1 and L1 accelerometers. All angles were expressed 
relative to the angles from the upright standing trial and the absolute of 
the lateral angles were calculated. Further, lumbar angles were 
normalized to maximum flexion and lateral bending range of motion (i. 
e., mean of the absolute angle from the left and right maximum bending 
trials). 
Pelvis anterior-posterior tilt and obliquity and lumbar flexion- 
extension and lateral bending angles were assessed by an amplitude 
probability distribution function (APDF) for each 20-min block ( Hao -
berg and Jonsson, 1975 ). Dependent variables for statistical analyses 
were angles at the 10%, 50%, and 90% of the APDFs for pelvis 
anterior-posterior tilt and obliquity and lumbar spine flexion-extension 
and lateral bending. 
Movement of the lumbar spine in the sagittal and frontal planes were 
evaluated by calculating the shift and fidget frequency and amplitude 
using modified parameters presented by Duarte and Zatsiorsky (1999) , 
which were later adapted by Dunk and Callaghan (2010) to measure 
micromovements of the lumbar spine in sitting. Individuals with 
sitting-aggravated low back pain demonstrated more frequent shifts and 
larger shifts and fidgets, potentially as mechanism to alleviate their pain 
( Dunk and Callaghan, 2010 ). Shifts were defined when the difference in 
the average lumbar angle of two 15-s sliding windows, separated by 3-s, 
exceeded 5
◦
. The shift amplitude was calculated by the average differ -
ence in the angle of two consecutive windows throughout the whole 
shift. Fidgets were defined when the lumbar angle at a data frame 
exceeded 3 standard deviations of the 60-s sliding window centered on 
that frame, for no longer than 3-s. The fidget amplitude was calculated 
by the average difference in the angles throughout the whole fidget and 
the average of the 60-s window. The total number of shifts and fidgets 
and the mean amplitude of the shifts and fidgets in the sagittal and 
frontal plane throughout the whole block were calculated. Lumbar 
flexion-extension and lateral bending shift and fidget frequency (per 20 
min) and average amplitude were input into statistical analyses. 
Data acquired from the upright standing trial were used to construct 
segment-fixed local coordinate systems for the thorax, pelvis, and 
thighs. Given the obstructed line-of-sight to the pelvis cluster during the 
seated conditions (i.e., due to the individual ’ s close interface with the 
seatback and seat pan), a three-dimensional accelerometer-based pelvis 
was calculated using the angular deviations of the S1 accelerometer and 
a three-dimensional vector rotation approach ( Zehr et al., 2021 ). 
Trunk-thigh (thigh relative thorax) and hip (thigh relative to 
accelerometer-based pelvis) were calculated using a Z-X-Y Cardan 
sequence ( Wu et al., 2002 ). Average trunk-thigh and right and left hip 
flexion angles were input into statistical analyses. 
Force data were padded ( Howarth and Callaghan, 2009 ) and then 
smoothed using a dual-pass second order low-pass Butterworth filter 
with an effective cutoff frequency of 10 Hz ( Noguchi et al., 2019 ) and 
expressed as a percentage of body weight. The force in the vertical and 
anterior-posterior direction were computed separately to indicate if in -
dividuals tended to forward brace on their legs.

2.5. Statistical analysis 
Statistical analyses were performed on the trunk-thigh, hip, pelvis, 
and lumbar spine kinematics, force, pain, and productivity variables to 
determine the effects of block (within-subject factor: Traditional, Dy -
namic, and/or Activity) and sex (between-participant factor: male or 
female). Sex was investigated as a factor because differences in seated 
kinematics emerged between males and females ( De Carvalho and 
Callaghan, 2023a ; Dunk and Callaghan, 2005 ; Gregory et al., 2006 ). 
When applicable, the effect of time (within-subject factor: pain = 3 in -
stances in each block) and side (within-subject factor: hip = left and 
right) were also evaluated. Specifically, mixed model analyses of vari -
ance (ANOVA) were performed at an alpha level of 0.05. When signif -
icance was achieved, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed 
with a Bonferroni correction. All statistical tests were completed in SPSS 
(Version 28.0.1, IBM Corporation, Somers, New York, USA).

There were no significant main effects or interactions for block or sex

4. Discussion 
Although lumbar spine kinematics and pain were similar when 
performing office work in traditional and dynamic sitting, performing

the frontal plane ( Figs. 2 and 3 ; Table 1 ). The second hypothesis was also 
supported as buttocks pain was higher during dynamic sitting, but no 
differences in low back pain existed between traditional and dynamic 
sitting ( Fig. 5 ). Collectively, these findings indicate that compared to

Pelvis and lumbar spine postures and movement were typically not 
different across the chairs and activities. Dynamic sitting resulted in 
modest decreases in posterior pelvic tilt, yet no differences in lumbar 
flexion angles were observed. Mean lumbar flexion was 62 %MaxFlex 
during simulated office work and 69 %MaxFlex during the activity 
guided tasks ( Fig. 3 ). These findings are consistent with the seat pan 
being able to tilt anteriorly ( De Carvalho and Callaghan, 2023b ) and are 
similar to lumbar flexion angles previously observed with dynamic 
sitting on a multiaxial rotating seat pan ( Frey et al., 2021 ). Additionally, 
the lack of change in lumbar spine angles is consistent with suggestions

not different between the chairs during the seated computer work, 
wherein mean lateral bending angles of 16 %MaxBend were observed 
for each of the three blocks ( Fig. 4 ). These angles correspond with lateral 
angles during seated computer work ( Davidson et al., 2024 ). Only when 
performing activity guided tasks on the dynamic chair did increases in 
peak lateral angles for the pelvis and lumbar spine occur, indicating 
increases in total lateral range of motion during the activity guided tasks 
( Fig. 4 ). Taken together, these findings indicate that unless prompted, 
motion of the pelvis and lumbar spine is similar between traditional and 
dynamic sitting. Although the dynamic chair provided the opportunity 
for spine motion in the sagittal and frontal plane, it did not increase 
lumbar flexion or introduce asymmetrical postures greater than the of -
fice chair when performing computer work. The lack of differences in 
lumbar spine flexion and lateral bending angles may represent a positive 
finding for this type of dynamic chair as awkward or non-neutral spinal 
postures are associated with an increased risk of spine injury in ergo -
nomic applications, particularly manual material handling tasks ( Marras 
et al., 1997 ). Moreover, asymmetrical postures in sitting have been 
linked to alterations in seat pressure distribution which could contribute 
to buttocks or low back pain ( Al-Eisa et al., 2013 ; Viggiani et al., 2014 ). 
On the other hand, this data also indicates that larger forward and 
backwards rotation of dynamic chairs may be necessary if the goal of the 
chair is to decrease flexed spine postures. 
Similar to the findings for lumbar spine postures, lumbar spine 
movement was largely consistent between traditional and dynamic 
sitting. The number of shifts and fidgets were not different between 
traditional and dynamic sitting during computer work, but larger lateral 
fidgets were observed in dynamic sitting ( Table 1 ). Larger shifts and 
fidgets were also observed during the activity guided tasks compared to 
the computer work conditions ( Table 1 ). Surprisingly, the number of 
shifts and fidgets were not different or less during the activity guided 
tasks; however, this may be related to the thresholds used in calculating 
these metrics. The shift and fidget dynamic metrics were initially 
designed to capture movement in a traditional non-rotating seat pan 
( Dunk and Callaghan, 2010 ). It is possible that movement in dynamic 
sitting was not captured by these metrics as the dynamic chair could 
have facilitated more rapid or smaller movements than those necessary 
to trigger a fidget or shift. For instance, the fidget movement threshold is 
based on 3 standard deviations of the movement range and would have 
been more difficult to achieve on the dynamic chair as there was more 
variability in participant movement. Greater variability in lumbar 
movement during the activity guided tasks is supported by the increases 
in lumber range of motion ( Figs. 3 and 4 ) and large shift and fidget 
amplitudes ( Table 1 ). Nevertheless, implementing activity guided tasks 
on a dynamic chair is likely an effective tool for increasing movement at 
the workplace. Similar to recommendations to rotate between sitting 
and standing and taking breaks throughout the workday, the current 
findings indicate that performing activities on a dynamic chair encour -
ages movement of the spine. Moreover, many individuals report low 
back pain with prolonged standing ( Gallagher et al., 2011 ; Nelson-Wong 
and Callaghan, 2014 ), while the current findings, along with other an -
alyses have not shown significant increases in low back pain develop -
ment with dynamic sitting ( Frey et al., 2021 ; L ´eger et al., 2022 ; 
O ’ Sullivan et al., 2012 ). 
Indeed, pain of the upper back, low back, and thighs remained low in 
both traditional and dynamic sitting, but buttocks pain was highest for 
dynamic sitting. Specifically, 6 of 16 participants reported meaningful 
increases (i.e., > ~10 mm ( Gallagher et al., 2014 ; Gallagher and Call -
aghan, 2015 ; H ¨agg et al., 2003 ; Nelson-Wong and Callaghan, 2010 ; 
Schinkel-Ivy et al., 2013 )) in the buttocks (3 females; 1 in Dynamic, 1 in 
Activity, and 1 in both) or low back (3 males; 1 in Dynamic, 1 in Activity, 
and 1 in both) during dynamic sitting. Higher reports of buttocks pain 
were observed for dynamic sitting in general, but significant differences 
were only observed between traditional sitting and the activity guided 
tasks ( Fig. 5 ). Buttocks pain in the activity was likely because partici -
pants were required to apply force to the seat pan to move their pelvis. 
Additionally, increases in vertical and anterior shear forces in dynamic 
sitting, particularly during the activity guided tasks ( Table 2 ), also 
indicate increases in lower limb involvement to resist sliding forward 
( Noguchi et al., 2019 ). Subsequent friction from the seat pan could have 
also contributed to buttocks pain. Pain may also be higher during the 
activity guided tasks as they were always completed last (i.e., a cumu -
lative total of 40-min on the dynamic chair with only short breaks be -
tween) and participants were required to complete the activities for 
20-min to ensure time equalization between the three blocks. In prac -
tice, the activity guided tasks would be recommended over shorter du -
rations ( < 10 min) to facilitate training individuals how to use the 
dynamic chair, for micro or short breaks during the workday, or when 
performing non-visual tasks like taking a phone call. Completing activity 
guided tasks at the workstation are not meant to replace active breaks (e. 
g., going for a walk), but rather provide additional opportunities for 
movement at the workplace. Further, implementing accommodation 
training when starting to use a dynamic chair may be effective for 
mitigating pain over time ( Jackson et al., 2013 ). 
There are limitations to be acknowledged. First, the activity guided 
tasks were designed to elicit motion of the pelvis on a dynamic chair and 
are not possible on a traditional office chair. Without the activity guided 
tasks on a traditional office chair, a factorial design (i.e., chair × task ) 
was not possible. Additionally, the applicability of the current findings 
are limited as a dynamic chair, coupled to a phone, is required to 
complete these specific activity guided tasks. Second, we assessed 20- 
min blocks, which is well below what would be typical of an entire 
workday or would be likely to occur during a single bout of sitting before 
a break ( Matthews et al., 2008 ; Parry and Straker, 2013 ). It is possible 
that with more prolonged bouts of seated work, pain ratings could 
continue to rise ( De Carvalho and Callaghan, 2023a ; Gallagher and 
Callaghan, 2015 ). However, as indicated by values of approximately 0 
mm at the start of each block ( Fig. 5 ), pain recovered quickly to those at 
the start of the collection during the short transitions between the 
20-min blocks. Lastly, only young and healthy participants, with a small 
variation in anthropometry were examined. The homogeneity amongst 
participants may have contributed to the lack of significant differences 
which existed between males and females. 
Overall, dynamic sitting, facilitated by a multiaxial rotating seat pan, 
while performing computer work did not result in large changes to pelvis 
and lumbar postures and movement or work productivity. Although 
upper back, low back, and thigh pain remained low during both tradi -
tional and dynamic sitting, buttocks pain tended to be higher during 
dynamic sitting, particularly in the activity guided tasks. The current 
findings indicate that introducing forward/backwards and lateral 
movement of the seat pan can occur without negatively impacting low 
back kinematics, pain, or productivity. Further, a dynamic chair facili -
tates the performance of activity guided tasks which appear to be an 
effective means of increasing spine movement at a user ’ s workstation.
