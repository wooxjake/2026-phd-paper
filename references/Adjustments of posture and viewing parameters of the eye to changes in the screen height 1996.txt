                                                                    To understand the motions caused by the interaction among the different body
                                                                    parts, adjustments of both eye position and body posture to screen height settings
                                                                    of 80, 90, 100, 110 and 120cm were determined in 10 subjects. The subjects
                                                                    engaged in a non-keyboard, interactive computer game requiring constant visual
                                                                    monitoring. Changes in body positions were determined by video image analysis
                                                                    for the posture parameters and by video frame analysis for the eye parameters.
                                                                    Analysis of variance and correlation analysis showed that neck angle, thoracic
                                                                    bending and vertical eye position were significantly affected by changes in the
                                                                    screen height of the VDT. The study of the interrelationship of eye position and
                                                                    body posture suggested that changes in body positions sewed to complement the
                                                                    eye position in attaining a better view of the visual target. Viewing angle was
                                                                    observed to be decided mainly by inclination of the neck and the eye. Thoracic
                                                                    bending was also noted to contribute in setting the viewing angle, although to a
                                                                    lesser extent. On the other hand, viewing distance and trunk inclination showed
                                                                    significant correlation.

                                                                                                     1. Introduction
                                                             The introduction of visual display terminals (VDT) in the workplace has led to
                                                             numerous reports of health disorders from its use. The disorders are predominantly
                                                             referable to the eye and the musculoskeletal system (Hiinting el al. 1981, Ardnt 1983,
                                                             Knave et al. 1985, Gobba et al. 1988, Bergqvist et al. 1995). Factors believed to ,
                                                             contribute to the development of these disorders include static position assumed
                                                             during work, increased work intensity and improper workstation design. As a
                                                             consequence, the spawning of many guidelines aimed at preventing adverse effects
                                                             in workers using visual display terminals were observed.

                                                                 Recommendations for visual comfort favour a downward eye inclination thereby
                                                             advocating workstation setting below the level of the eye (Kroemer and Hill 1986,
                                                             Heuer et al. 1991, Taptagaporn and Saito 1993). Workstation designs were proposed
                                                             to offset musculoskeletal problems (Grandjean 1984, Cakir el al. 1980). These
                                                             guidelines, however, apply to conventional VDT workstations where the CRT
                                                             screen is used in an office setting or in the performance of a desktop task.

                                                                 The increasing popularity of various screen sizes and height locations compounds
                                                             the dilemma of posture and eye position during work. The recent advent of large-size

                                                             symbols, may induce an upward gaze and a more erect body posture. Similar body
                                                             positions of workers may be noted in traffic control centres and nuclear power
                                                             facilities because of the upward location of the screen. The use of flat panel displays

                                                                 The integration of the VDT in many workplaces creates a new dimension to the
                                                             increasing number of ergonomic concerns with VDT use. Constrained postures are
                                                             imposed among VDT users performing tasks that require sustained visual monitoring
                                                             (Hiinting et al. 1980)such as in vigilance tasks observed in air traffic controls, medical
                                                             and industrial monitoring, and military target spotting (Craig and Davies 1991). The
                                                             introduction of various non-keyboard input devices, e.g. mice, pucks and joysticks, in
                                                             workstations for graphic and engineering designs and programming may further
                                                             restrict free movement among VDT users. Further, VDT tasks, while using these non-
                                                             keyboard input devices such as pointing, dragging and selection, require more
                                                             concentrated fixation of the operator's eyes on the screen that would often result in
                                                             constrained postures.

                                                                 To better understand the outcomes of VDT use, it is vital to comprehend how the
                                                             different body parts adapt to a complex problem. It is the aim of this study to
                                                             determine the ergonomic problems in VDT work with non-keyboard input devices.
                                                             Postural reaction to changes in the height of the screen while performing a non-
                                                             keyboard task was determined. Body and eye positions affected by screen height
                                                             modifications were ascertained. Mutually related body and eye movements were also
                                                             identified.

                                                                                               2. Subjects and method

                                                             2.1. Subjects
                                                             Ten subjects (8 male and 2 female) participated in the experiment. Mean age was
                                                             36.1 f 8.8 years. Mean height was 168.8 f 5.5cm.

                                                             2.2. Experimental workstation
                                                             The selection of the five screen height settings, i.e., 80,90, 100, 110and 120cm, used in
                                                             the experiment was based on the recommended table height of 67cm (Japanese
                                                             Industrial Standard S1010-1978 1993). The most prevalent CRT size of 14in. was
                                                             selected for the experiment. The central processing unit (CPU) and the CRT monitor
                                                             placed on top of a table of height 67cm resulted in a screen height of 100cm (from the
                                                             floor to the middle of the screen). Change of screen height was made at 10-cm
                                                             increments.

                                                                 To better evaluate the interaction between the screen and the operator, the
                                                             mouse table height was fixed. The table height upon which the mouse rests was
                                                             constant for all subjects at 67cm. The mouse table is detached from the adjustable
                                                             screen table.

                                                                 Prior to the start of the experiment, the subjects were asked to adjust the chair
                                                             until the forearm was parallel to the ground when handling the mouse. This forearm
                                                             position prevents extreme hand and arm posture responsible for local musculoskeletal
                                                             discomfort (Chaffin and Andersson 1991, Hagberg et al. 1995).The back support was
                                                             adjusted to desired levels. Thereafter, the chair setting remained unchanged through-
                                                             out the experiment. These experimental conditions are considered to be similar to the
                                                             actual VDT workstations where larger or smaller sizes of screens were used on
                                                             traditional desks.

                                                                 The illumination levelswere 230 lx on the mouse table and 120lx on the CRT. The
                                                             luminance level of the brightest area of the screen was 79cd/m2 while that of the
                                                             darkest area was. 11cd/m2.

Downloaded by [University of Sydney] at 00:17 02 April 2013  2.3. Task
                                                             The subjects were asked to engage in an interactive computer game. The game
                                                             required constant visual monitoring-similar to a vigilance task-to detect signals
                                                             that would determine the operator's next action. T o purely extract the effects of the
                                                             screen height on posture and eye position and to highlight human-screen interaction,
                                                             only the mouse was used as the input device in this experiment. Moreover, keyboard
                                                             operation sometimes requires human-keyboard interaction that may confound the
                                                             analysis.

                                                                 The subjects performed the VDT task for 3 min at each screen height to allow
                                                             adaptation to the workstation. Posture and eye position were evaluated at the end of
                                                             the allotted time.

                                                                 Results of long duration posture evaluation of 4 out of the 10 subjects provided a
                                                             basis for the observation period in this experiment. Under the same experimental
                                                             conditions as in the present study, the posture of the subjects was observed while
                                                             performing the task for 50 min (Japan Industrial Safety and Health Association 1985)
                                                             at the different screen height settings. The body positions measured were the same as
                                                             those described in 82.4. The mean coefficient of variation of the different body
                                                             positions evaluated during the long duration experiment was 3.1% (SD = 2.4%;
                                                             range = 0.7-9%).

                                                                 Rest was introduced in between height changes. The subjects were also asked to
                                                             stand away from the experimental workstation in between height changes.

                                                                     Reid's line is defined by a line connecting the outer canthus of the eye and
                                                                     the centre of the outer canal of the ipsilateral ear (figure 2). This study used the

                                                                     making its determination by video image difficult. Earlier findings showed that
                                                                     the Reid's line is approximately 10" above the Frankfurt plane.
                                                                 2. Thoracic bending: angle formed by C7, angulus inferior scapula and iliac crest.
                                                                    A smaller angle corresponds to a relatively more kyphotic spine.
                                                                 3. Trunk inclination: angle formed by C7 and iliac crest referenced against the
                                                                     horizontal plane. Reclining positions would assume a value of more than 90".
                                                                 4. Pelvic angle: angle formed by C7, iliac crest and knee joint. The angle
                                                                     corresponds to the degree of hip flexion.

                                                             (Sotoyama el al. 1995)were estimated by measuring the palpebral fissure width from
                                                             the video image of the eye. Correlation between palpebral fissure width and both
                                                             vertical gaze direction and ocular surface area have been established to be strong with
                                                             correlation coefficients greater than 0.95. The viewing angle is defined as the angle
                                                             formed by the horizontal plane and the line between the eye and the visual target.
                                                             Contributions of both neck inclination and eye inclination were taken into account to
                                                             determine the viewing angle (figure 2). Viewing distance was determined by measuring
                                                             the distance between the marker at the level of the middle of the screen and that on the
                                                             forehead.

                                                                                                         3. Results
                                                             3.1. Body posture and screen height
                                                             Results showed that neck angle and thoracic bending were significantly affected by
                                                             changes in screen height (table 1). Mean levels of neck angle at different screen heights
                                                             demonstrated that the neck became more erect as the screen position became higher.
                                                             The trend in the bending of the trunk similarly showed that the back straightened as
                                                             the screen height was raised.

                                                                 Trunk inclination and the pelvic angle did not significantlychange with the change
                                                             in the screen height. None the less, it is interesting to note that the mean trunk
                                                             inclination at all screen height settings showed that the subjects preferred a backward-
                                                             leaning position. A schematic representation was made to understand better the
                                                             interaction of the body positions and viewing angle at different height settings
                                                             (figure 3). The results of the changes in viewing angle will be discussed in the next
                                                             section.

                                                                 Comparison of the correlation coefficients suggested a stronger correlation
                                                             between neck angle and screen height (r = 0.740; slope = 0.566) than between the
                                                             latter and thoracic bending ( r = 0-568; slope = 0.162) (figures 4 and 5). The neck
                                                             moved more and was seemingly more vulnerable as the screen height was changed.

                                                                                                       4. Discussion
                                                             The adaptation of individuals to changes in the workstation dimension will involve
                                                             multiple movement of the anatomical elements. Lowering of the screen height of the
                                                             VDT has brought about the lowering of the vertical gaze direction, decrease in ocular
                                                             surface area and increase in neck flexion. The appropriate degree of downward eye
                                                             position may seem favourable from the viewpoint of eye comfort. Yaginuma et al.
                                                             (1990) and Tsubota and Nakamori (1993) reported that elevated viewing angle in
                                                             VDT work resulted in reduced blinking rate and that the widening of the ocular

                                                             unwanted consequences. Stress on the cervical spine is found to be higher in a flexed
                                                             head as compared to a head assuming a more vertical position (Colombini et al. 1986,
                                                             Schiildt et al. 1986). Fatigue also ensues earlier as the neck increases flexion (Chaffin
                                                             1973).

                                                                 The relationship between the screen height and the trunk posture is also evident.
                                                             The thoracic bending increased as the screen height was lowered. Throughout the
                                                             experiment, the subjects were observed to assume a backward-leaning of the trunk.
                                                             The meaning of these results, however, is not clear in view of an obscured relationship
                                                             between back posture and discomfort. An erect sitting posture was associated with
                                                             decreased discomfort (Sauter et al. 1991). .On the other hand, EMG studies of the
                                                             erector spinae muscle of the back showed that muscle activity was greater in an erect
                                                             spine, suggesting more stress, than in a bent spine (Colombini et al. 1986). Moreover,
                                                             a reclining posture is considered to reduce strain on the vertebral disc and back
                                                             muscles (Andersson and Ortengreen 1974).

                                                                 Direct comparison of parameters measured in this study to those measured by
                                                             other authors cannot be made considering the differences in methodology. None the
                                                             less, the relationship of the variables in this experiment is clear. The position of the eye
                                                             is inevitably associated with a change in the neck angle and thoracic bending.
                                                             Proponents of guidelines for downward vertical gaze direction will have to consider
                                                             the potential burden on the neck and on the spine.

                                                                 The trunk position maintained a backward-leaning position as the screen height
                                                             was varied. The pelvic angle, likewise, was noted to remain almost unchanged. The
                                                             seemingly immobile trunk and pelvis were also observed during VDT work in both
                                                             experimental and actual work setting (Nakaseko et al. 1993, Miyamoto et al. 1995).
                                                             Several factors may account for this constrained posture. The mouse table height and
                                                             chair height were fixed for the duration of the experiment thereby imposing a constant
                                                             work environment for the lower body. Another factor is the nature of the task. The
                                                             game required constant visual monitoring by the subjects to be able to respond
                                                             appropriately to the signal given by the computer. Further, the mouse was the only
                                                             tool used as an input device of the computer. Handling of the mouse relied solely on
                                                             tactile and spatial perception that eliminated the need to look down towards the
                                                             mouse table. These factors present in this experimental situation may have produced
                                                             visual fixation towards the screen. Body movements were simplified and concentrated
                                                             only on the right hand, precluding the need to alter trunk and pelvic positions. Hence,
                                                             after the preferred viewing angle has been decided and the corresponding neck
                                                             position has been assumed, the posture of the trunk and pelvis were held almost
                                                             constant.

                                                                 The correlation matrix gave an important perspective concerning the relationship
                                                             of body parts as they move when the workstation dimension is changed. From the
                                                             results, posture is apparently decided by the demand on the visual system. Although
                                                             no correlation between trunk inclination and screen height exists, an understanding of
                                                             how the trunk functions to stabilize the viewing distance is evident. The neck appears
                                                             to be the most susceptible and most important in adjusting its position to find the
                                                             preferred viewing angle. The outcome of the experiment suggests that the neck
                                                             inclination supports eye inclination especially at higher screen heights. Studies on eye
                                                             physiology have demonstrated that the downward gaze leads to visual comfort during
