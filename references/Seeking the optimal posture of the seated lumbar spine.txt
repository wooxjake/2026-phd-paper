Seeking the optimal posture of the seated lumbar
spine
Jenny Pynt, Joy Higgs, and Martin Mackey
Prolonged sitting with poor posture is associated with the development of lower
back pain. Ergonomic texts for physiotherapists contain diverging and confusing
views on recommended postures for the lumbar spine when seated that will
promote postural health and optimal functioning of the lumbar spine. A review of
the literature reveals that proponents of both the lordosed and kyphosed lumbar
seated position use similar arguments with contradictory conclusions. The
arguments of those advocating the kyphosed lumbar seated posture are, however,
often anecdotal and unsubstantiated by research. This paper evaluates the
con/bullet6 icting views and concludes that the lordosed seated posture, regularly
interspersed with movement, is the optimal sitting posture and assists in
maintaining lumbar postural health and preventing low back pain.
INTRODUCTION
An important goal in physiotherapy is to foster
health and prevent injury. This paper deals
with the postural health of the lumbar spine
and the positioning of the spine to promote
optimal functioning in chair sitting. Two ways
to address this goal are to educate healthy indi-
viduals and re-educate people with disorders
related to poor sitting posture regarding the
bene/bullet5 cial effects of optimal sitting postures
on the spine. This is especially important for
people with low back dysfunction or those with
largely sedentary lifestyles.
To explore this topic, it is /bullet5 rst necessary
to clarify what is meant by posture, seated

The Posture Committee of the American
Academy of Orthopaedic Surgery ( 1947, cited
in Cailliet, 1981, p. 23 ) de/bullet5 ned healthy posture
as ‘‘skeletal alignment re/bullet5 ned as a relative
arrangement of the parts of the body in a
state of balance that protects the supporting
structures of the body against injury or
progressive deformity.’’ In sitting, there is no
one ideal posture, nor should one posture be
sustained. Healthy sitting posture, therefore,
is best thought of as an active, not a static
phenomenon. In the resting position, between
movements, healthy sitting posture can be
thought of as occurring when unnecessary
( static ) muscle activity, ligamentous tension,
intradiscal pressure, and zygapophysial joint
forces are minimized, and when the body
weight is distributed evenly through the ischial
tuberosities and thighs to the seat and through
the torso via the backrest.
Excluded from co nsideration in this paper
are healthy seated postures for those with bony
or soft tissue anomalies or diseases, particularly
spondylolisthesis and canal stenosis. Here we
describe the effects of seating on the lumbar
spine; only brief consideration, where appro-
priate, is given to the cervical spine. To ensure
adequate review of the literature, we have
reviewed studies from many disciplines. In view
of the lack of well-designed epidemiological
clinical trials or systematic reviews investigating
optimal seated posture, it has been necessary
to rely largely on studies with lower levels of
evidence ( e.g., cadaveric studies ).
ACHIEVING HEALTHY LUMBAR
SITTING POSTURE
To understand the co ncept of healthy sitting
posture it is necessary to consider the effects
of sitting on the intervertebral disc ( IVD ),
the zygapophysial joints, and the muscles and
ligaments of the lumbar spine. It also is essential
to consider the importance of movement, the
behavior of the sitter and the chair’s role in
healthy sitting. These aspects are summarized
in Table 1.
In summary, healthy seated postures are
those that lower intradiscal pressure, minimize
creep, minimize unnecessary myoelectrical
activity in the posterior lumbar musculature
and the least tension in posterior ligaments,
and encourage load-sharing between the IVD
and zygapophysial joints. As noted in Table 1,
creep is de/bullet5 ned as the phenomenon in which
deformation of a tissue increases with time
when the load is held constant ( Frankel &
Nordin, 1980 ). No one posture is correct, and
movement is essential to facilitate nutrition and
relieve muscle fatigue. Finally, the behavior
of the sitter must be co nsidered, in order to
address user education.
Educating the sitter in healthy seated
postures and optimizing chair use is an essen-
tial part of treatment for the sedentary wo rker
with back pain. Although a lack of ef/bullet5 cacy
of education alone has been demonstrated by
Daltroy et al ( 1997 ), such results do not relieve
the physiotherapist from the onus of improving
the standard of education and understanding,
while recognizing the limitations. Further, the
literature co ntains co n/bullet6 icting opinions as to
the correct seated lumbar posture, creating
dif/bullet5 culty for the physiotherapist wishing to
educate a patient. Advice on the ‘‘correct’’
sitting posture has not always been substanti-
ated by research, and conclusions from research
have not always been logical. The question
therefore remains: Which models of seated
posture ful/bullet5 ll the criteria for seated postural
health?

Effect of the kyphosed sitting
posture on IVD pressure
The intradiscal pressure at the level of the
third lumbar vertebra is increased by 40%
during unsupported slumped sitting with the
lumbar spine in kyphosis, when compared
with intradiscal pressure in erect standing.

subject leans forward during sitting while the
spine remains in kyphosis ( Nachemson, 1976 ).

Intradiscal pressure is further increased

The effect of the /bullet6 exed sitting posture

sitting position, the zygapophysial joints are

Those who advocate the /bullet6 exed sitting
posture acknowledge that an increase in
intradiscal pressure occurs in this position, but
consider this increase suf/bullet5 ciently small to be
ineffective in creating disc damage ( Adams
& Hutton, 1985; Dolan, Adams, & Hutton,

sure ranging from 0.8MPa and 1.49MPa have
been demonstrated in upright unsupported
sitting ( Nachemson & Morris, 1964 ) and an
increase in intradiscal pressure within this
range, sustained over a period of hours, has
been shown to be deleterious to the IVD of
mice, ultimately promoting disc degeneration
( Lotz et al, 1998 ). These authors demonstrated
that static co mpressive loads alter the biolog-
ical activity of the mouse disc and result in
decreased biomechanical performance, irre-
versible disorganization in the anulus, and cell
death. The /bullet6 uid forced out of mouse discs by
a sustained spinal co mpression of 0.8 MPa over
one week decreased nucleus volume and led
to a loss of tensile stress in the inner anulus,
accompanied by architectural breakdown in the
inner and middle anulus. A correlation also was
demonstrated between cell death in the nucleus
and inner anulus and sustained mechanical
loading in the IVD. There is a critical point in
loading ( 1.3 MPa ) where suf/bullet5 cient cell death
has occurred to prevent the disc recovering
when loading is removed. A constant state of
compression in the anulus then occurs that
alters the biological activity of the disc.
It should be noted that there are several
limitations in extrapolating results from mouse
studies to humans. Notochordal cells present in
the mouse disc are not seen in the adult human;
growth plates in the mouse vertebral end plates
are not found in adult humans, mice lack
laminae, laminar processes, and zygapophysial
joints ( Lotz et al, 1998 ), and the disc surface
area is obviously smaller. However, the data
gathered by Lotz and coauthors demonstrated
that IVD degeneration was induced in mouse
discs by sustained compressive loading alone, at
pressures similar to those achieved in unsup-
ported sitting, and that these degenerative
changes were the same as those seen in humans.
This evidence provides some support for the
theory that a deleterious effect can be triggered
when a human disc is consistently compressed
above resting loads, such as occurs in the /bullet6 exed
sitting posture.

An in –vitro study by Hedman and Fernie

response of zygapophysial joints and IVDs to
vertical creep deformation and intervertebral
force transmission in the /bullet6 exed and extended

they also reported /bullet6 exed sitting postures
created large increases in the tensile forces
in the posterior anulus, which did not occur
in extended sitting postures. These potentially
damaging forces were due to the signi/bullet5 cantly
increased compressive loads and shear force

combined with excessively high force escalation
in the posterior ligaments as a result of creep-
related loading in the /bullet6 exed sitting posture. A
compressive load in the /bullet6 exed sitting lumbar

height by an average of 2.1 mm ( Adams &
Hutton, 1983; Adams, McNally, & Dolan 1996;
McMillan, Garbutt, & Adams, 1996 ), narrowing
the intervertebral foramen ( Adams & Hutton,

the /bullet6 exed sitting position also leads to a
decrease in /bullet6 uid co ntent in all parts of the
disc except the posterior 2 mm of the anulus
( McMillan et al, 1996 ). This loss of /bullet6 uid creates
a severely dehydrated disc ( McMillan et al,
1996 ) and consequently decreases nutrition
and accelerates disc degeneration ( Handa et al,
1997; Ohshima et al, 1989 ).

nutrition is one of the arguments used by those
favoring /bullet6 exed sitting postures. In cadaveric
motion segment studies Adams and Hutton
( 1983, 1985 ) found that cadaveric discs that
were continuously loaded to simulate sustained
/bullet6 exed sitting exhibited more creep than those
loaded to simulate erect sitting. From this they
concluded that /bullet6 exed sitting positions increase
/bullet6 uid /bullet6 ow. This can aid the nutrition of the

/bullet6 ow, but sustained kyphotic positions in sitting

dehydrated disc ( McMillan, Garbutt, & Adams,
1996 ). Therefore, the argument of Adams and
Hutton ( 1983, 1985 ) may be /bullet6 awed as well
as functionally invalid, if one considers the

high compressive load on the posterior /bullet5 bers

and increases nutrition, thereby decreasing the
degenerative changes that lead to back pain
( Adams & Hutton 1980, 1985; Fahrni, 1975;

Empirical research has shown that while
the /bullet6 exed sitting posture may decrease poste-
rior anulus compression and unload the
zygapophysial joints, it also serves to increase
anterior anulus load ( Horst & Brinckmann,
1981 ) and posterior anulus tensile forces.
This increased intradiscal load, combined with
deformation of the disc and increased liga-
mentous tensioning, causes a rise in intradiscal
pressure, with resultant increase in IVD creep
and decrease in nutrition. If such co mpres-
sion is sustained, it may ultimately contribute
to disc degeneration and subsequent pain.
Further, although the lordosed sitting posture
may load the zygapophysial joints, radiolog-
ical evidence shows that IVD degeneration
precedes zygapophysial degeneration ( Butler
et al, 1990 ) . Degeneration of the IVD then
creates mechanical alterations in the loading
of the zygapophysial joints and may be respon-
sible for their subsequent degeneration ( Butler
et al, 1990 ).
It would appear that sitting postures
that lower the intradiscal pressure are more
conducive to spinal health than those postures
that directly unload the zygapophysial joints at
the expense of raised intradiscal pressure. We
can therefore conclude that sustained main-
tenance of the /bullet6 exed sitting posture is not
conducive to postural health.
Lordosed sitting posture
Opposing the proponents of the kyphotic
sitting posture are those who promote the
maintenance of some degree of lumbar lordosis
during sitting. The natural curve in the lumbar
spine is known as the lordosis and is the concave
curve formed between the superior surface of
the /bullet5 rst lumbar vertebra ( L1 ) and the superior
surface of the sacrum ( see Fig. 1, angle 1 ).
This curve varies between individuals and is
attributable to a greater height anteriorly in
lumbar IVDs than posteriorly, a similar wedge
shape in the /bullet5 fth lumbar vertebra ( L5 ), and
an inclination of each of the vertebrae superior
to L5 ( Bogduk & Twomey, 1987 ). In upright
sitting with a lumbar support of 4 cm, the
lumbar lordosis averages 47 ° ( Adams & Hutton,
1985 ). When the sacral horizontal angle ( see
Fig. 1, angle 2 ) is more than 16 ° the pelvis
rotates anteriorly. The downward slope of
the superior surface of the sacrum requires
a compensatory angulation in the lumbar
vertebrae to maintain an upright posture. This
is achieved by the wedged shape of both the
lumbo-sacral disc and the L5 vertebral body
and the inclination of the lumbar vertebral
bodies that gradually changes, so that in the
erect posture, the L1 vertebra lies vertically
above the sacrum ( Schoberth, 1962 ). Those
favoring maintenance of lordosis in sitting
argue that kyphotic sitting postures increase
ligamentous stress, while lordotic postures
bene/bullet5 t spinal health by decreasing intradiscal
pressure, increasing nutrition, and decreasing
disc degeneration ( Andersson, ¨Ortengren,
Nachemson, & Elfstr ¨om, 1974a; Hedman &
Fernie, 1997; Lotz et al, 1998; McK enzie 1981 ).

pressure in the lordotic sitting position when
compared with the kyphosed sitting position

cadaveric studies in which the IVD and adjacent

the lordotic sitting posture, particularly in

already narrowed ( Adams & Hutton, 1980,

zygapophysial impingement and zygapophysial

that there is a reduction in zygapophysial

anterior forces in the zygapophysial joints.

of potential damage to the posterior ligament

potential for IVD degeneration and low back

sitting position.

postures.

hip /bullet6 exion. In the extreme case, a greater
reduction in hip /bullet6 exion may be achieved by
assuming a semi-lying position. This position

such a position as advocated by Cranz may

placing the lumbar support at the pelvic rim

the apex of the lumbar support is placed so

THE ROLE OF THE CHAIR IN
HEALTHY SITTING
Reclined seating, forward inclined seating,
and enhancing the lumbar lordosis are all
functions of chair design. Of/bullet5 ce workers today
require a chair that can maintain all these
healthy postures to accommodate the multi-
task nature of their duties. It is important,
therefore, to provide seating with adjustments
that are readily reached and manipulated,
respond immediately, accommodate varying
body shapes and sizes, and are easily understood
( Helander, Zhang, & Michel, 1995; Herman
Miller, Inc., 1994 ) . The most frequently used
adjustment is seat height ( Coleman, Hull, &
Ellitt, 1998; Helander, Zhang, & Michel, 1995;
Hochandel 1995 ). The reticence to make use
of other adjustments may be re/bullet6 ective of the
bewildering array of adjustments on modern
chairs, and the lack of training and reference
to user manuals ( Helander, Zhang, & Michel,
1995 ). It also may re/bullet6 ect user ignorance
regarding the impact of seated posture on
health. This ignorance may be overcome by
stressing that the chair’s role in healthy sitting
posture necessitates the use of adjustments that
have been provided to ensure maintenance
of lumbar lordosis while allowing alternating
postures between reclined and forward tilt
seating, thus avoiding one sustained posture. It
is equally important to ensure that the subject
chooses a chair that addresses the task at hand

CONCLUSION
Cadaveric studies demonstrate that disc degen-
eration is associated with sedentary work
( Videman, Nurminen, & Troup, 1990 ) and
epidemiological studies show that sedentary
occupations are a cause of low back pain
( K elsey, 1975; Magora, 1972 ) . Poor seated
posture, sitting behavior and seat design are
all thought to be co ntributing factors to this
pain. Poor seated posture may be addressed
by alerting the sitter to the potential damage
caused to the discs, joints and soft tissues
by sustained kyphotic postures, and to the
bene/bullet5 ts to postural health arising from main-
tenance of lordosis, interspersed with move-
ment. Rhythmic movement of the lumbar spine
through lordosis to kyphosis is bene/bullet5 cial to
disc nutrition, provided the kyphosed posi-
tion is not sustained. Movement into standing
from sitting also should be enco uraged to
relieve disc compression. It should be empha-
sised that no one posture is ideal, and that
healthy sitting requires a chair that allows
posture change and is easily adjustable between
reclined and forward tilt, dependent on task
demands. Finally, it is essential that the sitter
understands that pain arising from poor seated
posture is insidious, and that healthy backs
may not register pain until irreversible prob-
lems ( e.g., IVD degeneration ) have occurred.
Fo r this reason, the teaching of healthy seated
posture should be seen as both preventive and
therapeutic.
