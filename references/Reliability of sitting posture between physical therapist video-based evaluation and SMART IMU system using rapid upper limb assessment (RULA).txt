This study investigates the ergonomic assessment of sitting postures and the potential for work-
related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) in office environments by comparing traditional physical 
therapist evaluations with Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) technology by determining the reliability 
and accuracy of sitting posture assessment using the rapid upper limb assessment (RULA) method. 
In this experiment, neck and body angle data is collected from twenty participants while sitting 
and working. The study aims to capture and compare the neck and trunk posture score based RULA 
protocol system to evaluate ergonomic risks. The findings revealed a strong correlation between the 
video-based evaluations by the physical therapist and the data obtained from the SMART IMU system, 
demonstrating the feasibility of using these combined approaches for ergonomic assessments. The 
results highlight the effectiveness of integrating traditional ergonomic assessment tools with modern 
IMU technology in comprehensively analyzing the ergonomic risks associated with prolonged sitting.
Keywords Work-related musculoskeletal disorders, Inertial measurement unit, Sitting posture, Rapid upper 
limb assessment
Currently, the increasing trend of using computers and smartphones affects health issues related to prolonged 
sitting in an office, particularly work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs). According to the world health 
organization (WHO), WMSDs refer to abnormal function of the musculoskeletal system associated with the 
work environment1. This includes issues arising from the body’s organs involving movement, such as muscles, 
tendons, spinal bones, vertebral discs, blood vessels, bone tissue, and nerves. Musculoskeletal discomfort starts 
with a few atypical symptoms and might become persistent, causing impairment 2. Due to repetitive muscular 
use, long periods of sitting (especially in an office or when using a computer) can cause pain. Sitting when 
working on a computer without changing posture may cause discomfort and fatigue in the arms, hands, and 
fingertips due to the peripheral nerves in each position being enforced3–5.
Prolonged sitting, especially in contexts such as office work or computer use, can lead to discomfort and pain 
due to sustained muscle contractions and poor posture. Two upper-body muscles significantly impacted during 
sitting are the upper trapezius and sternocleidomastoid. The upper trapezius, which extends from the neck to the 
shoulders and upper back, stabilizes the shoulder blades and supports the neck’s weight. When the head is held 
forward during prolonged sitting, this muscle remains contracted to maintain head position, causing fatigue and 
discomfort. Similarly, the sternocleidomastoids, which run along both sides of the neck from the sternum and 
collar bone to the mastoid bone behind the ear, assist in neck rotation and bending. Poor posture while sitting, 
particularly with the head and shoulders hunched forward, can over activate these muscles, leading to tightness 
and neck pain. Piotr Kocur et al. found that forward head posture alone has little impact on muscle stiffness,

Numerous studies have assessed the link between prolonged sitting and musculoskeletal pain. For example, 
a study published in the Journal of Occupational Medicine and Toxicology, Dang, T.H.A et al. explored the 
relationship between sedentary behaviors and musculoskeletal pain among German workers7. Using the Nordic 
Musculoskeletal Questionnaire, researchers identified a correlation between sitting duration and complaints 
of neck, shoulder, and back pain, highlighting the significant impact of prolonged sitting on workplace health. 
Another study published in the Journal of Physical Therapy Science, Putsa, B. et al. emphasized that sitting 
for more than 60  min without breaks is particularly detrimental, increasing discomfort and stiffness in the 
musculoskeletal system 8. In general, sitting continuously for periods exceeding 30 to 60  min is considered 
detrimental to musculoskeletal health. Regular breaks, stretching, and adopting ergonomic postures are essential 
strategies to mitigate the risks associated with prolonged sitting.
Via the HSE’s Historical Picture statistics in Great Britain in 2023, a survey of 1.8 million working people found 
that 26% had WMSDs 9. Depending on working position, there are many methods for evaluating ergonomics. 
These evaluation methods are specifically designed to identify and understand musculoskeletal problems arising 
in the workplace. These methods include the rapid upper limp assessment (RULA), rapid entire body assessment 
(REBA), or other assessments based on work posture10–12.
There are many researchers focus on evaluating the poor sitting posture by using many assessment tools 
including RULA and wearable sensors. Lynn McAtamney and Nigel Corlett created RULA in 1993 to assess 
ergonomic posture by observing upper body movements. The assessment is divided into two main groups: 
First, group A evaluates the upper extremities: lower arm and wrist. Second, group B is neck, trunk, and leg 
assessments13–16 with scores based on specified angles. This assessment can be used to evaluate ergonomics at 
work stations, such as workers’ posture in factories or other workplaces. It is particularly helpful in determining 
the level of risk or danger associated with the activity that these workers do. Namwongsa S et al. examines 
the ergonomic risk assessment of a sample of people in their typical posture while using a smartphone using 
the RULA tool to assess the relationship between musculoskeletal disorders. It was found that the relationship 
between musculoskeletal disorders and ergonomic risks is statistically significant. The limitation is that 
experience-based evaluations were conducted only by licensed physical therapists 17. Jang et al. study aimed to 
classify poor posture of the head and shoulders using wearable magnetic sensors and a deep learning algorithm. 
The craniovertebral angle (CV A) was used to analyze forward head posture and shoulder angle (FSA) was used 
to analyze rounded shoulder posture. From the experimental results, the accuracy for deep neural networks 
(DNN) and convolutional neural networks (CNN) was found to be 88.1 and 88.7% 18. Jose Antonio Diego-Mas 
et al., using the Kinect sensor in observational methods for assessing postures at work, explore the potential of 
the Kinect range sensor in the ergonomic assessment of workplace postures. The development of a computerized 
Ovako Working Posture Analysis System (OW AS) was used to gather and process Kinect data. The results were 
compared to results obtained via human observer assessments. The study found that the result of classification 
is high accuracy. The limitations of the Kinect are its natural operating environment and that not being directly 
facing the sensor will reduce accuracy19. Y assary et al. conducted a study on the evaluation of head posture using 
an IMU. The study validated IMU with an inclinometer. A strong correlation was found ( r = 0.99; p < 0.001) 
with cervical range of motion (CROM), which can be a reliable way to analyze head posture. The study also 
assessed head posture variation in healthy subjects and found that the IMU system accurately analyzed head 
posture. The device was cheap, easy to use, and could be used in various environments 20. Xuzhong Y an et al. 
presented a wearable inertial measurement unit (WIMU) system for real-time motion warnings to prevent 
musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) in construction workers. It emphasizes the risk of lower back and neck pain 
due to unsafe postures in construction tasks. The system uses two IMUs to assess ergonomic risk in real-time 
and alerts the wearer via a connected smartphone app when the sensors detect dangerous postures. The study 
validates the system’s effectiveness through lab and field experiments, demonstrating its potential to enhance 
self-awareness and self-management of WMSDs among workers21. Ahmed Humadi et al. examined the accuracy 
and repeatability of wearable IMUs to assess ergonomic risk through RULA in manual material handling tasks. 
This research compared 3D and 2D angle measurements against a motion-capture camera system. The results 
showed that the 2D method had lower error margins and was more consistent in estimating trunk and neck 
angles, thus being more reliable for RULA scoring. The limitations of this research are that it used a small sample 
size and only males. The experiment was conducted for a short period in each posture22.
Many researchers focus on the isolated operation method such as traditional ergonomic assessment tools 
like physical therapist evaluation-based RULA or innovative technologies such as IMU. A gap exists for research 
that integrates these tools to provide a more comprehensive assessment of ergonomic risks. Confirming the 
effectiveness of integrating traditional ergonomic assessment tools like video-based physical therapy with IMU 
for a more comprehensive ergonomic risk assessment involves conducting studies that apply both physical 
therapist evaluation and IMU technologies concurrently in settings and activities.
The contribution of this research is to investigate the reliability of the video-based physical therapist evaluation 
and SMART IMU system23 using the RULA assessment protocol. The aim involves capturing both the detailed 
posture analysis and comparing subjective assessments of ergonomic risk factors associated with upper extremity 
MSD obtained by physical therapist with objective measurements of neck, shoulder, and trunk joint angles and 
upper body movements captured by SMART IMU during task performance by using intraclass correlation 
coefficient. The hypothesis is that the results of both methods would correlate in identifying ergonomic risks.

Materials and methods
The material part of this study is composed of participants, the SMART IMU device, RULA assessment, and 
webcam cameras. The method is composed of the experimental design and data analysis.
Participants
The study involves 20 healthy participants, consisting of 10 men and 10 women. Equation  ( 1) was used to 
calculate the sample size, which indicated that a minimum of 10 participants would be necessary. However, the 
actual response rate exceeded expectations. As a result, 20 participants were included in the study, as a larger 
sample size is expected to enhance the statistical power of the analysis. These individuals are office professionals 
and Mahidol University students who use a desktop or laptop for at least six hours a day, four days a week. The 
participants must possess at least six months of computer experience. However, people with a history of neck, 
shoulder, or back injuries in the preceding six months, and those with medical conditions that affect the spine, 
muscles, bones, and joints in the upper back, such as spinal degeneration, scoliosis, and herniated discs, are 
excluded from the study. Table 1 displays the demographic data of twenty participants.

Equipment
The equipment in this research consists of a device for measuring angles, a sitting posture assessment, and 
webcam cameras for recording the experiment.
SMART IMU
The SMART IMU system was developed by the SMART Laboratory (Biomedical Engineering, Mahidol 
University, Thailand)23. The IMU sensor comprises an accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer integrated 
with a processing circuit to calculate angular data along the X, Y , and Z axes. Raw data from the SMART IMU 
sensors are filtered using a low-pass filter and a Kalman filter. The low-pass filter eliminates high-frequency 
noise, while the Kalman filter smooths the data to ensure accurate orientation measurements. The angles for the 
X and Y axes are derived from the filtered acceleration data, while the Z-axis angle is calculated using magnetic 
field data. Equations (2), (3), and (4) illustrate the calculations for the X, Y , and Z angles, respectively.

Webcams
Two webcams (Logitech C270 with 720p resolution and 30 frames per second) are set to record the movement 
of subjects on the frontal and sagittal sides.
Experimental design
The experiment are performed in the SMART laboratory (Mahidol University, Thailand). The neck and trunk 
joint angles are collected by the SMART IMU system with a sampling rate of 30 Hz. The video of the participants 
captured via the two webcams was evaluated by a physical therapist at a later stage.
Each participant is asked to score their neck, shoulder, and back pain from 0 to 10 before and after the 
experiment. During the experiment, a participant sits in the neutral position in front of the computer. Three 
IMU sensors are placed on the subject at the right temporal area of the head, the seventh cervical spine, and 
the fifth lumbar spine positions (Fig.  4). The SMART IMU system is calibrated and reset to eliminate any zero 
drift. In the neutral sitting position, each joint angle is set to zero degrees. Two webcams are set to record the

The video recordings are used to evaluate the RULA score of neck and trunk parts by physical therapists. The 
neck and trunk angles from the SMART IMU system are converted to a RULA score following the RULA 
assessment. The scores based on RULA assessments from both the physical therapist and SMART IMU system 
are compared. To ensure that the ergonomic risk assessment accurately reflects real-world posture dynamics, 
the frequency and duration of different postures adopted during typing are considered. This comprehensive 
assessment allowed us to identify potential risks associated with the dynamic nature of typing, providing a more 
realistic evaluation of ergonomic risks.
Data analysis
RULA assessment is the tool used for interpreting the data from video recordings and the SMART IMU system. 
This study utilized a 60  min data collection period to evaluate ergonomic postures. Before beginning the 
experiment, participants were instructed to sit still for one minute to establish a baseline measurement. Data 
analysis was conducted at 10-minute intervals, with samples taken at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min. The focus 
was on key postures, including neck twisting, neck flexion, neck extension, neck side bending, trunk forward 
bending, trunk twisting, and trunk side bending, in accordance with the RULA assessment criteria. Movement 
data captured from video recordings, assessed by expert physical therapist, were compared with scores derived 
from IMU data to evaluate reliability and consistency between the two methods.
Video recording analysis
In the video recording interpretation, the physical therapist was asked to review the video recording and ascertain 
the score of the joint angle deviation from sitting posture following the RULA assessment. RULA scores of the 
neck angle and trunk angle are based on the flexion, extension, twisting, and side bending angles. Neck flexion 
is the motion of the chin toward the chest from a neutral position. Neck extension is the movement of the chin 
backward and away from the chest. Neck twisting is the small rotation of the neck that occurs between adjacent 
vertebrae. Neck side bending or lateral flexion is the movement of the neck to the lateral side. Trunk flexion is the 
movement of forward forward-bending of the spine which opposit direction to trunk extension. Trunk twisting 
movement is the rotation of the spine from the center of the body. Trunk side bending is the movement to the 
left and right of the trunk.

Evaluation of the neck score
If the head is straight or slightly bent forward (the head’s angle is not more than 10 degrees from the vertical or 
the neck’s axis), the score will be 1 point. If the head is flexed forward to the vertical between 10 and 20 degrees, 
the score will be 2 points. If the head is flexed forward greater than 20 degrees to the vertical, the score will be 3 
points. If the head is extended, the score will be 4 points. If the neck is twisting or bending, the score is increased 
1 point.
Evaluation of the trunk score
If the trunk extends 0–10 degrees, the score will be 1 point. If the trunk flexes 0–20 degrees, the score will be 
2 points. If the trunk moves forward between 20 and 60 degrees, the score will be 3 points. If the trunk moves 
forward greater than 60 degrees, the score will be 4 points. If the trunk is twisting or bending, the score is added 
one point.

correlation coefficients of neck and trunk angle scores. The results from the SMART IMU system and physical

Discussion
The primary objective of this study is to compare the reliability of ergonomic assessments conducted using 
the SMART IMU system against those performed by video-based physical therapist using the RULA method. 
The results from Table  5 indicate that IMU technology demonstrates a high degree of reliability, close to the 
consistency achieved through traditional physical therapist assessments. The high reliability of SMART IMU-
based assessments, as evidenced by the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). The neck score compared 
between the two systems had an ICC 0.89–0.92 which is interpreted as a very strong correlation25–31. The trunk 
score evaluation had an ICC 0.84–0.89, also indicating a strong correlation. Our findings highlight not only the 
potential for enhanced ergonomic assessment but also the challenges and considerations that accompany the 
integration of these tools.
The WMSD risk assessment study indicated that RULA is the most popular sitting posture examination11,15,32–37. 
The assessment model contains specific angles for scoring to analyze the risk of work postures generating 
WMSDs. It can evaluate the appropriateness of posture while working which makes it applicable to many types 
of work. Video-based RULA ’s weakness is that it still requires ergonomic experts. The evaluation score depends 
on their expertise, which may still be unreliable. The study of Namwongsa S et al.17 also mentions the need for a 
physical therapist’s experience to use RULA for ergonomic evaluation.
The novelty of this study lies in its integration of traditional ergonomic assessment tools (video-based 
physical therapist evaluation) with modern IMU technology. While both approaches have individual benefits, 
their combination provides a more comprehensive and objective assessment of ergonomic risks, addressing the 
limitations of each method when used in isolation, particularly the reliance on physical therapists’ expertise in 
using RULA. This integration allows for a more detailed analysis of sitting posture and upper body movements, 
potentially leading to more effective interventions for preventing WMSDs.
The SMART IMU system was developed for motion analysis, and it was applied to evaluate sitting positions 
when working on a computer in this research. A factor that affects the accuracy of SMART IMU devices in 
measuring angles is the error that may occur during the calibration process. While doing the calibration, the 
subject is requested to stay still in the neutral sitting position. The error may occur if the subject is moving. 
Some researchers such as Wonil Lee et al. also found limitations to using IMU sensors which were related to 
the size and placement of the sensors. They investigate the effectiveness of wearable sensors for analyzing trunk 
posture in construction workers. It compares single-parameter monitoring wearable sensors (SPMWS) and 
multi-parameter monitoring wearable sensors (MPMWS) placed at different body locations during simulated 
construction tasks. The study found that sensor placement significantly affects the accuracy of trunk posture 
analysis, with chest and back placements providing the most reliable data. The research suggests that wearable 
sensors can offer valuable insights into ergonomic risk in construction, but the choice of sensor type and 
placement is crucial for accurate posture analysis. The limitation of this study is the controlled laboratory setting, 
which may only partially replicate natural environment construction activities38.
This study highlights the potential of the SMART IMU system for ergonomic assessment, demonstrating 
its high reliability compared to traditional video-based RULA evaluations. However, several limitations should 
be considered. First, the study was conducted in a controlled laboratory environment, using only a single desk 
type. While this setup ensured consistent measurements, it does not reflect the variability of real-world office 
settings, where factors such as inconsistent desk height, inappropriate chair dimensions, and individual physical 
characteristics can significantly influence sitting posture and muscle group usage. Additionally, the experiment’s 
observation period was limited to just one hour, which may be insufficient to capture long-term changes in 
posture or the onset of fatigue, especially in healthy individuals who may adopt different postures over extended 
periods.
Another limitation is the controlled nature of the study, which excluded many dynamic and uncontrollable 
factors present in real-world office settings, such as environmental distractions or spontaneous movements.

Moreover, the analysis focused solely on angular measurements and posture evaluation without incorporating 
complementary tools such as electromyography (EMG) or 3D motion capture. This restricts the ability to assess 
muscle fatigue, tension, and pain caused by poor sitting postures, such as rounded shoulders or forward head 
positions, which could enhance the understanding of ergonomic risks.
Pose estimation algorithms like OpenPose and MediaPipe present significant potential for estimating 
body angles from video recordings, offering several advantages. These methods are non-invasive, as they do 
not require attaching sensors to the body, which enhances comfort and allows for natural movement during 
assessment39. Additionally, they are often more cost-effective and provide visual representations of body posture, 
aiding in the understanding of movement patterns and the identification of ergonomic risks. Incorporating 
a comparison between pose estimation and IMU-based methods within future studies could enhance the 
relevance of the research, providing valuable insights into the strengths and limitations of each approach. Factors 
such as accuracy, cost, invasiveness, and data visualization capabilities should be considered, contributing to 
a comprehensive understanding of ergonomic assessment tools and their optimal application across different 
settings.
Building on this, future research should address current limitations and explore the integration of IMU 
technology with other advanced analysis tools. Studies conducted in real-world office environments with diverse 
furniture setups and extended observation periods would provide more practical insights. Combining IMU-
based assessments with electromyography (EMG) could further enhance the evaluation of muscle fatigue and 
identify postures that lead to discomfort or pain. Additionally, integrating 3D motion capture technology could 
offer detailed analyses of body movements and their ergonomic implications. These advancements, alongside 
comparisons with pose estimation methods, could guide the redesign of work environments and industrial 
facilities, ultimately improving quality of life and reducing the prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) 
through more effective risk identification and intervention strategies.
Conclusion
This research contributes to the validation of the subjective method (video-based physical therapist) with the 
objective method (SMART IMU system) based on the RULA assessment protocol. The results show there are no 
significant differences between evaluations from physical therapist and the SMART IMU system. The integration 
of both the subjective method and objective method can enhance the effectiveness of the evaluation which may 
contribute to the prevention of work-related musculoskeletal disorders. In future studies, IMU system-based 
RULA assessment can be applied with other analysis tools such as electromyograpgy (EMG) and 3D motion 
capture to advance the practice of ergonomic risk assessment. The research can be applied to redesign working 
environments and industries including office settings and working facilities for better quality of life. The research 
lays the groundwork for future innovations in ergonomic assessment, with the ultimate goal of reducing the 
prevalence of MSDs in the workplace through more effective risk identification and intervention strategies.
